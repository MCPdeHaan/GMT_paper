# Data preparation logbook

This document tracks the data and methodology decisions made in the analysis pipeline. Each entry describes what was done in economic and policy terms; programming detail belongs in the scripts and their diagnostic output, not here.

## 2026-05-04 - Stage 1 constrained-Beta ETR distribution and stricter Table IV validation

This entry records the replacement of the Stage 1 piecewise-uniform percentile distribution with a constrained-Beta distribution. The economic motivation is that ETRs are bounded ratios of tax accrued to pre-tax profit, so the distributional fallback should live strictly on the economically admissible interval and should not require an ad hoc location shift of published percentiles. The new fit uses the jurisdiction-average Stage 1 ETR as an exact mean constraint and estimates the remaining Beta precision parameter by minimising the squared distance between the fitted and observed CbCR percentiles P5, P25, P50, P75 and P95. The low-taxed share is now the Beta CDF evaluated at the 15 per cent threshold, and the average top-up rate uses the closed-form truncated first moment of the Beta distribution.

The publication claim is deliberately narrower than the initial proposal. A mean-constrained two-parameter Beta distribution has only one free shape degree of freedom after the mean is fixed. It is therefore smoother and more reproducible than the old shift-and-interpolation method, but it cannot generally fit five percentiles when the true affiliate-year ETR distribution is bimodal or has point masses. The Stage 1 diagnostics now report the Beta quantile-fit error by year: the mean absolute fit error is 0.112 in 2021 and 0.082 in 2022, with large maximum errors in boundary or highly inconsistent cells. This should be written as a disciplined parametric fallback, not as proof that the Beta family is the true distribution.

The Table I versus Table IV cross-validation was also strengthened and moved to the results appendix. The overlap remains 239 affiliate-years. The constrained-Beta fit does not materially solve the validation problem: the low-taxed-share mean absolute error is 0.308, the 90th percentile absolute error is 0.667, the median positive-partition total variation distance is 0.546 and the calibration regression has an R-squared of only 0.181. The fitted low-taxed share is statistically related to the direct Table IV share, with a calibration slope of 0.666 and a significant p-value, but the error is too large for the table to support a main-text validation claim. The correct interpretation is that Table IV remains the preferred observed source wherever it exists; the Beta percentile branch is a smooth fallback for missing Table IV coverage.

The deterministic pipeline was rerun from Stage 1 through Stage 4.6 after the change, including the generated results tables. The 500-draw Stage 1 measurement-error simulation was started but deliberately not completed in this turn, because it is computationally slower and can be rerun later from `code/09_stage1_measurement_error_simulation.R`. Any measurement-error interval that depends on that script should therefore be treated as not refreshed by this entry.

## 2026-05-04 - Stage 4.6 reframed as structural variance and Shapley-style mechanism decomposition

This entry records a reframing of the 2^5 revenue scenario matrix. The factorial design remains the correct empirical object, because it already varies the five contested modelling choices jointly and therefore identifies multi-way interactions that one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis cannot see. The change is in interpretation and reporting. The decomposition is now presented as a structural variance decomposition rather than as a generic ANOVA exercise. The economic analogy is the Oaxaca-Blinder distinction between endowments and coefficients: the ETR measurement and substance factors change the measured tax base and shield, while the behavioural factor changes the semi-elasticity applied to that base. Legal allocation through the Side-by-Side regime is treated as an institutional rule factor rather than as either a tax-base endowment or a behavioural coefficient.

The Stage 4.6 script now writes three additional outputs. `step7_stage4_6_structural_variance_pooled.csv` labels each orthogonal contrast by economic mechanism and uncertainty class. `step7_stage4_6_shapley_factor_attribution.csv` allocates every interaction share equally across the factors that enter the interaction, giving a Shapley-style factor attribution that sums to the full scenario variance. `step7_stage4_6_uncertainty_summary.csv` separates epistemic measurement uncertainty, epistemic legal-regime uncertainty, policy-design scenario uncertainty, behavioural specification uncertainty and mixed interactions. The generated thesis table `stage4_6_shapley_factor_attribution.tex` reports the corresponding mechanism ranking.

The current pooled results are economically sharp. After allocating interaction variance Shapley-style, ETR measurement accounts for 53.0 per cent of scenario variance, the SbS legal regime for 37.3 per cent, the behavioural response for 5.4 per cent, the substance specification for 4.1 per cent and the SBIE rate endpoint for 0.2 per cent. This strengthens the publication narrative: the main disagreement in public-CbCR GMT revenue estimates is an endowment and legal-regime problem, not primarily a behavioural-coefficient problem. The largest interaction, ETR measurement times SbS, should be interpreted substantively: the distributional ETR method identifies low-taxed profit that is partly attached to US-parented groups, and the SbS regime then removes IIR and UTPR collection on those groups while leaving QDMTTs intact.

One critical limitation is now explicit. The behavioural factorial contrast is not the same as aleatoric uncertainty from bootstrapped Hines-Rice coefficients. It is the difference between a mechanical scenario and a point-estimated behavioural scenario. Aleatoric uncertainty would require a separate coefficient-propagation exercise that resamples the Hines-Rice estimation and reruns the revenue allocation. The thesis text now states this instead of implying that the Stage 4.6 scenario variance already contains bootstrapped behavioural standard errors. This is more defensible for publication and prevents the scenario spread from being confused with statistical confidence intervals.

The methodology text was also corrected mathematically. The two-way interaction sum of squares in a balanced 2^K design uses the factor n/16 on the difference-in-differences contrast, not n/4. The code was already computing the orthogonal contrast correctly; the written equation now matches the implementation. The full R test suite passes with 128 successful expectations, and the thesis compiles after adding the new citations and generated table.

## 2026-05-04 - Lower-tail ETR nonlinearity kept as support diagnostic rather than IPW

This entry records the decision on whether to up-weight observations in the extreme lower tail of the ETR distribution to identify nonlinear profit-shifting responses. The proposal is understandable, because the logarithmic Hines-Rice specification is most policy-relevant near zero ETRs. However, inverse probability weighting is not the right primary estimator for the current public CbCR panel. IPW requires a defensible probability of treatment, observation, or selection into the target state. In this setting, being below a 5 per cent ETR is not an externally assigned treatment or an observed sampling event. It is part of the tax-planning outcome whose behavioural response is being estimated. Up-weighting those cells would therefore change the estimand and create a tail-emphasised robustness exercise, not an inverse-probability-weighted causal estimate.

The pipeline now reports the lower-tail support explicitly in Stage 3. In the baseline fixed-effect estimation sample, below-5 per cent ETR observations account for 1,569 of 8,971 rows, or 17.5 per cent of the sample, but only 10.1 per cent of the profit mass. There is real support, but it is not dominant support: 607 of 1,936 parent-affiliate pairs switch between the below-5 per cent tail and the rest of the ETR distribution. An illustrative tail-balanced design that forces the below-5 per cent group to 50 per cent of weighted rows would require a tail multiplier of 2.86 and would reduce the effective sample size to 5,178, with a design effect of 1.73.

The publishable interpretation is therefore narrower and stronger. The panel supports a negative behavioural tax response, and the linear specification remains the preferred BIC model. The exact curvature of the response at near-zero ETRs remains a sensitivity margin rather than a separately identified structural feature. The thesis now reports this in the Stage 3 robustness discussion and in Appendix Table `tab:app_stage3_etr_tail_support`. The diagnostic files are `data/processed/diagnostics/step5_stage3_etr_tail_support.csv` and `data/processed/diagnostics/step5_stage3_tail_reweighting_feasibility.csv`.

## 2026-05-04 - SESH notch-bunching moved from structural claim to pre-reform diagnostic

This entry records a methodological decision on the Simplified ETR Safe Harbour notch. The proposed structural bunching route is attractive for publication, but it is not identified from the current public CbCR panel. A true notch-bunching estimator needs the post-policy density around the 15 per cent threshold: excess mass at the safe-harbour point and missing mass in the dominated region just below it. The realised CbCR data end in 2022, before the January 2026 Simplified ETR Safe Harbour can affect reported simplified ETRs. Using these pre-reform data to estimate both the counterfactual density and the behavioural response would therefore confuse baseline exposure with realised bunching.

The thesis now treats the current SESH band in Stage 3 as an upper-bound behavioural sensitivity rather than as an estimated structural response. The liability filter still says that jurisdictions inside the assumed band below 15 per cent can reach the safe harbour without cross-border profit reallocation, but the text now states that this is a counterfactual assumption. The identification-design diagnostic in `code/05_stage3_behavioral.R` has been expanded with a separate SESH notch-bunching row, which makes explicit that structural estimation would require post-SESH MNE-jurisdiction microdata with simplified ETRs and safe-harbour elections.

A new diagnostic script, `code/11_sesh_notch_bunching_diagnostic.R`, implements the publishable next step that the current data can support. It bins pre-reform jurisdiction-year average ETRs around the 15 per cent threshold, fits a polynomial counterfactual density while excluding the threshold region, and reports placebo excess and missing mass for the global and EU27 samples. The output is written to `data/processed/sesh_notch_*.rds` and `data/processed/diagnostics/step11_sesh_notch_*.csv`. These files should be interpreted as exposure and design diagnostics, not as evidence that firms have already bunched.

The writing in `writing/thesisv2.tex` has been tightened accordingly. The SESH section now cites the structural notch-bunching logic of Kleven and Waseem, but it also explains why the present thesis cannot estimate that model as the main behavioural input. The clean publication route is to report the current revenue effect as an upper-bound SESH sensitivity and position the structural bunching estimator as a follow-up that becomes feasible once post-reform microdata or administrative safe-harbour elections are observed.

## 2026-05-03 - Formal sectoral measurement bridge for the SBIE and SBTI layer

This entry records the rebuild of the sectoral decomposition as a formal measurement bridge. The key methodological decision is that CbCR remains the liability base. The sectoral datasets do not observe GloBE income, covered taxes, QDMTT liability, actual SBTI claims or the Pillar Two in-scope population. They are therefore used only to allocate CbCR country-level payroll, tangible assets and positive profit across sectors in the 2022 EU reference matrix. This keeps the sectoral sensitivity layer conceptually separate from the legal top-up-tax base.

`code/02_base_correction_and_merging.R` now writes an explicit source hierarchy and crosswalk for the sectoral branch. The hierarchy is: FATS personnel costs for payroll shares, with employees and turnover as scaled fallbacks; EUKLEMS `K_Tang` tangible capital stock for asset shares when at least 75 per cent of FATS turnover is covered, with FATS investment as the transparent fallback; FATS gross operating surplus for profit shares, with value added and turnover as scaled fallbacks. BEA is assigned a validation role only. The new diagnostics are `step2_sectoral_source_hierarchy.csv`, `step2_sectoral_nace_crosswalk_rules.csv`, `step2_sectoral_measurement_bridge.csv`, `step2_sectoral_measurement_bridge_summary.csv` and `step2_sectoral_bridge_country_year.csv`.

The 2022 bridge still contains 27 Member States and 40 non-overlapping FATS leaf sectors per Member State. Payroll, asset and profit shares each sum to one within every Member State up to numerical precision. Fifteen Member States use EUKLEMS stock shares for the asset leg, of which seven require investment-based gap-filling for suppressed EUKLEMS cells. Twelve Member States fall back to FATS investment because EUKLEMS stock coverage does not reach the 75 per cent turnover threshold. The missing sector cells that cannot be supported by any activity anchor are left missing and counted in the diagnostics, rather than filled with invented sector values.

`code/04_stage2_sbie.R` now carries the source labels into the Stage 2 sectoral matrix and writes `step4_stage2_sectoral_bridge_source_summary.csv` and `step4_stage2_sectoral_bridge_country_quality.csv`. In the 2022 EU reference matrix, 98.1 per cent of the allocated payroll level comes directly from FATS personnel costs; 83.2 per cent of the allocated tangible-asset level uses EUKLEMS stock information, including gap-filled cells; and 88.2 per cent of the allocated sectoral profit denominator comes directly from FATS gross operating surplus.

The BEA broad-sector diagnostic is now interpreted as a validation decision rather than a source contest. BEA employment, compensation and PPE expenditure remain supportive external benchmarks for the labour and activity bridge. BEA total assets remain an alarming warning against using BEA assets as the sectoral asset allocator: after the rebuild, the BEA total-asset share correlation with the FATS/EUKLEMS asset bridge is 0.165 and the mean absolute gap is 10.8 percentage points. The code therefore classifies BEA total assets as rejected for primary asset allocation, while retaining BEA as a Side-by-Side-relevant US-parent benchmark.

Stages 2 through 7 were rerun after the bridge rebuild, including the generated results tables. The full interpretation in the thesis should now be narrower and stronger: the project constructs a public CbCR-based GMT revenue estimate and overlays a source-ranked EU sectoral SBIE and SBTI sensitivity. It does not claim to observe sectoral GloBE income or actual tax-incentive take-up.

## 2026-05-02 - Critical positioning of FATS, EUKLEMS and BEA in the GMT substance layer

This entry records a critical audit of the sectoral data strategy after the EUKLEMS and BEA additions. The central policy rule is that the GMT liability is jurisdictional and group-based, while the SBIE removes a percentage of eligible payroll costs and eligible tangible assets from excess profit. Article 28 of Directive (EU) 2022/2523 defines eligible payroll costs and eligible tangible assets in this legal sense, and the January 2026 OECD Side-by-Side package leaves QDMTTs unaffected while adding a separate substance-based tax-incentive safe harbour. The data architecture should therefore not treat any sectoral dataset as if it directly observed GloBE income, GloBE ETRs or actual qualifying tax-incentive claims. The sectoral datasets are best used to distribute aggregate CbCR levels across economically relevant sectors, not to replace CbCR as the revenue base.

The preferred hierarchy is now explicit. Public CbCR remains the liability base because it is the only source in the pipeline that observes parent-affiliate tax, profit, employee and tangible-asset quantities close to the Pillar Two population. Eurostat inward FATS is used only as an EU host-country sectoral exposure map for foreign-controlled enterprises. This is appropriate because FATS has the NACE detail needed to allocate payroll, investment, turnover, value added and operating-surplus shares across sectors, but it is not restricted to groups above the EUR 750 million Pillar Two threshold and contains no ETR information. EUKLEMS & INTANProd is used only inside the asset leg of the sectoral bridge, because its tangible capital stocks are closer to the stock concept of the SBIE than FATS gross investment flows. EUKLEMS is still a national-accounts dataset for all firms, not a foreign-controlled MNE dataset, so it should only determine within-country sectoral shares where coverage is strong. BEA is now positioned as a U.S.-parented MNE benchmark, which is especially relevant for the Side-by-Side regime, but not as a direct replacement for CbCR because the available BEA sidecar does not identify GloBE ETRs, Pillar Two scope, QDMTT liability, or a clean eligible tangible-asset stock for every EU host country and sector.

The audit found one material BEA implementation problem. The earlier BEA sidecar used both aggregate manufacturing and detailed manufacturing rows in the same denominator. That would double-count U.S.-parented activity in the benchmark shares, which is precisely the type of overlap already removed from the FATS bridge. `code/02_base_correction_and_merging.R` now rebuilds the BEA benchmark as a non-overlapping top-level sector object. Manufacturing is used once as a top-level sector; wholesale and retail are combined into a broad trade sector; mining, information, finance and professional services are kept as separate broad sectors; and BEA `Other industries` is retained as an unmapped residual. The BEA shares are normalised to the reported all-industries total where it is available, so the diagnostic records both mapped-sector coverage and top-level coverage instead of silently imposing a full sector map.

`code/04_stage2_sbie.R` now writes `data/processed/diagnostics/step4_stage2_bea_fats_broad_sector_validation.csv` and the matching summary file. This diagnostic compares the 2022 FATS/EUKLEMS broad-sector bridge with the closest BEA U.S.-parent measures. The comparison is deliberately broad-sector because BEA and FATS do not share the same detailed industry taxonomy. The results are informative. BEA compensation in 2023 aligns reasonably well with FATS personnel-cost shares in the 2022 EU reference matrix, with a correlation of 0.833 and a mean absolute share gap of 5.6 percentage points. BEA employment in 2022 also aligns reasonably well with FATS employee shares, with a correlation of 0.771 and a mean absolute gap of 6.6 percentage points. BEA PPE expenditure in 2023 is moderately consistent with FATS investment-flow shares, with a correlation of 0.791 and a mean absolute gap of 8.5 percentage points. BEA total assets in 2022 are weakly aligned with the FATS/EUKLEMS asset bridge, with a correlation of only 0.150 and a mean absolute gap of 10.8 percentage points. This last result is substantively important: BEA total assets should not be used as the preferred tangible-asset allocation measure.

The sectoral baseline therefore remains unchanged in its core logic but sharper in its interpretation. For the aggregate revenue estimate, use CbCR levels and the wage-based payroll proxy. For the sectoral sensitivity, use FATS personnel-cost shares for payroll, positive FATS gross operating surplus where available for profit allocation, EUKLEMS tangible-stock shares for assets when at least 75 per cent of FATS turnover is covered, and FATS investment only as a transparent fallback. In the 2022 EU reference matrix, 15 Member States use EUKLEMS asset shares and 12 fall back to FATS investment because EUKLEMS sectoral stock coverage is insufficient. BEA should be cited as an external U.S.-parent benchmark and as a Side-by-Side-relevant validation dataset, not as the source of the main sectoral SBIE parameters.

The practical implication for the thesis is a narrower but more defensible claim. The study can say that it combines CbCR, FATS and EUKLEMS to construct a public sectoral SBIE and SBTI-capacity sensitivity for EU host countries. It should not say that the pipeline observes sectoral GloBE income, sectoral GloBE ETRs, actual qualifying tax incentives, or a BEA-based replacement for CbCR assets. The BEA evidence supports the use of FATS for labour and activity shares among U.S.-parented MNEs, but it warns against treating BEA total assets as a clean tangible-asset stock. The full test suite was rerun after the code changes and passes with 63 successful expectations.

## 2026-05-01 - Sectoral SBIE bridge implemented with EUKLEMS stocks

This entry records the implementation of the revised sectoral substance strategy in the R pipeline. The sectoral branch now uses Eurostat inward FATS only as an EU host-country sectoral exposure map for foreign-controlled enterprises. The code selects the `WRL_REST` controlling-country total, which represents foreign control excluding domestic control, rather than summing across country-of-control categories. This corrects a material double-counting problem: the FATS country-of-control dimension contains totals, subtotals and individual countries, so summing it would count the same foreign-controlled activity multiple times.

The FATS sector set is now restricted to non-overlapping leaf sectors. Broad aggregates such as `B-S_X_O_S94`, `G-S_X_O_S94`, `C`, `H`, `J`, `M`, `N` and `Q` are excluded from the sectoral shares because they overlap with their detailed children. The bridge maps the remaining FATS NACE sectors to EUKLEMS sectors. Where EUKLEMS is broader than FATS, for example `C31-C33`, `J58-J60`, `M`, `N` and `S`, the broader EUKLEMS tangible stock is allocated across the FATS children using the within-family FATS investment distribution. This keeps the sectoral object at the FATS detail level without pretending that EUKLEMS observes a finer stock breakdown than it actually does.

The asset leg of the sectoral SBIE sensitivity now uses EUKLEMS `K_Tang` tangible-capital stock shares when sectoral stock coverage is adequate. If EUKLEMS covers at least 75 per cent of the FATS turnover in an affiliate-year, missing stock cells are gap-filled with FATS investment scaled by the observed stock-to-investment ratio in that same country-year. If coverage is weaker, the asset leg falls back to FATS gross investment. This makes the construction more stock-based while still being explicit about where the public EUKLEMS detail is insufficient. In the 2022 EU reference matrix, 15 of 27 Member States use EUKLEMS stock shares, and 12 fall back to FATS investment because EUKLEMS lacks enough sectoral tangible-stock detail.

The sectoral profit allocation has also been tightened. The code now uses positive FATS gross operating surplus where available, then positive value added, and turnover only as a last fallback. This is economically preferable because turnover is a scale measure, while operating surplus is closer to the profit concept that the Stage 2 effective-floor calculation needs.

BEA US MNE data are now ingested into a processed country-industry benchmark object for U.S.-parented majority-owned foreign affiliates. The object covers historical total assets and employment plus preliminary 2023 PPE expenditures and compensation. It is deliberately labelled as a benchmark rather than a direct GloBE replacement: the BEA PPE table used here is an expenditure flow, and the historical BEA assets file is total assets rather than a clean GloBE tangible-asset stock. A later bridge can use these data for U.S.-parented sensitivity work, but the preferred public estimate remains the aggregate CbCR SBIE.

The refreshed checks confirm that the revised 2022 sectoral payroll, asset and profit weights each sum to one up to machine precision, the Stage 2 sectoral floors remain bounded between zero and the 15 per cent GMT rate, and the full test suite passes with 63 successful expectations. Stages 5, 6 and 7 were rerun after the Stage 2 change, so the behavioural, legal allocation, final decomposition and generated thesis-table outputs now inherit the revised sectoral construction.

## 2026-05-01 - FATS reframing and EUKLEMS/INTAN/BEA raw-data collection

This entry records a data-source reframing for the sectoral SBIE work. Eurostat inward FATS should be used as an EU host-country sectoral exposure bridge for foreign-controlled enterprises, not as a direct Pillar Two population and not as a source of ETR information. FATS remains relevant because foreign-controlled enterprises are economically important in the EU despite being a small share of enterprises, but it is not restricted to groups above the Pillar Two revenue threshold and it does not observe GloBE ETRs. The aggregate public-CbCR SBIE therefore remains the preferred revenue estimate. The FATS-based sectoral branch should be described as a sensitivity that imposes public EU host-country sector shares on the aggregate CbCR base.

The raw-data collector now downloads the LUISS EUKLEMS & INTANProd all-country national accounts, labour accounts, capital accounts and analytical intangibles files. The sectoral asset leg should move from FATS gross investment flows towards EUKLEMS tangible capital-stock shares, using the `K_Tang` stock variable where NACE coverage permits. INTAN variables are useful for robustness and interpretation, especially to identify sectors where legal tangible-asset substance is low despite high intangible capital, but they should not enter the SBIE asset base because the SBIE does not generally include intangible assets.

The BEA collector now also downloads the preliminary 2023 U.S. direct investment abroad MOFA table groups for property, plant and equipment, employment and compensation, selected activity data, value added, and the complete table ZIP. These files are the natural benchmark for U.S.-parented MNEs. They are not yet integrated into the processed Stage 2 panel, so the thesis should not claim that BEA already replaces CbCR assets and wage-imputed payroll. The next pipeline step is a BEA-to-CbCR bridge for `parent == "USA"` and a EUKLEMS-to-FATS NACE harmonisation table for the sectoral EU27 sensitivity.

## 2026-04-26 - Strategic behavioural response and profit-conserving reallocation

This entry records the implementation of a richer behavioural response in `code/05_stage3_behavioral.R`, with refreshed downstream outputs from `code/06_stage4_5_revenue_allocation_sbs.R` and `code/07_stage4_6_revenue_pipeline_decomposition.R`. The old Stage 3 behavioural branch treated behaviour mainly as a semi-elasticity-based liability filter. That was useful as a conservative benchmark, but it did not represent the post-Pillar Two planning margin very well. The new implementation keeps the same 32-scenario contract used by Stages 4.5 and 4.6, but the `behavioural` level of Factor 5 now means a strategic behavioural response rather than a uniform scalar shrinkage.

The static fixed-effect semi-elasticity is still estimated and stored in `data/processed/stage3_behavioral_affiliate_year.rds`. The new strategic block then maps that estimated response onto pair-year cells using observed CbCR proxies for planning capacity. The row-level multiplier depends on the post-SBIE top-up exposure, QDMTT/IIR/UTPR/SbS collection exposure, IP/holding/intra-group finance activity counts, conduit-haven status, low-substance intensity, profit per employee, and the sectoral SBIE/SBTI branch. This is deliberately presented as a calibrated structural scenario, not as a causal estimate of post-Pillar Two behaviour, because public CbCR does not observe group-level GloBE ETRs, intra-group royalties, intra-group debt, legal entity chains, qualified refundable credits, or actual consultant-led restructurings.

The channel split is now explicit. Reallocated profit is divided across transfer-pricing/IP, debt, and substance-planning channels. The baseline priors are 67 per cent transfer-pricing/IP, 25 per cent debt, and 8 per cent substance planning, then adjusted by the same CbCR proxies and normalised row by row. This is anchored in the profit-shifting literature rather than chosen to make the behavioural effect large. The cap on the strategic reallocation share is 65 per cent, so the model cannot mechanically eliminate all low-taxed profit in the aggressive cells.

The reallocation diagnostic is now profit-conserving at the scenario-year level. Reallocated profit is allocated to statutory high-tax, non-conduit destination jurisdictions using a Garcia-Bernardo-style activity key: 25 per cent employment, 25 per cent payroll, and 50 per cent unrelated-party revenue. If no statutory high-tax non-conduit destination exists in a scenario-year, the code falls back to all positive-activity jurisdictions and reports the fallback source. The maximum reallocation conservation gap is below USD 0.001, which is numerical rounding. The new outputs are `data/processed/stage3_strategy_scenario_summary.rds`, `data/processed/stage3_reallocation_conservation.rds`, `data/processed/stage3_reallocation_channel_summary.rds`, `data/processed/stage3_reallocation_destination_summary.rds`, and their matching `step5_stage3_*` CSV diagnostics.

In the preferred initial-rate, aggregate-SBIE, distributional-ETR, universal scenario, the strategic behavioural branch starts from about USD 1.31 trillion of pre-behaviour top-up liability over 2017-2022 and reduces it by about USD 338.8 billion. The implied profit reallocation is about USD 3.15 trillion. The channel diagnostic allocates roughly USD 2.25 trillion to transfer-pricing/IP, USD 687 billion to debt, and USD 209 billion to substance planning. The separate ordinary CIT recovery diagnostic gives about USD 547.8 billion of net recovery in the same preferred branch. This ordinary CIT diagnostic is not added to GMT revenue; it is reported separately because it reflects where shifted profits would be taxed under the destination allocation, not a GloBE top-up tax collection channel.

The final legal revenue decomposition changes accordingly. Preferred EU GMT revenue under the universal regime is now about USD 391.2 billion after strategic behaviour, compared with about USD 524.0 billion mechanically. Under the SbS regime it is about USD 253.6 billion after strategic behaviour, compared with about USD 354.5 billion mechanically. In the final Stage 4.6 ANOVA, the behavioural response is now the third largest main effect, accounting for about 15.2 per cent of scenario variation. ETR measurement remains the largest effect at about 40.4 per cent, SbS remains second at about 32.9 per cent, and the pooled interaction share is about 5.7 per cent. This makes the behavioural part economically visible while preserving the earlier finding that ETR measurement and legal allocation assumptions still dominate the scenario space.

The full test suite was rerun after the implementation and now passes with 63 successful tests. Four new test expectations were added around the strategic behavioural mechanics, including the beta cap and channel-share conservation. The methodological caveat for the thesis is important: this is a stronger behavioural scenario model, not direct evidence that every in-scope MNE will execute these strategies. For each positive-profit parent-affiliate-year cell, the SBIE is computed as the relevant payroll carve-out plus the tangible-asset carve-out, using the wage-based payroll proxy and the CbCR tangible assets already prepared in Step 2. The resulting SBIE-to-profit ratio is capped at one, so the top-up base cannot turn negative. This makes the Stage 2 filter directly multiplicative with the Stage 1 liability object, which is exactly how the pipeline is written in the methodology chapter.

One implementation choice had to be made explicit. About 10.7 per cent of positive-profit pair-years in the global Stage 1 panel still lack a payroll proxy because the affiliate-year wage benchmark is missing, even after the OECD and ILO hierarchy. These rows account for only 3 EU pair-years and a negligible share of EU Stage 1 liability, but they still matter in the non-EU extension. The Stage 2 baseline therefore treats missing payroll as a zero payroll contribution while retaining the observed asset component, and flags every affected row. This is a conservative lower-bound treatment of the substance shield, not a claim that payroll is truly zero. The diagnostics now report exactly how much Stage 1 liability sits in these fallback rows.

The effective-floor calculation also needed a methodological refinement. The thesis text writes the floor as `T - (T - τ) φ`, which is correct in the jurisdiction-average case but too coarse once Stage 1 already recovers the average top-up rate on the low-taxed segment. The implemented aggregate Stage 2 therefore uses the Stage 1 top-up-rate object itself as the tax-gap term. In other words, the average branch uses the Stage 1 jurisdiction-average top-up rate, and the distributional branch uses the Stage 1 average top-up rate on the low-taxed mass. This keeps Stage 2 coherent with the actual Stage 1 measurement rather than reverting to a cruder average-ETR approximation.

The sectoral branch is implemented as a separate 2022 EU27 reference-year matrix rather than as a full historical panel back to 2017. This is a deliberate restriction, and it follows from the data rather than from convenience. Eurostat inward FATS only become structurally comparable in the post-2021 `fats_activ` release, and `writing/strategic_direction.md` already narrowed the sectoral analysis to a single 2022 baseline matrix with the 2024-2033 transition path computed mechanically. The sectoral matrix should therefore be read as the institutional mapping of Stage 2 heterogeneity across EU countries and sectors, not as an observed sector panel over the entire CbCR horizon.

Three further approximations in the sectoral branch are now explicit. First, sectoral payroll shares are taken directly from FATS personnel-cost shares. Second, because the repository contains no cleaned STAN or KLEMS capital-stock file, sectoral tangible-asset shares are proxied with FATS investment shares. This preserves exact within-country share summation and is transparent, but it is weaker than the draft text's capital-intensity-plus-KLEMS aspiration. Third, sectoral profit shares are not observed in either CbCR or FATS, so the sectoral denominator uses FATS net-turnover shares as the profit-allocation proxy. This is the strongest practical way to obtain a complete sector matrix from the data currently in the repository, but it is still a proxy and must be described as such in the methodology and results chapters.

The January 2026 SBTI Safe Harbour is implemented as a capacity overlay, not as a realised tax-incentive claim. Public data do not reveal actual qualifying tax incentives. The script therefore constructs the sector-level capacity proxy `κ` exactly as described in the methodology, from the larger of the Method 1 payroll arm and the Method 2 carrying-value arm, each normalised by the sectoral profit proxy. The overlay then reduces the sectoral effective floor by up to this capacity, bounded below by zero. This makes the SBTI result an upper-bound policy-capacity exercise: it shows how much room each sector has to preserve incentives without triggering top-up tax, not how much room firms actually claim in practice.

One inconsistency in the written transition description also had to be resolved. The thesis text states that the payroll carve-out falls by 0.5 percentage points per year from 10 per cent in 2024 to 5 per cent in 2033, and that the asset carve-out falls by about 0.3 points from 8 per cent to 5 per cent over the same interval. Taken literally, these annual decrements do not land exactly on the 2033 endpoint. The implemented transition path therefore uses exact linear interpolation between the 2024 and 2033 endpoints. The two factorial endpoints remain unchanged, initial at 10/8 and long-run at 5/5, but the intermediate years now follow a mathematically consistent path.

The first Stage 2 results are economically sensible. In the aggregate bilateral panel the initial-rate SBIE materially reduces the Stage 1 top-up base, with the expected stronger effect at the 10/8 rates than at the 5/5 rates. In the 2022 EU27 sectoral matrix, the lowest effective floors remain concentrated in the more substance-heavy country-sector combinations, and the SBTI-capacity overlay lowers those floors further. The H5 cross-section, estimated on the 2024 initial-rate matrix with Member State fixed effects, yields a negative coefficient of the change in the effective floor on sectoral capital intensity, consistent with the production-subsidy logic of the SBIE and the SBTI. This result should be written cautiously because it is conditional on the turnover-based profit proxy and the investment-based asset proxy, but the directional pattern is now in the data rather than only in the theory section.

## 2026-04-19 - Pre-methodology audit of Steps 1 and 2

This entry supersedes all earlier references in this logbook to observed CbCR payroll or to CbCR `EARNINGS` as a labour-cost measure. The audit was triggered by a full review of `01_data_cleaning.R`, `02_base_correction_and_merging.R`, the merged outputs, and the appendix tables before moving on to the methodology itself.

### Central correction: CbCR `EARNINGS` are accumulated earnings, not payroll

The decisive factual error in the earlier Step 2 construction was conceptual rather than computational. The OECD describes the CbCR template as containing stated capital, accumulated earnings, number of employees, and tangible assets. It does not provide a payroll variable. The previous pipeline had treated the CbCR measure code `EARNINGS` as if it were labour compensation, which then fed directly into the SBIE, the behavioural regressions, and the appendix tables. That interpretation was wrong.

Step 1 now renames the CbCR `EARNINGS` measure to `accumulated_earnings` in the cleaned tables, so the data no longer invite the same mistake. Step 2 no longer treats any CbCR monetary field as observed payroll. Instead, the SBIE payroll input is now an explicit wage-based proxy, constructed as employee counts times the affiliate-jurisdiction annual wage benchmark. The appendix tables have been regenerated so they describe this variable correctly. The same point must now also be reflected in the thesis text before the methodology chapter is executed or interpreted.

This repair removes the original measurement error, but it also changes the interpretation of Stage 2. The payroll leg of the SBIE is now a second-best proxy rather than an observed tax-administration measure. That is methodologically defensible given the public data constraint, but it is not innocuous. Any claim that the thesis observes bilateral payroll directly from CbCR would now be false and should not appear in the written chapter.

### Second correction: a narrow employee-count screen was required

Once payroll was rebuilt from employee counts, a second data problem became visible. A very small number of pair-year cells carry employee counts that are impossible on any plausible reading of the bilateral data. The most obvious cases sit in the 2022 Romanian disclosure wave, including Romania-Israel with more than 41 million employees and Romania-United States with more than 52 million employees. These are not just large numbers. They imply millions of employees per reported constituent entity, and in one case they exceed the host-country population.

The response is deliberately narrow. Employee counts are now screened only when they exceed a conservative plausibility cap defined as the lower of host-country population and 100,000 employees per reported constituent entity. This affects 9 of the 16,915 pair-year cells, all in 2022. The rest of the panel is left untouched. This is preferable to broad winsorisation because the problem is highly concentrated and clearly tied to an identifiable disclosure anomaly rather than to a diffuse heavy tail.

The consequence is economically important. Before the screen, the wage-based payroll proxy inherited the impossible Romanian employee counts and generated trillion-dollar payroll bases for several small foreign legs. After the screen, the largest payroll proxies are large domestic economies such as the United States rather than implausible foreign outliers. The employee screen therefore protects both the SBIE base and the later labour control in the behavioural regressions without rewriting the bulk of the sample.

### Readiness must be separated by stage

The earlier `has_estimation_inputs` flag overstated readiness because it captured only the broad Hines-Rice regression requirements and ignored the narrower data requirements of Stage 1. The merged panel now separates these objects explicitly. The behavioural estimation sample contains 9,935 pair-year cells. The narrower Stage 1 hybrid-ready sample contains 2,512 pair-year cells. The direct pair-year overlap used for simultaneous percentile and Table IV information contains only 49 cells, but that is not the relevant aggregation level for Stage 1 itself.

Stage 1 works at the affiliate-year level after pooling across parents, and the new diagnostic file `diagnostics/step2_stage1_affiliate_year_coverage.csv` makes that visible. For 2021 and 2022 together, the global sample contains 370 affiliate-years with a complete Panel A-I percentile set, 410 affiliate-years with either Panel A-I or `_T` percentiles, and 199 affiliate-years with both a usable percentile source and a bilateral Table IV observation. For the EU-27, all 54 affiliate-years in 2021 and 2022 have a complete Panel A-I percentile set and all 54 also sit in the percentile-Table IV overlap. That is the economically relevant Stage 1 coverage statement; the 49 pair-year overlap in the bilateral base panel should not be confused with it.

### Supplementary controls and what is now genuinely ready

The statutory-tax-rate coverage diagnosis has also been tightened. The earlier appendix used only the OECD `top_all` combined statutory rate, which made coverage look much worse than it really is. The merged panel now carries a headline statutory CIT measure that uses the combined rate where available and otherwise falls back to the standard statutory rate. Coverage rises from 2,766 to 13,818 pair-year cells under this economically sensible cascade.

The new diagnostics show that the base panel is now technically ready for the behavioural estimation step, conditional on the thesis accepting the wage-based payroll proxy and the narrow employee screen. The panel is also ready for the hybrid Stage 1 design on its actual support: Table IV direct information from 2019 onward, and percentile-based information from 2021 onward. The methodology as written already works correctly , the bracket observation simply is not available for three of seven vintages , but the temporal statement needs correcting.
2. The cross-validation sample of `§5.1.3` (overlap between Table I percentiles and Table IV brackets) reduces to 2021--2022 by construction, since that is the only window where both objects exist. The Wilcoxon test, total-variation distance and regression `\eqref{eq:crossval_regression}` should be run on this two-year window and results should not be generalised to 2016--2020 without a stated assumption about temporal stability of the approximation error.

### Finding 3 , Adjusted profit regression is underpowered; adopt a benchmark ratio from Hugger (2026) and keep the regression only as a diagnostic

`PROFIT_ADJ` is observed for 33 Panel A-I cells in 2021--2022 (32 in 2021, 1 in 2022). All 33 of those cells also carry the holding-company share needed by equation `\eqref{eq:correction_model}`, so the regression is identified , but with N = 33 and a single covariate, the standard error on $\hat\beta$ will be wide enough that the predicted $\hat\gamma_{j,t}$ for 2016--2020 is essentially noise around a constant. Using this regression as the primary correction input, as `§4.5` currently does, is not supportable.

**Resolution:**

1. Replace the primary correction with the published ratio from \citet{hugger2026}: a median downward adjustment of 32.4% applied to the parent leg of UPE profit, capped at a 55.5% reduction. This is the correction set the OECD used in its own low-taxed profit estimates and has the virtue of being built on tax-authority information rather than an aggregate regression. Apply the Hugger cap to every parent--affiliate cell for 2016--2020; the 2021--2022 cells keep their observed `PROFIT_ADJ` where available and the Hugger ratio otherwise. This becomes the "baseline" level of Factor 4 (profit-base correction), replacing the predicted-ratio specification.
2. Keep the holding-share regression `\eqref{eq:correction_model}` as a descriptive test of the \citet{blouin2023} hypothesis that double counting scales with conduit intensity; report $\hat\beta$ and its standard error but do not use its fitted values to backcast. If $\hat\beta$ is significant and negative, that is supporting evidence for the Hugger-style correction; it is not a substitute for it.
3. Supplement the Hugger ratio with country-specific corrections from \citet{garcia-bernardo2022a} for the jurisdictions they explicitly document (Netherlands 16%, Sweden 51%, UK 51%, US domestic 54%, plus 10% of tax-haven profits for non-US MNEs). These enter as an overlay on top of the Hugger baseline for the named jurisdictions only.
4. The three factorial levels of Factor 4 become: (i) no correction ($\pi^{*} = \pi$) , upper bound; (ii) Hugger baseline with Garcia-Bernardo country overlay , central estimate; (iii) aggressive , apply the 55.5% cap uniformly. The spread across (i)--(iii) continues to play its role in the ANOVA decomposition of `§5.5`.

The cleaning script already exposes `act_holding_panelai` and `entities_count_panelai`, so no changes to Step 1 are needed for this; the overlay happens in Step 2.

### Finding 4 , Zero-profit cells in Panel A-I: 82 total, 8 of them with positive tax accrued

Of the 82 cells with `profit_panelai = 0`, 74 also have zero tax (the ETR numerator is 0/0 and the cell is uninformative) and 8 have positive tax (the ratio is $+\infty$). \citet{boukal2026} and \citet{hugger2026} both bound CbCR ETRs at zero and cap the upper tail at the statutory rate plus loss adjustment; neither defines ETR on a zero-profit denominator.

**Resolution:**

1. Drop the 82 zero-profit cells from ETR numerator/denominator calculations in Step 3. They do not enter `\eqref{eq:etr}`, `\eqref{eq:jurisdiction_percentile}`, or `\eqref{eq:panel_linear}` (since $\log \pi$ is undefined at zero anyway).
2. Retain them in the substance base aggregation used for SBIE (payroll, assets, employee counts). The zero profit is a legitimate observation of real activity with no accounting profit in the reporting year; excluding the substance would bias the SBIE denominator.
3. Winsorise the per-cell ETR at $[0, \bar\tau^{\text{stat}}_j + \text{loss adjustment}]$ following the Hugger bounds, applied after the location shift of `\eqref{eq:shift}`. This handles the 8 positive-tax-on-zero-profit cells implicitly by excluding them (division by zero) without needing a separate rule.

## 2026-04-18 - Step 2: base correction and merging (`code/02_base_correction_and_merging.R`)

### Merge architecture

Step 2 builds the bilateral base panel on the economically meaningful CbCR unit, one row per parent jurisdiction, affiliate jurisdiction, and fiscal year. Table I remains the spine because it is the only table that covers the full 2016-2022 horizon with the profit, tax, payroll, assets, and percentile information needed later in the pipeline. The merge removes only the non-country aggregate affiliate codes, namely the regional totals, `W`, `W_O`, `WXD`, and `STLS`. Domestic cells are retained. They are essential for the double-counting correction because the Hugger-style adjustment is defined on the domestic UPE leg.

The resulting country-like panel contains 16,915 bilateral rows, 58 parent jurisdictions, 228 affiliate jurisdictions, and all seven fiscal years from 2016 to 2022. Fiscal year 2016 is retained but explicitly flagged as a transition vintage, consistent with the thesis text and the OECD treatment of the first CbCR release. The main estimation window remains 2017-2022.

The other CbCR tables are added only where they can be reduced to the same pair-year key without inventing structure. Table II is merged in full as interquartile-mean controls. Table III and Table IV are not flattened across all ETR brackets, which would create bracket-level duplication of the bilateral key. Instead the script keeps the policy-relevant `RATE_0-15` slice from each table, because that is the direct low-tax benchmark needed for Stage 1 cross-validation. Table V is merged on the parent-year key because MNE size is a parent characteristic. Table VI is widened over its size classes before the join, so the 2022 size composition is kept without breaking one-row-per-pair-year uniqueness.

The same principle governs the non-CbCR sources. Supplementary datasets that live naturally on a jurisdiction-year key, such as statutory CIT rates, GDP per capita, population, BEPS implementation indicators, CbCR filing rules, and Pillar Two implementation dates, are merged directly onto the bilateral panel with explicit parent and affiliate prefixes. Datasets that remain fundamentally multi-dimensional are not forced into the base panel. Eurostat FATS is saved separately as `base_panel_fats_sector_shares.rds` at the affiliate-year-sector level. The forward-looking OECD ETR files, the dividend tax series, the hourly wage series, and the broad comparative tax-revenue file remain in their cleaned long form from Step 1. Flattening them now would require an arbitrary measure choice that belongs in a later analytical step, not in the construction of the base data.

### Missing-value handling

The only economically defensible imputation carried out in Step 2 is the payroll imputation already stated in the methodology. The script constructs an affiliate-year wage benchmark with a strict hierarchy. OECD annual average wages in PPP-adjusted USD come first. If that is unavailable, the script uses ILO monthly earnings in PPP terms and annualises them by multiplying by twelve. Only if PPP data are absent does it fall back to ILO monthly earnings in USD. Within each source, the nearest available year for the same jurisdiction is used, with ties broken in favour of the earlier observation. This keeps the imputation close to the observed wage path of the jurisdiction rather than importing a global average.

The imputation burden is small. Only 25 Panel A-I rows and 27 Panel A-II rows require wage-based payroll imputation. Wage coverage exists for 14,685 of the 16,915 bilateral cells. Missing payroll after this step is therefore concentrated in the difficult cells where neither CbCR payroll nor the employee count-wage combination is available. The script does not impute tangible assets, profits, taxes, or percentile moments. Those objects either have no credible deterministic bridge or belong to later modelling steps. Instead the panel records transparent coverage flags, so later scripts can distinguish a true economic zero from a missing observation.

For the SBIE base the script prefers the broader Panel A-II activity measures and falls back to Panel A-I only where the all-entity series is missing. This is an important policy choice. The ETR denominator belongs on profit-making entities, but the SBIE is a substance measure and should not be narrowed to profit-makers if a broader activity measure is available. The merged panel therefore carries a dedicated `payroll_sbie` and `assets_sbie` base, together with source flags showing whether the value comes from observed Panel A-II, imputed Panel A-II, observed Panel A-I, imputed Panel A-I, or the panel-agnostic aggregate.

### Profit-base correction

Step 2 applies the deterministic profit correction in the form now used by the thesis, and this required one conceptual clarification. Earlier notes referred to profit correction as a separate "Factor 4". The current thesis text no longer treats it as one of the five orthogonal pipeline factors. It is now an upstream data-conditioning choice. The code follows that structure. It stores three profit bases side by side: the uncorrected upper bound, the preferred baseline correction, and the aggressive lower-bound sensitivity. The baseline corrected profit is also exposed as `analysis_profit_base`, because it is the object later used in the estimation and revenue pipeline.

The baseline correction follows equations (4.5) and (4.6) in the current thesis draft. If `PROFIT_ADJ` is observed, the script uses it directly. If not, domestic UPE cells are reduced by 54 per cent for the United States, 16 per cent for the Netherlands, and 32.4 per cent for all other parents. Foreign non-US cells in separately identified conduit or haven jurisdictions receive a 10 per cent downward overlay. The conduit-and-haven set used in the code is the set explicitly named in the literature discussion of the thesis: Bermuda, the Cayman Islands, Ireland, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Switzerland, Singapore, Hong Kong, Puerto Rico, and the United Kingdom. The aggressive correction keeps observed `PROFIT_ADJ` and the same conduit overlay, but replaces the generic 32.4 per cent domestic rate with the Hugger cap of 55.5 per cent.

The merged data confirm that the correction must remain deterministic rather than regression-driven. The `PROFIT_ADJ` donor sample is still far too narrow for a globally transportable fitted model, but the exact pattern in the draft text needed correction. The panel contains 36 country-like Panel A-I observations with reported `PROFIT_ADJ`, not 33. Twenty-seven belong to the Dutch 2021 disclosure wave, one is the Dutch domestic 2022 cell, and eight are earlier UK and Dutch domestic observations in 2017-2020. None of the observed ratios exceeds one, and the minimum observed `PROFIT_ADJ / PROFIT` ratio is 0.516. The sample remains too concentrated to support a global regression correction, but the empirical description in the thesis draft should now be updated to reflect these 36 observations rather than the earlier 33-cell Netherlands-only statement.

In the baseline merged panel, 308 domestic rows receive the Hugger-style domestic correction, 1,436 foreign conduit or haven rows receive the 10 per cent overlay, and 36 rows use observed `PROFIT_ADJ`. The remaining 15,135 foreign operating rows are left unchanged. This is materially more targeted than a parent-wide multiplicative haircut, and it matches the accounting mechanism discussed by Blouin more closely.

### Descriptive flags raised during the join

The join produces several descriptive flags that matter for later interpretation. First, direct subsidiary-level low-tax information from Table IV is available for 760 bilateral rows once the panel is restricted to country-like affiliates. Among these observed cells, the mean `RATE_0-15` profit share is 47.1 per cent of Panel A-I profit, and the 95th percentile reaches 100 per cent. This confirms that the hybrid Stage 1 design will be working with substantial heterogeneity, not with marginal bracket noise.

Second, the missingness pattern of the supplementary controls is asymmetric. World Bank GDP per capita and population are missing for only about 2 to 4 per cent of rows, mainly because of small territories and special jurisdictions not covered by the World Bank country list. By contrast, the OECD statutory CIT series covers only a minority of the bilateral panel because many small offshore jurisdictions and dependencies are not in the OECD tax database. This is not an error in the join. It is a property of the underlying source coverage and needs to be respected in later regressions.

Third, the FATS sidecar is cleaner than expected once it is aggregated over controlling countries. For all 27 EU Member States and both 2021 and 2022, the post-2021 FATS file yields 48 NACE rows per affiliate-year and the payroll, employment, investment, and turnover shares each sum to one up to rounding error. The main structural limitation of FATS remains conceptual rather than mechanical: the shares describe all foreign-controlled firms, not only the large in-scope CbCR population.

Finally, the merged panel now carries explicit readiness flags for later stages. A row is marked as ready for the main Hines-Rice estimation sample only if it lies in 2017-2022 and has positive corrected profit, non-missing ETR, and non-missing SBIE inputs. After all joins and imputations, 5,393 rows satisfy these conditions. This is the effective Step 2 bridge from raw CbCR coverage to the estimation sample.

### Outputs

Step 2 writes the main bilateral object to `data/processed/base_panel.rds` and the EU sectoral sidecar to `data/processed/base_panel_fats_sector_shares.rds`. The diagnostics folder now also contains six step-specific files: a panel summary, a year-by-year payroll imputation summary, a year-by-year payroll-plausibility summary, a year-by-year profit-correction summary, a missingness summary for the merged controls, and a FATS sector-coverage file. These files are intended to make the later analytical steps transparent and to prevent rediscovering the same data limitations at each stage.

### Working-appendix descriptive tables

The working appendix now pulls the Step 2 descriptive statistics from a dedicated regeneration script, `code/02b_base_panel_appendix_tables.R`, rather than hard-coding the numbers in `writing/thesisv2.tex`. This is methodologically preferable because the appendix tables are direct descriptive outputs of the merged base panel; if the Step 2 merge logic or the processed inputs change, the appendix should refresh mechanically rather than relying on manual editing.

The script reads `data/processed/base_panel.rds` and writes a LaTeX fragment to `writing/generated/base_panel_appendix_tables.tex`. The thesis file includes that fragment with `\input{generated/base_panel_appendix_tables.tex}` inside the working appendix block. Regeneration therefore requires only two steps: rerun `code/02_base_correction_and_merging.R` if the merged panel has changed, then rerun `code/02b_base_panel_appendix_tables.R` to refresh the appendix tables.

The script currently produces three appendix tables. First, a year-by-year coverage table for the full 2016-2022 bilateral panel, including the number of cells, parent jurisdictions, affiliate jurisdictions, percentile availability, direct Table IV low-tax observations, and the size of the estimation-ready sample. Second, an unweighted summary-statistics table for the main 2017-2022 estimation sample, reporting the mean, standard deviation, quartiles, and observation count for the corrected profit base, tax accrued, SBIE inputs, raw ETR, and core jurisdiction controls. Third, a construction-flags table that records how many cells are domestic, EU-based, directly corrected with observed `PROFIT_ADJ`, adjusted by the domestic Hugger rule, adjusted by the conduit overlay, supported by observed or imputed payroll, supported by direct Table IV bracket information, or retained in the estimation sample.

This setup also makes the empirical definitions transparent. The appendix summary table is computed on the exact `has_estimation_inputs` sample used downstream, not on the full merged panel. The flag table is instead anchored to the full 16,915-cell panel, so each share is interpretable as a construction share of the universe built in Step 2. That distinction matters. Mixing the two denominators would look tidy in presentation terms, but it would be econometrically sloppy because it would conflate sample-selection diagnostics with descriptive moments of the estimation sample.

### Payroll plausibility correction after appendix review

The first round of appendix statistics revealed a genuine Step 2 measurement problem rather than a cosmetic outlier issue. The initial merged panel produced an average SBIE payroll base of USD 10.2 billion per pair-year in the estimation sample, but the median was only USD 55 million and the maximum exceeded USD 25 trillion. The clearest example was the 2017 China--Greece cell, which carried roughly USD 25.1 trillion of payroll for 1,482 employees. That is not an economically meaningful heavy tail. It is a contaminated payroll observation, and leaving it in would mechanically overstate the SBIE, shrink the excess-profit base, and bias GMT revenue downward.

The correction is now built directly into `code/02_base_correction_and_merging.R`. The script keeps the raw payroll values, but it also constructs a jurisdiction-specific benchmark equal to employee counts times the local annual wage used elsewhere in Step 2. For Panel A-I, Panel A-II, and the aggregate fallback alike, observed payroll is capped when it exceeds twenty times that benchmark. The threshold is deliberately conservative. A factor of twenty is high enough to avoid forcing productive multinationals onto the local average wage, but low enough to catch clearly impossible CbCR values. The code stores both the raw and corrected payroll inputs, a correction flag, the raw-to-benchmark ratio, the effective cap, and the share of payroll removed by the correction.

Empirically the correction matters. Across the full 16,915-cell merged panel, 1,103 cells are capped by the plausibility rule. Within the 5,393-cell main estimation sample, 775 cells are capped, or 14.4% of the sample. The impact is concentrated in the right tail, exactly where the earlier appendix diagnosis suggested it should be. The mean SBIE payroll base in the estimation sample falls from USD 10.194 billion to USD 4.467 billion, while the median moves only from roughly USD 55 million to USD 47 million and the sample size does not change. In aggregate, the correction removes 56.2% of raw payroll mass from the estimation sample. That sounds large, but it is economically more plausible than leaving a small set of impossible payroll values to dominate the substance denominator.

The year pattern confirms that the problem was persistent rather than confined to one disclosure wave. The new diagnostic file `diagnostics/step2_payroll_plausibility_by_year.csv` shows capped shares between 5.2% and 7.0% of all pair-year cells in every vintage from 2016 to 2022. The raw payroll reduction is especially large in 2017 because of the extreme China--Greece observation, but every year exhibits a non-trivial correction. The companion file `diagnostics/step2_payroll_imputation_by_year.csv` now also records the number of capped Panel A-I, Panel A-II, and aggregate payroll cells, so the plausibility correction can be read alongside the original wage-imputation summary rather than being hidden as an undocumented overwrite.

This change fits the thesis methodology cleanly and should be described that way in the text. In the Stage 2 section of `thesisv2.tex`, the payroll component $\hat{W}_{p,j,t}$ is now defined as observed or imputed payroll subject to a conservative plausibility cap based on local wages and employment. This is not a departure from the economic logic of the chapter. It is a protection against a specific data-quality failure that would otherwise push the substance carve-out in the wrong direction. Because the same payroll variable is later used as a control in the Hines--Rice estimation, the correction also reduces measurement error in the regression stage rather than affecting only the SBIE calculation.

The working appendix now reflects this revised interpretation. `code/02b_base_panel_appendix_tables.R` regenerates the appendix tables from the corrected base panel, labels the payroll statistic explicitly as the corrected SBIE payroll base, and records the new payroll-capping flag in the construction table. The appendix notes also clarify that the reported ETR summary remains the raw Panel A-I ratio before the later Stage 3 bounding step. This is important for interpretation. The payroll problem required a Step 2 correction because it polluted the underlying quantity directly; the raw ETR tail remains a modelling issue for later stages because it reflects small denominators and tax refunds rather than an obvious merge error.

### Source trace of the extreme payroll values

After implementing the plausibility cap, the next question was whether the extreme payroll values originated in the thesis pipeline or in the source data itself. This was traced all the way back through `code/02_base_correction_and_merging.R`, `code/01_data_cleaning.R`, the raw OECD CSV file in `data/raw/oecd_cbcr/`, and the original download logic in `code/collect_all_data.py`.

The collection step is not the source of the anomaly. In `code/collect_all_data.py`, the CbCR collector only constructs the OECD SDMX endpoint and streams the returned bytes directly to disk. The relevant logic is operationally simple: `download_oecd()` builds the SDMX URL and `download_file()` writes the response to `data/raw/oecd_cbcr/cbcr_table1_aggregate_totals.csv` without any parsing, rescaling, or column-wise manipulation. The collector therefore cannot inflate `EARNINGS`, change units, multiply values, or duplicate observations. If a payroll value is extreme in the raw CSV, that extremity comes from the OECD response itself.

The cleaning step is not the source either. In `code/01_data_cleaning.R`, Table I is read from `data/raw/oecd_cbcr/cbcr_table1_aggregate_totals.csv`, columns are renamed, and the long file is pivoted wide into `cbcr_table1.rds`. There is no arithmetic on `EARNINGS`, no currency conversion, no aggregation over multiple rows, and no adjustment by panel. A direct check on the raw `EARNINGS` keys showed zero duplicate combinations of reporting jurisdiction, affiliate jurisdiction, year, panel, and statistical operation before the pivot. This means the wide transformation does not combine multiple `EARNINGS` rows into one inflated value.

The flagship anomaly, the China--Greece pair in 2017, is present in the original OECD download exactly as it later appears in the processed panel. In `data/raw/oecd_cbcr/cbcr_table1_aggregate_totals.csv`, the rows for `REF_AREA = CHN`, `COUNTERPART_AREA = GRC`, `TIME_PERIOD = 2017`, `MEASURE = EARNINGS` report `OBS_VALUE = 2.507134e+13` both for `PROFIT_GROUPING = PANELAI` and for `PROFIT_GROUPING = _T`. The file records these values in `UNIT_MEASURE = USD`, `DECIMALS = 0`, and `OBS_STATUS = A`. The same value then appears in `data/processed/cbcr_table1.rds`, and from there enters the merged base panel. The thesis pipeline therefore preserved the value; it did not create it.

The same conclusion holds for other large cells that triggered the plausibility correction. Belgium--United Kingdom in 2020, Saudi Arabia domestic in 2021, United States--Bermuda in 2017, and Indonesia--Mauritius in 2022 all appear directly in the raw OECD CSV as `EARNINGS` observations in USD with `DECIMALS = 0` and `OBS_STATUS = A`. Some of these observations are very large but still potentially plausible for major domestic economies; others are not. The key point is narrower and more important: the pipeline is not manufacturing them.

To verify this systematically rather than anecdotally, the capped observations in the final base panel were matched back to the original OECD rows. All 1,103 capped cells in `base_panel.rds` matched an underlying raw `EARNINGS` observation in the downloaded OECD file. None of the capped cells lacked a corresponding raw row. The source-panel composition is also informative. Of the capped cells, 514 originate from Panel A-II `EARNINGS`, 388 from Panel A-I, and 201 from the aggregate `_T` fallback. Every matched raw row carried `UNIT_MEASURE = USD`, and every matched raw row carried `OBS_STATUS = A`. This rules out the simplest internal-pipeline explanations such as a misaligned join, a duplication of Panel A-I and Panel A-II rows, or a mismatch between currencies.

The broader pattern in raw Table I confirms that this is not one isolated cell. Among the raw Panel A-I observations with both payroll and employment reported, 95 imply more than USD 100 million of payroll per employee and 20 imply more than USD 1 billion per employee. These magnitudes are impossible as labour costs. More generally, the distribution of raw `EARNINGS / EMPLOYEES` is far too dispersed to be interpreted literally in every cell. The worst values are concentrated in well-known conduit and haven locations and in some very small affiliate jurisdictions, but they are not limited to them.

What, then, is the likely root cause. The evidence supports a source-data problem, but not a single neatly identifiable mechanism. With only the public OECD aggregates, we cannot distinguish with certainty between several possibilities: reporting errors by national tax authorities, miscoding of one financial item as `EARNINGS`, contamination of payroll by non-labour flows during national aggregation, or more complex panel-definition inconsistencies between Panel A-I, Panel A-II, and `_T`. What we can say with confidence is that the anomaly is upstream of the thesis code. It is present in the original OECD Table I file as downloaded by the collector, survives the cleaning step unchanged, and only becomes consequential in Step 2 when payroll is used as a direct substance input for the SBIE and as a regression control.

This distinction matters for the thesis. The payroll plausibility cap is not a patch for a programming bug. It is a deliberate data-conditioning correction for an upstream measurement problem in the published CbCR aggregates. That is methodologically defensible. If the public source contains payroll values that are internally incompatible with employment counts and local wage levels, the analyst should not treat those values as economically meaningful substance. At the same time, the source trace implies that the thesis should not overstate certainty about the mechanism behind the anomaly. The correct claim is that some published OECD CbCR payroll aggregates are not empirically credible as labour-cost measures at the pair-year level, and therefore require screening before they can enter SBIE calculations.

For the written thesis, this source trace supports two presentational choices. First, the Stage 2 methodology should explicitly state that the payroll plausibility cap addresses upstream source anomalies in published CbCR payroll aggregates rather than missing-data imputation error. Second, the working appendix should keep the raw-ETR summary and the corrected payroll summary conceptually separate. The former remains a later modelling issue; the latter is now shown to be a verified source-data issue already visible in the original OECD Table I release.

## 2026-04-20 , Stage 1 audit, thesis corrections, and results section

This entry documents a full audit of the Stage 1 pipeline against the thesis methodology, a set of targeted corrections to the thesis text, and a new Stage 1 results section. No change to the economic logic of the pipeline. The motivation is alignment: several numerical claims in the methodology section of `writing/thesisv2.tex` were written against an earlier vintage of the diagnostics and were no longer consistent with the outputs actually produced by `code/03_stage1_etr.R` on the current `base_panel.rds`.

### Audit findings from the current outputs

The authoritative Stage 1 diagnostic CSVs in `data/processed/diagnostics/` were re-read against the thesis prose. Three sets of numbers disagreed.

First, the bilateral-panel branch counts. The thesis claimed 760 direct Table IV cells, 2,976 percentile-fit cells, 3,899 BSPI shape-transport cells, and 9,280 jurisdiction-average fallback cells. The current pipeline produces 9,395, 874, 3,899, and 1,459 respectively on the 15,758 pair-year panel, plus 131 residual cells without any source. The shape-transport count still matches; the other three were stale. The BSPI shape-transport count is stable because the 2017-2018 EU27 bilateral universe has been fixed by the step 2 merge for some time. The other counts moved because the step 2 revision introduced tighter Stage 1 input availability rules and because the percentile cascade from Panel A-I to `_T` was finalised after those numbers were first written.

Second, the EU27 BSPI shape-stability statistics. The thesis claimed a median total-variation distance of 0.10 with a maximum of 0.52 in Malta, and listed five EU affiliates above the 0.39 flag threshold (Lithuania, Croatia, Malta, Latvia, Greece). The current outputs give a median of 0.09, a maximum of 0.67 in Lithuania, and only three affiliates above the threshold: Lithuania (0.67), Croatia (0.56), and Greece (0.42). Malta turns out to have TV = 0.02, comfortably in the stable group. The country-specific TV values for the big conduits are close but not identical to the earlier numbers, for example Luxembourg now 0.11 rather than 0.10 and the Netherlands 0.12 rather than 0.09. The economically important conclusion is unchanged: the conduit jurisdictions pass the shape-stability test, and the three flagged affiliates are small-profit cases where an anchor-to-validation shift is plausibly real.

Third, the BSPI coverage claim. The thesis said BSPI delivers heterogeneity for 1,016 of the 1,165 pre-2019 EU27 pair-year cells, with 149 remaining cells falling back to the jurisdiction-average rule. The current bilateral panel contains exactly 1,016 pre-2019 EU27 pair-years and all of them are populated through BSPI; there is no pre-2019 EU27 fallback subset. ## 2026-05-02, Stage 3 IV and SESH methodology revision

This entry records the implementation of the statutory-CIT instrumental-variable robustness design and a tightening of the SESH bunching interpretation in `writing/thesisv2.tex`. The starting point was a proposed narrative that would have treated the statutory-rate diagnostic as a full identification solution. The evidence does not support that stronger claim. The lagged statutory CIT rate is a relevant instrument for CbCR ETR, but the reduced form and the 2SLS coefficient are imprecise. The thesis should therefore present the design as a formal linear FE-2SLS robustness estimate, not as the preferred estimator for the nonlinear revenue counterfactual.

## 2026-05-03, Stage 1 ETR measurement-error propagation

This entry documents an optional uncertainty layer for the distributional ETR measurement. The substantive aim is narrower than the existing 32-scenario factorial spread. The factorial design compares different modelling choices, including average versus distributional ETR measurement. The new simulation instead asks how much revenue moves if the preferred distributional Stage 1 input is noisy, conditional on keeping the preferred ETR approach, aggregate SBIE, and initial carve-out rates fixed.

The implementation is in `code/09_stage1_measurement_error_simulation.R`. The calibration uses the existing 2021-2022 validation overlap where Table I percentile-fit estimates can be compared with direct Table IV subsidiary-bracket observations. For each affiliate-year in the simulation, the script draws two centred empirical residuals from that overlap: one for the low-taxed profit share and one for the mean ETR inside the low-taxed bracket. The centring is deliberate. It treats the cross-validation bias as a known validation result already reported in Stage 1, while the Monte Carlo interval isolates dispersion around that validation relationship. The deterministic baseline scripts remain unchanged. This is important for the thesis architecture: the baseline pipeline still produces one reproducible set of preferred estimates, and the measurement-error simulation is a separate uncertainty layer that can be run when needed.

The simulation writes calibration, draw-level and interval outputs to `data/processed/stage1_measurement_error_*.rds` and `data/processed/diagnostics/step9_stage1_measurement_error_*.csv`. ## 2026-05-03, Stage 3 simulated tax-code instrument diagnostic

This entry records the behavioural-identification revision prompted by the simulated-instrument idea. The substantive decision is that the thesis should not claim that the current IV solves the endogeneity problem. The fixed-effect model absorbs persistent parent-affiliate heterogeneity and common year shocks, but the CbCR ETR remains mechanically endogenous because reported profit is also the denominator of the tax rate. A simulated tax-code instrument is a better diagnostic than the raw statutory rate, but the public data do not contain the legal schedules needed to simulate depreciation rules, R&D credits and BEPS rules manually for each jurisdiction. The defensible implementation therefore uses the OECD forward-looking effective average tax rate for a fixed composite investment project. This holds the investment project fixed and varies the affiliate jurisdiction's tax-code environment as modelled by the OECD.

The code change is in `code/05_stage3_behavioral.R`. ## 2026-05-04, Behavioural cap and reallocation-channel revision

This entry records a substantive tightening of the Stage 3 behavioural counterfactual. The motivation was publication risk. The previous behavioural layer contained three choices that would be difficult to defend in a seminar or referee report: a 65 per cent cap on the strategic reallocation share, a 67 per cent transfer-pricing/IP, 25 per cent debt and 8 per cent substance-planning split, and an activity-score multiplier that amplified the panel-estimated semi-elasticity. Those choices were not identified from public CbCR data. They also created a mismatch with the thesis text, which presents the behavioural response as the estimated semi-elasticity response, not as a planning-score simulation.

The revised decision is conservative. The legally allocated GMT revenue matrix now uses the estimated semi-elasticity response from the fixed-effect CbCR model, the SESH notch, and a transparent legal collection exposure indicator. If a QDMTT applies, or if the universal regime leaves a collection channel active, the semi-elasticity response is active. If the SbS regime exempts a US-parented group and no QDMTT applies, the response is switched off because the legal top-up exposure is extinguished. The old fractional exposure weights of 0.90, 0.70 and 0.25 are removed. The 65 per cent cap is also removed. The only remaining cap is the logical unit-interval bound, since a public aggregate CbCR panel does not identify a lower structural maximum.

The channel decomposition is now diagnostic only. It no longer affects legally allocated top-up tax revenue. The short-run channel split is anchored on the Heckemeyer and Overesch meta-regression distinction between transfer-pricing/licensing and financial-planning channels: 82 per cent transfer pricing or IP/licensing, 18 per cent debt, and 0 per cent substance relocation in the short-run behavioural filter. This is more coherent with the SBIE methodology because payroll and tangible assets are held fixed in the short-run counterfactual. Substance relocation remains a longer-run real activity margin, suitable for a limitations section or a separate extension, not for the baseline behavioural filter.

`code/05_stage3_behavioral.R` now writes a new diagnostic object, `step5_stage3_behavioural_identification_design.csv`, also saved as `stage3_behavioural_identification_design.rds`. This table states which behavioural components are identified in the current thesis and which require additional data. The key conclusion is that a structural gravity, conditional-logit or PPML destination model is not identified by public OECD CbCR alone. Public CbCR reports parent-affiliate profit stocks, not origin-destination flows of shifted income. Estimating PPML on those stocks would mix real activity, previously shifted book profit and the unobserved destination of post-GMT reallocation. A structural extension would require group-level or affiliate-level CbCR, intra-group royalty and service payments, interest flows, debt positions, IP ownership, withholding-tax and treaty links, and anti-avoidance rules such as interest limitations and CFC regimes.

`code/08_generate_results_tables.R` now turns this diagnostic into `writing/generated/results_tables/appendix_stage3_behavioural_design.tex`, and the generated appendix manifest includes the new table. `writing/thesisv2.tex` now makes the methodological boundary explicit. The behavioural-response section says that the revenue matrix does not multiply the semi-elasticity response by channel-specific planning scores and does not impose a discretionary cap below one. The reallocation subsection explains why a structural PPML or conditional-logit matrix would be attractive in principle but not identified with the public data used here.

The numerical impact is material and improves interpretability. In the preferred behavioural scenario, avoided top-up tax falls from roughly USD 338.8 billion under the previous heuristic-amplified layer to USD 193.9 billion under the revised semi-elasticity-based layer. Preferred reallocated profit falls from roughly USD 3.15 trillion to USD 1.82 trillion. In the Stage 4.6 pooled ANOVA, the behavioural main-effect share falls to 5.0 per cent, while ETR measurement and the SbS regime remain the dominant modelling choices. ## 2026-05-04, Leave-one-jurisdiction influence robustness

This entry records the influence diagnostic added after reviewing the current robustness strategy. The key methodological point is that the existing robustness checks mostly test modelling choices inside the behavioural estimation, while they do not answer a separate publication-risk question: whether the public CbCR panel is dominated by a small number of large parent or affiliate jurisdictions. That influence question is especially important for this thesis because the final revenue object combines a bilateral CbCR tax base with legal collection rules. A result can be robust to functional form and still be too dependent on the United States as parent, or on the Netherlands, Ireland, Luxembourg, Germany, France, Switzerland or Singapore as affiliate jurisdictions.

The first decision was to correct the robustness narrative in `writing/thesisv2.tex`. The previous text said that the pipeline included ETR-quartile splits and a 2017 cross-sectional replication of Garcia-Bernardo and Jansky. Those diagnostics are not currently written by the code, so the claim was too strong. The revised text now describes the implemented checks: balanced-pair, stale-wage exclusion and no-2020-2021 samples; affiliate and parent-affiliate clustered inference; the OECD simulated fixed-project EATR and lagged statutory-CIT IV diagnostics; and the new leave-one influence suite. This makes the manuscript more defensible because it no longer promises robustness evidence that is absent from the reproducible pipeline.

The implementation is in `code/10_jurisdiction_influence_robustness.R`. The script selects the named jurisdictions requested for the policy question and adds the largest high-profit parent and affiliate jurisdictions in the Stage 1 base. In the current run this produced 26 cases. For each case it drops the jurisdiction once as a parent and once as an affiliate where available. It then refits the Stage 3 semi-elasticity model, recomputes profit-weighted Stage 1 liability rates, rebuilds the Stage 4.5 legal allocation, and recomputes the Stage 4.6 factorial decomposition. The deterministic Stage 1 distributional measurement and Stage 2 SBIE rules remain fixed. This is deliberate: the diagnostic asks how influential the observed jurisdiction rows are, not whether the entire measurement architecture should be re-estimated after pretending a genuine large jurisdiction does not exist.

The outputs are written to `data/processed/diagnostics/step10_jurisdiction_influence_*.csv` and `data/processed/jurisdiction_influence_*.rds`. `code/08_generate_results_tables.R` now turns the case summary and decomposition ranges into two appendix tables: `appendix_jurisdiction_influence_summary.tex` and `appendix_jurisdiction_influence_decomposition.tex`. The Stage 1 liability rate and final revenue decomposition are more exposed to jurisdiction composition.
