## Factors taken into account in grading process

- **Societal relevance**: consistency of aim of research and question, proposed main research question and logical subquestions, connection to societal challenges. Demarcation, contextualisation, accountability of topic that leads to clear and relevant aim 
- **Scientific relevance**: describe anticipated contribution to existing literature, literature made applicable to own research, extend and relevancce of studied literature is outstanding, independent critical and in-depth review on literature, anticipated contribution to literature clearly specified, coherence between research question and anticipated contribution to literature/hypotheses
- **Methodology**: excellent research approach for research question, accountability of data collection and analysis, systematic operationalisation of research question, validity and reliability of study
- **Research approach** and implementation: one of most important parts: adequate quantitative analysis, adequate hypothesis testing or derivation of propositions, complete and to the point presentation of results
- **Interpretation of findings**: most important, answer based on literature and empirical/theoretical research, confrontation and intelligent combination of theoretical insights and empirical data, reflection on results via practical significance and policy recommendations
- **General presentation**: clear and functional structuring of thesis, appealing layout, grammatically correct and very good British English, accountability of sources and quotes
- **Conducting research in independent and self-responsible way**: independent, dealing with feedback, ingenuity, daring and relecance, performance in meetings

# Writing style

- British English
- Very concrete sentences that have meaning
- Never write more or less than you need to make your point.
- master thesis should be written in good, active, clean and simple academic English. Be proud of your work and humble about its implications. The reader is always right. If the reader fails to get your brilliant ideas, it is your responsibility to improve your text. You need to decide on what you want to say and convince/seduce the reader to pay attention and read on.
- Every paragraph starts with a sentence summarizing its content.
- mix short and long sentences
-  Avoid sentences with sub-clauses, Avoid comma if you can use period, Avoid auxillary words, Avoid repetition of the same or similar terms, Avoid fill words that do not affect the content, Avoid technical jargon of the discipline
- avoid plagarism at all costs, in my .md notes I have written many times in the same language as the article or summarised by copying the most important parts of a sentence, including this again is still plagaraism. So avoid this and write in new language.

## Supervisor feedback (March 2026)

- Avoid hedging constructions: "this thesis aims to", "can be seen as", "it would appear that", "may suggest". Replace with direct, declarative statements.
- Be confident. "The GMT is the most consequential reform..." not "The GMT can be seen as one of the most ambitious...".
- Contributions should be stated actively with strong verbs: "This thesis delivers / provides / demonstrates / cross-validates..." not "it is the first..." (lowercase bullet).
- No bullets of any form
- Check each time that the writing style is really in line with what is expected from an academic paper

- Avoid AI sounding language
- do not use constructions with "not this, but yes this" that is often used in ChatGPT language
- Watch for formulaic sentence patterns: topic sentence → supporting evidence → transition. Vary the structure.
- Flag phrases like "In this thesis I...", "The goal is to...", "This approach allows...", "It is worth noting that..." — these are common AI filler constructions.
- Read each paragraph aloud. If it sounds like a template, rewrite it.
- State the main finding in the introduction and abstract first. The reader should know the answer before reading the argument. They can stop reading if uninterested; they should want to read on because the argument is interesting, not because the answer is hidden.
- In the discussion/conclusion section: state the answer to the research question in the first paragraph, then explain and nuance.
- When a claim in the main text carries a known limitation or a dissenting view in the literature, add a footnote rather than cluttering the main argument.
- Format: main text states the position you take; footnote notes the contrasting evidence and why you take your position despite it. E.g.: main text says jurisdiction-average ETRs understate the GMT base; footnote cites Barake (2022) who uses them anyway and explains the data constraint that justified it.
- This shows the reader that you have thought carefully about the evidence and have made a deliberate choice, rather than overlooking the alternative.
- For each paper discussed: state what it contributes, but also state what it cannot do or where it falls short. One sentence of critique per paper is the minimum.
- Identify which limitations of prior work your thesis addresses. Make this connection explicit rather than leaving the reader to infer it.
- Position papers against each other where they disagree. Do not just list; argue.
- Whenever you introduce a new formula, notation, or framework that the literature has not used, state this explicitly: "The $\rho$ notation is introduced in this thesis..." or "The application of a $2^5$ factorial design to GMT estimation is original to this thesis."
- check that all abreheviations are first mentioned in full in the text, and also included in the list of abbreviations
- always mention tables in the text. 
- Help the reader see where to cite you in future work.
- Before submitting, go through the thesis and check: for every non-trivial element, is it clearly attributed to a prior source, or clearly marked as your own contribution? Nothing should be ambiguous.
- Any named dataset or database should have a URL in a footnote on first reference in the appendix (and optionally in the main text). The reader should be able to locate the data without further searching.
- Include the access date if the dataset is regularly updated.
- When using another author's formula or notation with minor modification, note explicitly what you changed and why. E.g.: "Following \citet{hugger2026}, but replacing their absolute SBIE with the ratio $\rho$..."
- Check original sources for claims about new concepts (e.g., SBIE-to-profit ratio: is this from Devereux, Schjelderup, or is the analytical use of the ratio original to you?). Verify before submitting.

- Each section and each subsection begins with one plain-language, but still academic, sentence stating what the section does and why the reader should care. No formula, no abbreviation, no citation in that opening sentence. The reader should know from it alone whether to continue, skim, or skip.
- Define every abbreviation at first use in the running text, even when the abbreviation also appears in the list of abbreviations. Write the full term followed by the abbreviation in parentheses the first time. Do not rely on the reader remembering what "IIR" or "UTPR" means three subsections later. If an abbreviation is reintroduced after a long gap, repeat the full form once.
- Before long equation chains (mixture distributions, counterfactual integrals, ANOVA identities), give the reader a one-line map: what the chain produces, what the inputs are, and where it is used downstream. The mathematics then follows as derivation rather than as revelation.
- Anchor notation in a single reference table (Table \ref{tab:notation}) and resist redefining symbols across sections. When a new symbol is introduced locally, remind the reader of it in words: "$\phi$, the ratio of the substance carve-out to profit".
- SAt the end of each major section, one sentence recaps what was established and what the next section will do. This is not a summary; it is a navigation aid.
