# Boadway & Keen (2026) — "A Global Minimum Wealth Tax: Issues of Economics and Implementation"
**Intertax, 54(1), 49–54**


### **Introduction and Context (Page 49)**

This note responds to the proposal by Gabriel Zucman (2024, 2025) for a global minimum wealth tax, sympathy for the basic objective of ensuring that ultra-high net worth individuals (UHNWs) pay significantly more tax than they currently do, objective  rationalised by both equity concerns and the need to curb the political influence associated with massive wealth.

The core of Zucman’s proposal is a hybrid scheme: it ensures that UHNWs (specifically those with wealth over USD 1 billion) pay income tax, wealth tax, and equivalent charges totalling at least 2% of their wealth. While accepting the premise that effective tax rates for the ultra-rich are often too low, Boadway and Keen question whether this specific hybrid mechanism is the best or most practical solution. They argue that other paths, such as taxing capital gains on accrual and enhancing inheritance taxation, might achieve similar ends without the anomalies of the proposed hybrid system.

### **International Coordination (Pages 52-53)**

The proposal relies on international coordination to prevent tax competition and capital flight, drawing an analogy to Pillar Two of the OECD/G20 project. However, the authors argue this analogy is imperfect.

1. **Jurisdictional vs. Worldwide:** Pillar Two applies on a jurisdictional basis (topping up tax in each specific country), whereas Zucman’s proposal applies to the *worldwide* wealth of the individual. This makes it closer to the US "GILTI" tax than Pillar Two.
2. **Information Requirements:** A worldwide minimum tax is harder to implement than a jurisdictional one. Topping up taxes requires information on taxes paid by the UHNW in *every* other country to ensure the global minimum is not exceeded. This data is not readily available.
3. **The "UTPR" Analogue:** A key enforcement mechanism in Pillar Two is the Undertaxed Profits Rule (UTPR), allowing countries to tax local assets of a parent entity if the parent is undertaxed elsewhere. An analogue for the wealth tax would allow a country to tax the assets of a non-resident UHNW if they are not subject to the minimum tax in their home country.
* However, the informational requirements are daunting (knowing global tax payments), and the extra-territoriality of such a measure is extremely contentious.
* Without a strong enforcement mechanism like the UTPR, countries have little incentive to adopt the tax, and UHNWs may simply move to non-adopting jurisdictions.

The authors also note that while Pillar Two was driven by a commitment from major capital exporters, a wealth tax requires commitment from countries where UHNWs reside. Currently, the G20 shows little urgency to pursue this.

### **Conclusion (Pages 53-54)**

The authors conclude that the prospects for near-term implementation of the Global Minimum Wealth Tax are bleak. While the hybrid idea is ingenious, it faces significant conceptual and practical hurdles regarding tax equivalence and international coordination.

They recommend alternative paths that may be more effective and politically feasible:

* **Fixing Capital Income Tax:** Addressing the valuation and deferral problems within the existing income tax structure, specifically by taxing capital gains on accrual.
* **Inheritance and Gift Taxes:** Combining income tax reforms with strong, progressive inheritance taxes to address inter-generational inequality.
* **Leveraging Information Exchange:** Building on the existing infrastructure for automatic information exchange to tax capital income more effectively, potentially using a jurisdictional top-up approach similar to Pillar Two rather than a worldwide wealth tax.
