## p. 1 — Abstract & Introduction

### Core research question
How do MNCs adjust their **debt** and **real operations** across subsidiaries in response to anti-tax avoidance policies? Specifically, does a "worldwide approach" to interest deductibility limitation successfully curb debt shifting, and what are the broader real economic consequences?

### Policy context: The UK Worldwide Debt Cap (WDC)
- Introduced by HMRC in January 2010 — the UK was the **first country** to implement a worldwide anti-avoidance approach of this kind.
- The WDC set a maximum ratio of UK net debt relative to the MNC's worldwide gross debt (the "gateway ratio"). If UK net debt exceeded **75%** of worldwide gross debt, the excess interest was **disallowed for tax deduction**.
- Only MNCs that **failed the gateway test** were affected by the WDC — this creates a natural treatment/control split for difference-in-differences (DID) estimation.
- Motivation for the WDC: after moving to a territorial tax system, the UK could no longer tax dividends repatriated by MNCs. Restricting interest deductions was one compensating measure to protect the tax base (fn. 7: Miller (2017) estimates that restrictions on interest relief have been the main mechanism by which UK tax revenues were raised since 2010).

### Main findings (preview)
1. The WDC effectively curbed excessive borrowing in the UK: treated MNCs reduced their gateway ratios by approximately **29%** on average, achieved by both reducing UK net debt and increasing worldwide gross debt.
2. Affected MNCs reallocated debt to **non-UK subsidiaries**, particularly in countries with **higher statutory corporate tax rates**.
3. Affected MNCs reduced UK real operations (total assets –12.2%, fixed assets –17.1%, employment –3.9% over 2010–2014) and expanded non-UK operations.
4. At the group (consolidated) level, the WDC did **not** significantly change total real operations or total tax — it caused **reallocation**, not aggregate reduction.
5. **Foreign MNCs** (headquartered outside the UK) had far more flexibility than domestic MNCs and showed larger responses on both debt and real margins.
6. The WDC succeeded in raising UK tax revenue only from **domestic** MNCs. Foreign MNCs shrank their UK tax base by reducing UK operations, offsetting the revenue gain from lower interest deductions.
7. Foreign countries received more capital and employment but **no increase in tax revenue**, because the real activity gains were offset by increased debt (and thus interest deductions) in those locations.

### Policy relevance mentioned
- After the UK WDC, the **US Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (2017)** introduced similar limits on net interest expense deductions (fn. 3: a concurrent paper, Carrizosa et al. (2020), examines TCJA effects on US firm capital structure).
- In **2019**, the European Commission recommended the Anti-Tax Avoidance Directive (ATAD) with similar interest deductibility limitations (fn. 4).
- The paper's closest relative is **Suárez Serrato (2018)**, which shows that US MNCs shifted investment and employment in response to a tax loophole repeal.

### Literature positioning
- Adds to literature on MNCs' **tax-motivated debt shifting** (Desai et al. 2004; Huizinga et al. 2008; Buettner et al. 2012; Blouin et al. 2014).
- Adds to literature on **real activity reallocation** in response to policy shocks (Giroud & Mueller 2015; Suárez Serrato 2018).
- Bridges the two strands: provides evidence that **anti-tax avoidance policies affect real operations**, not just financial structure.
- Key theoretical point from Desai & Dharmapala (2015) and Dharmapala (2014): the worldwide approach should be **more difficult to circumvent** than thin-capitalisation rules, because circumvention requires manipulating the group-level consolidated debt (not just a single subsidiary's balance sheet).

## p. 2 — Policy Background

### Thin-capitalisation rules (status quo before WDC)
- Standard approach: fix a maximum debt-to-equity or interest coverage ratio per subsidiary; interest on excess debt disallowed.
- These are "stand-alone" rules — they treat each subsidiary separately.
- Limitation: MNCs can easily inject equity into highly leveraged subsidiaries, or use their internal capital market to adjust a single subsidiary's financing. UK's thin-cap rule was "conducted on a case by case basis and hence, is considerably more discretionary" (fn. 8).
- Buettner et al. (2012) provides a summary of thin-capitalisation rules across countries.

### The worldwide approach
- OECD recommendation: supplement thin-cap rules with a worldwide approach benchmarking each country's debt allocation against the MNC's worldwide consolidated debt or EBITDA (see OECD (2015) BEPS 2015 Final Report, Action 4, fn. 6).
- More difficult to circumvent because MNCs would need to manipulate group-level consolidated figures rather than a single subsidiary.
- The WDC was explicitly implemented to complement existing thin-cap rules.

### WDC: key mechanics
- Applicable from **1 January 2010** to **1 April 2017**.
- Applies to "qualifying MNCs" with a UK corporate tax residence (UK company or UK permanent establishment of a non-UK company, fn. 9): more than 250 employees, above €50m turnover, and/or above €43m balance sheet total assets.
- **UK net debt** = relevant liabilities minus relevant assets, aggregated across all UK subsidiaries that are 75%+ owned. Relevant liabilities include short-term loans, overdrafts, and long-term debt. Trade credit and preference shares are excluded. Relevant assets include cash, lending, government/company securities, and financial lease investments.
- **Worldwide gross debt** = consolidated external liabilities of the worldwide group (internal debt excluded from denominator, fn. 10).
- **Gateway ratio** = UK net debt / worldwide gross debt. If >75%, interest deductions on the excess are disallowed.
- Gateway ratio uses **two-year averages** of the underlying variables (so the 2010 ratio uses 2009 data as well).

### Modification in 2017
- On **1 April 2017**, the UK replaced the worldwide gross debt denominator with **EBITDA** (to address the concern that the original WDC incentivised MNCs to increase worldwide gross debt, thereby raising default risk). The paper confirms this concern was valid.

### Concurrent UK tax changes
Two other reforms occurred during the same period and are potential confounders:
1. **Territorial tax system reform (2009):** UK began exempting dividend repatriation from MNCs. Led to more repatriation, higher payouts, and profit shifting to low-tax countries (Egger et al. 2015; Langenmayr & Liu 2020). This applies to all UK companies, not just the treated group.
2. **Corporate tax rate reduction:** From 28% (2010) to 20% (2015). Also applies to all UK companies. Discussed as a robustness concern in Section 5.

## p. 3 — Hypothesis Development

### Debt reallocation
- With full interest deductibility, cost of debt = $i(1-\tau)$, where $i$ = nominal interest rate, $\tau$ = statutory CIT rate.
- Pre-WDC, the implied after-tax cost of debt in the UK was approximately **1.89%** (based on average UK interest rate 2008–2014 of 2.58% and average CIT rate of 26.8%), below the OECD average of **2.08%** (OECD average rate 2.74%, average CIT 24%). This made the UK an attractive location to hold debt.
- After the WDC, for treated MNCs the net-of-tax UK interest rate rises to $i$ (no tax relief on excess), substantially higher than countries without such restrictions.
- Prediction: affected MNCs should reduce UK borrowing and, if relocation is cheap, shift debt to high-tax jurisdictions abroad (where the tax saving on interest is largest).

### Real activity reallocation
- The WDC increases the **user cost of capital** in the UK for treated MNCs, because marginal investment must now be financed with non-deductible debt or equity rather than tax-deductible debt.
- Simultaneously, the user cost of capital in non-UK subsidiaries **decreases**, because they can now attract more cheap (tax-deductible) debt via debt shifting.
- This relative change should cause capital to flow from UK to non-UK subsidiaries until marginal returns equal marginal costs in both locations.
- If **labour is complementary to capital**, employment should follow capital.
- Reallocation should be **more prominent in high-tax non-UK subsidiaries**: the high CIT rate may reduce attractiveness, but this second-order effect is dampened if investment is mainly debt-financed (Buettner et al. 2012) and if profit shifting reduces the income-shifting-adjusted user cost of equity (Grubert & Slemrod 1998; Mintz & Smart 2004; Suárez Serrato 2018).

## pp. 3–5 — Data and Empirical Strategy

### Data sources
Three databases are combined:

1. **Osiris (Bureau van Dijk):** Consolidated financial data for MNC groups. Used to extract MNCs with UK operations (requiring 75%+ ownership of at least one UK subsidiary in 2010). Financial services firms and firms below €43m total assets excluded. Annual vintages 2005–2014 used for subsidiary ownership tracking.

2. **FAME:** Unconsolidated financial data for UK subsidiaries. Allows construction of UK net debt following HMRC definition. Coverage starts in **2008**, which limits pre-reform trend analysis to two years (2008–2009). Used for UK-level analyses.

3. **Orbis (Bureau van Dijk):** Financial data for non-UK subsidiaries, covering 2005–2014. Used for subsidiary-level analyses of non-UK operations. Note: Orbis does not allow replication of the exact HMRC gateway ratio (hence FAME used for gateway ratio calculations).

### Sample construction
- Subsidiaries must be at least **50%+ owned** (effectively controlled) to be included, as these are more likely used for debt shifting.
- Ownership structures tracked year-by-year across 2005–2014 vintages.
- The **2010 gateway ratio** is calculated using two-year averages (2009–2010 data), following HMRC guidance.
- **Treatment group:** 197 MNCs that failed the gateway test in 2010 (148 UK-headquartered, rest foreign-headquartered).
- **Control group:** MNCs that never failed the gateway test.
- **Propensity score matching** applied (one-to-one nearest neighbour, without replacement) on GUO industry, GUO location, and group size (log total assets). After matching: **188 treated and 188 control MNCs.**
- 1,176 unique UK subsidiaries and 668 non-UK subsidiaries (with financial data in Orbis) observed for treated MNCs. Almost 50% of non-UK affiliates are located in European countries (France, Germany, Belgium, Italy, Spain, Norway, Sweden).

### Matching balance (Table 1, measured in 2009)
- Before matching, treated MNCs were smaller, less profitable, had more UK net debt, and much higher gateway ratios than controls.
- After matching, differences in group size, gross debt, and group-level ETR are substantially reduced. UK-level variables (assets, employment, profitability, ETR) also become more comparable.
- Key pre-match characteristic: treated MNCs held **21% of total assets** and **17% of total employees** among all MNCs in the sample, suggesting the WDC would have economically significant effects on the UK economy.
- Average UK net debt of treated MNCs: $13.8 million total assets, 4,886 employees. Average worldwide gross debt: approximately $510 million (matched sample) vs. $900 million for controls.
- Average internal debt share in UK assets: approximately **61%** for a sub-sample of treated firms with available data, confirming active use of internal capital markets.
- Average non-UK CIT rate for treated MNCs: 26.0%; for control group: 25.4% (not statistically different, Table B.1 in Appendix).

### Empirical strategy: DID
**Group-level specification (Eq. 1):**

$$Y^{UK}_{i,t} = \alpha + \beta \cdot \text{Failed}_i \cdot \text{Post}_t + \gamma_t + \omega_i + \varepsilon_{i,t}$$

- $\text{Failed}_i$ = 1 if MNC $i$ failed the gateway test in 2010.
- $\text{Post}_t$ = 1 from 2010 onwards.
- $\gamma_t$ = year fixed effects; $\omega_i$ = MNC group fixed effects.
- Standard errors clustered at MNC level.
- $\beta$ is the coefficient of interest.
- Outcome variables include: gateway ratio (level and log), UK net debt (log), worldwide gross debt (log), UK total assets (log), UK fixed assets (log), UK employment (log), UK tax paid (log).

**Subsidiary-level specification for non-UK operations (Eq. 2):**

$$Y^{nonUK}_{i,j,s,t} = \alpha + \beta \cdot \text{Failed}_i \cdot \text{Post}_t + \delta \cdot X'_{i,j,s,t} + \gamma_t + \lambda_j + \varepsilon_{i,j,s,t}$$

- $\lambda_j$ = subsidiary fixed effects; $X'_{i,j,s,t}$ = subsidiary and group-level controls.
- Some specifications also include country-specific year fixed effects to control for country-level business cycle effects.
- Standard errors clustered at subsidiary level.
- For debt-shifting analysis: leverage ratio as outcome; interaction $\text{Failed}_i \cdot \text{Post}_t \cdot CIT_{jst}$ added to test tax sensitivity.

## pp. 4–6 — Which MNCs failed the gateway test & Distribution of gateway ratios

### Characteristics of the treated group
- The 188 matched treated MNCs constitute approximately **12% of all MNCs in the UK** and own **13% of all UK subsidiaries** in 2009.
- Industry composition: 121 service firms, 37 manufacturing, 18 wholesale, remainder miscellaneous. Most represented: **drug manufacturing** (16 groups) and **computer programming/data processing** (15 groups).
- Geographical clustering: majority of subsidiaries located in **London** (135 subsidiaries), with smaller clusters in Wolverhampton (32), Leeds (12), Aberdeen (11), Maidenhead (11), and Manchester (10).

### Distribution of gateway ratios over time (Fig. 1)
- Panels A–D show treated MNCs' gateway ratio distribution gradually shifted left (towards lower ratios) after 2010.
- By 2014, **62% of treated MNCs** had reduced their gateway ratios below the 75% threshold.
- Control group distribution remained largely unchanged (Panel E).
- Panel F (2014): treated MNCs still had substantially higher gateway ratios than controls, confirming that full compliance was not universal.
- Reasons for non-adjustment: (a) debt contracts may take time to adjust; (b) firms may borrow for genuine financial (not just tax) reasons and thus accept the higher cost.
- Some treated MNCs reduced their gateway ratios **far below** 75% (likely due to adjustment costs — firms overshoot to create buffer, fn. 18: Table B.3 shows firms with lower adjustment costs tend to land closer to threshold).
- MNCs that reduced more: those that were larger, faced higher average non-UK CIT rates, or were headquartered outside the UK.
- Domestic MNCs that did **not** adjust below 75% were more likely borrowing to finance real operations (not purely for tax minimisation).

### Historical context
- Before the WDC, the UK had a relatively high CIT rate **combined with generous interest deductibility** — the combination attracted debt location to the UK. France and Germany had higher CIT rates but stricter anti-debt shifting rules (Blouin et al. 2014). The WDC disrupted this combination.

## p. 7 — Cost of Capital Analysis

### User cost of capital framework
Following **Devereux and Griffith (2003)**, the tax component of the user cost of capital is calculated for three asset types: machinery, buildings, and intangibles. Parameters (present value of capital allowances $A$, statutory CIT rate $\tau$, tax depreciation $\varphi$, economic depreciation $\delta$, and interest rate $r$) are sourced from the Oxford University Centre for Business Taxation Tax Database and Bank of England data (fn. 19, 20).

Two financing assumptions:
- Under **tax-deductible debt** (pre-WDC for treated firms): $TaxD = \frac{(1-A)}{(1-\tau)}(r+\delta) - \frac{r\tau(1-\varphi\tau)}{(1-\tau)}$
- Under **non-deductible sources** (equity or non-deductible debt, post-WDC): $TaxE = \frac{(1-A)}{(1-\tau)}(r+\delta)$

Weighted average across asset types: 41.3% buildings, 44.2% machinery, 14.5% intangibles (fn. 33).

**Key result (Table A.2):** In fiscal year 2011, the WDC increased the tax component of the user cost of capital for treated MNCs from **0.166** (deductible debt) to **0.186** (non-deductible financing), a **11.1% increase**. The average over 2011–2014 implies a 10.0% change; comparing 2010 debt to 2014 equity gives 13.0%.

**Dynamic UCC (Fig. 2):** Using firm-specific weighted UCC ($UCC_{i,t} = (1-h_{i,t}) \cdot UCC_{equity} + h_{i,t} \cdot UCC_{debt}$, where $h_{i,t}$ = ratio of deductible debt to total assets), treated MNCs experienced a **6.5% increase** in weighted UCC post-WDC relative to controls.

## pp. 7–9 — Group-Level Evidence: Debt

### Gateway ratio and debt (Table 2)
All results from DID on matched sample (376 groups, 2,055 observations):

- **Gateway ratio (level):** Treated MNCs reduced gateway ratio by **–1.691** (p<0.01). A median treated MNC had a gateway ratio of 2.19 in 2010, so needed to reduce by 1.44 to reach 75%.
- **Gateway ratio (log):** Coefficient –0.345, implying a **–29% reduction** ($e^{-0.345} - 1$).
- **UK net debt (log):** Coefficient –1.008, implying a **–64% reduction** ($e^{-1.008} - 1$). For a median firm with UK net debt of £12,060.5k and gross debt of £5,513k, cutting to the gateway threshold requires reducing log UK net debt by approximately 1.07 (fn. 22).
- **Worldwide gross debt (log):** Coefficient +0.327, implying a **+39% increase** ($e^{0.327} - 1$).

The reduction in UK net debt was mainly achieved by **lowering relevant liabilities** rather than increasing relevant assets (Table B.2, columns 1–2). The increase in worldwide gross debt was mainly achieved by **raising private borrowing** (bank loans), not public debt (Table B.2, columns 3–5). This lends support to the concern that the original WDC (pre-2017) could encourage MNCs to increase external borrowing and thereby exacerbate default risk — which is why the 2017 reform switched the denominator to EBITDA.

### Heterogeneous responses by gateway ratio (Fig. 3 / Table 7.1 in Online Appendix)
Triple DID specification: interact $\text{Failed}_i \cdot \text{Post}_t$ with a dummy for being above various gateway threshold percentages. MNCs further above the 75% threshold responded more strongly to the WDC. This validates DID as the preferred method over RDD for this setting.

### Domestic vs. foreign MNCs (Table 3)
**Domestic MNCs** (Panel A, 278 groups):
- UK net debt: **–59%**
- Worldwide gross debt: **+25%** (not statistically significant)

**Foreign MNCs** (Panel B, 98 groups):
- UK net debt: **–78%**
- Worldwide gross debt: **+97%**

Differences between domestic and foreign are statistically significant (p=0.000). Foreign MNCs have more options for debt reallocation given weaker home bias.

### Dynamic effects (Fig. 4)
Parallel trends assumption holds for 2008 and 2009 (no statistically significant pre-trends). Little adjustment visible within 2010 (short adjustment window). From **2011** onwards, treated MNCs reduced gateway ratios significantly relative to controls. Reduction is gradual and continues through 2014. The pattern is driven purely by treated firms — control group firms show no significant adjustment. Figs. 5a–5d show domestic and foreign MNC dynamics separately, confirming far greater adjustment by foreign MNCs.

### Robustness concerns
Contemporaneous events examined as potential confounders (discussed in Section 5 and Online Appendix Section 1):
- 2008 financial crisis
- 2009 territorial tax reform
- UK CIT rate cuts

The DID design partially addresses these because they affect all UK MNCs. Triple DID specifications show no differential treatment effect for MNCs more exposed to these alternative events. Group-level results are therefore "unlikely to be contaminated."

Using Eq. 2 with net-of-cash leverage ratio of non-UK subsidiaries as outcome (17,205–17,266 observations across 2,416–2,421 subsidiary groups):

## pp. 10–12 — Subsidiary-Level Evidence: Real Activity Reallocation

### UK operations (Table 6)
All outcomes in logs, controlling for group size:

**All MNCs (Columns 1–3):**
- Total assets: **–12.2%** (p<0.01)
- Fixed assets: **–17.1%** (p<0.01)
- Employment: **–3.9%** (n.s. at conventional levels)

**Domestic MNCs (Columns 4–6):** All coefficients negative but small and statistically insignificant. Consistent with stronger home bias.

**Foreign MNCs (Columns 7–9):**
- Total assets: **–30.6%** (p<0.05)
- Fixed assets: **–32.1%** (p<0.05)
- Employment: **–11.3%** (p<0.10)

Dynamic evolution (Fig. 6): parallel pre-trends hold. Divergence begins in **2011** for assets and fixed assets. Employment adjustment comes with a slight lag — only significant from **2012** (attributed to higher adjustment costs for labour). Timing is consistent with debt adjustment pattern.

### Implied user cost elasticities (Table A.3)
Assuming treated MNCs switched from deductible debt to equity financing:

- **Fixed assets elasticity (all MNCs):** 1.53 (= 17.1% / 11.1%). This is **above** the survey range of 0.5–1.0 (Hassett & Hubbard 2002) and above Bond & Xing (2015) who find 0.4 for total capital and 0.7 for equipment.
- **Domestic MNCs:** Fixed assets elasticity ≈ 0.36 (not statistically significant). Within range of existing literature.
- **Foreign MNCs:** Fixed assets elasticity ≈ 2.88 (p<0.05). Substantially above existing estimates.
- **Employment elasticity (all MNCs):** 0.35 (= 3.9% / 11.1%).
- Implied semi-elasticity of employment with respect to ETR: approximately **2.2** (fn. 30: derived by converting a 11.9% tax increase using an ETR of 15%, giving 1.785 pp change, and dividing 3.9% employment reduction by this). Suárez Serrato (2018) finds 1.2–1.44 for US data.
- Using the weighted UCC (which incorporates firm-specific deductible debt ratios), elasticities are larger but qualitative conclusions remain: domestic MNCs' elasticities are comparable to literature, foreign MNCs' are higher.
- Note (fn. 29): Neoclassical investment theory (Jorgenson 1963; Hall & Jorgenson 1967) predicts an elasticity of –1 under Cobb-Douglas production technology.

### Non-UK operations (Table 7)

**Panel A — Average effects across all non-UK subsidiaries (17,205 obs., 2,416 groups):**

*All MNCs:*
- Total assets: **+15.8%** (p<0.01)
- Fixed assets: **+12.5%** (p<0.05)
- Employment: **+10.9%** (p<0.01)

*Domestic MNCs:* Positive effects for all three; only total assets is statistically significant (possibly driven by increase in foreign debt rather than real investment).

*Foreign MNCs:* Positive and statistically significant for all three (total assets +24.6%, fixed assets +23.9%, employment +19.8%).

**Panel B — Interaction with host country CIT rate:**
- All MNCs: Interaction $\text{Failed}_i \cdot \text{Post}_t \times CIT_{st}$ positive and significant for total assets (+0.381, p<0.05), fixed assets (+0.410, p<0.05), employment (+0.285, p<0.10).
- Mainly driven by foreign MNCs: all three interactions significant (total assets +0.779, fixed assets +0.797, employment +0.582).
- Confirms that reallocation of real business activities followed the **same direction** as debt reallocation (both towards high-tax countries).

Dynamic evolution (Figs. 7b–7d): no pre-reform differences; non-UK operations increase from 2011 onwards for treated MNCs while control group remains stable.

### Organisational changes (Online Appendix Section 2)
Affected MNCs:
- Reduced the percentage of **relevant UK subsidiaries** included in the UK net debt calculation.
- Increased the percentage of subsidiaries in **higher-tax countries** than the UK.
- Decreased the percentage of subsidiaries in **lower-tax countries** than the UK.
These are extensive margin adjustments (subsidiary-level restructuring) on top of intensive margin adjustments in assets, fixed assets, and employment.

## pp. 13–14 — Tax Burden: UK and Non-UK

### UK tax payments (Table 8, Panel A)
- **All MNCs:** UK tax +11.9% (n.s.)
- **Domestic MNCs:** UK tax **+25.7%** (p<0.10). Domestic MNCs paid more UK tax because they did not significantly reduce UK operations.
- **Foreign MNCs:** UK tax +22.5% (n.s.). Despite lower UK interest deductions, foreign MNCs shrank their UK operations, offsetting the expected revenue gain. "The WDC failed to collect more tax revenue from them even with a lower level of UK net debt."

The paper notes that UK corporate tax revenues increased from GBP 30.8 billion (2009/10) to GBP 35.3 billion (2010/11) excluding North Sea oil (fn. 32). Bilicka (2019) provides evidence of increasing net tax payments by MNCs in this period.

### Non-UK tax payments (Table 8, Panel B)
- For all MNC types, the estimated effect of the WDC on foreign subsidiary tax payments is **positive but not statistically significant** (all MNCs: +10.8%; domestic: +4.8%; foreign: +14.0%).
- Interpretation: the positive effect of increased real operations on the non-UK tax base was **offset by the negative effect of more debt** in those jurisdictions. Foreign countries "benefit from UK's WDC by receiving more capital and employees" but "did not enjoy any increase in tax revenue."

## p. 15 — Conclusions

### Summary of main findings
1. WDC effectively curbed excessive UK borrowing: gateway ratios fell by 29% on average.
2. **Unintended consequence:** treated MNCs increased their **worldwide external debt**, potentially raising default risk. This is the economic rationale for the 2017 reform shifting from a worldwide debt benchmark to an EBITDA benchmark.
3. WDC led to real reallocation (not aggregate reduction): UK operations shrank while non-UK operations expanded, particularly for foreign MNCs with weak home bias.
4. WDC raised UK tax revenue from **domestic MNCs only**; foreign MNCs offset lower interest deductions by reducing their UK tax base.
5. Foreign countries received more capital and employment but **not more tax revenue** (gains offset by debt-shifting).

### Policy implications
- **International cooperation** is necessary to prevent unintended consequences from unilateral anti-avoidance measures.
- A unilateral worldwide approach may create competitive disadvantages for **domestic MNCs** relative to foreign MNCs operating in the same jurisdiction (domestic firms could not as easily relocate operations).
- The paper notes this as a significant limitation of country-level anti-avoidance policy: "such unilateral moves may have unintended consequences and significant spillover effects."

## Appendix A — User Cost of Capital (pp. 15–16)

Formulas for the tax component:

Non-deductible financing ("equity"): $TaxE = \frac{(1-A)}{(1-\tau)}(r+\delta)$

Tax-deductible debt: $TaxD = \frac{(1-A)}{(1-\tau)}(r+\delta) - \frac{r\tau(1-\varphi\tau)}{(1-\tau)}$

Assumptions following Devereux & Griffith (2003): inflation rate = 0; shareholder discount rate = real interest rate = nominal interest rate (fn.: these are strong assumptions but standard in the literature).

Table A.1 provides parameters (A, $\tau$, $\varphi$, r, $\delta$) for machinery, buildings, and intangibles, for each year 2008–2014. Interest rate sourced from Bank of England (weighted average sterling rate on other loans with initial fixation >5 years to private non-financial corporations, not seasonally adjusted).

Key calculated values (Table A.2):
- Fiscal year 2011: equity UCC = 0.186; debt UCC = 0.166; **change = 11.1%**
- Average 2011–2014: equity = 0.188; debt = 0.170; change = 10.0%
- 2010 debt vs. 2014 equity: change = 13.0%

## Appendix B — Additional Results (pp. 17–18)

### Table B.1 — Summary statistics for matched sample (2009)
- Treated domestic MNCs hold the vast majority of UK assets and employment (£2,482m vs. £454m for controls; 527,500 vs. 277,035 employees).
- Treated MNCs held on average **74% of their total group assets in the UK**; controls held 53%.
- Non-UK leverage of treated MNCs is lower than controls pre-reform (0.138 vs. 0.233), consistent with treated firms using UK (not non-UK) subsidiaries for debt holding.

### Table B.2 — Debt decomposition
- UK net debt reduction achieved mainly via **lower relevant liabilities** (coefficient –0.199, p<0.05), not increasing relevant assets (coefficient +0.038, n.s.).
- Increase in worldwide gross debt is primarily **private debt** (bank loans), not public/traded debt. (Smaller sample of 179 groups for this decomposition.)

### Table B.3 — Distance to gateway threshold
- "Distance" = absolute deviation of gateway ratio from 75%.
- Treated MNCs with smaller adjustment costs (lower share of private debt, higher share of financial subsidiaries) tend to reduce their distance to the threshold more (landing closer to 75%).
- Proxies for adjustment costs: share of private debt and the dummy for above-median fraction of financial subsidiaries in group structure.

---

## Key Methodological Notes for Thesis Use

- The **gateway test** creates a clean treatment/control split, making DID credible. The threshold is binding, observable, and exogenous to contemporaneous events.
- The **propensity score matching** on industry, HQ location, and group size further improves comparability, though UK net debt and gateway ratio remain higher in the treatment group after matching (as expected given treatment assignment).
- **Parallel trends** are supported for 2008–2009 across all main outcomes.
- **Fiscal year timing:** most UK firms' fiscal year 2010 ended in April 2010, leaving little time to adjust within 2010. This is why 2010 is set as the reference year, with meaningful changes visible from **2011** onwards.
- Data limitation: investment is not directly observable in FAME or Orbis — fixed assets (net of depreciation and disposal) are used as a proxy. This introduces some measurement error.
- The paper cannot distinguish **internal from external debt** for most UK subsidiaries, but for a sub-sample, internal debt accounts for about 61% of UK subsidiary assets.

## Connections to Global Minimum Tax (GMT / Pillar Two)

- The finding that **anti-avoidance measures can cause real activity reallocation** is directly relevant to evaluating Pillar Two: if MNCs respond to a minimum effective tax rate by relocating real activity (not just financial flows), the revenue gains may be partially offset.
- The **SBIE (Substance-based Income Exclusion)** in Pillar Two explicitly excludes a portion of income based on tangible assets and payroll — partly to prevent excessive real activity dislocation. This paper provides empirical grounding for why such carve-outs matter.
- The **heterogeneity between domestic and foreign MNCs** is relevant: under GMT, jurisdictions with many foreign-headquartered MNCs may face greater real activity outflows if minimum tax rules raise the effective cost of capital in that jurisdiction.
- The result that **unilateral anti-avoidance measures create spillover effects** and competitive distortions between domestic and foreign MNCs directly supports the argument for coordinated international implementation (i.e., the multilateral OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework approach underlying Pillar Two).
- The 2017 shift from worldwide debt to **EBITDA benchmark** mirrors the logic in Pillar Two's use of EBITDA in computing the ETR (GloBE income) — avoiding incentives to inflate gross debt.
- The paper's finding that **foreign countries receive more investment but not more tax revenue** (due to simultaneous debt shifting) is a clean illustration of why an international minimum tax floor (preventing competitive debt shifting) would be complementary to unilateral anti-avoidance rules.
