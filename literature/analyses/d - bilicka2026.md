## Page 1 – Abstract and Introduction (start)

### Key highlights (from article)
- Subsidiaries of aggressive MNCs respond more significantly to local business tax (LBT) rate increases
- After an LBT rate increase, LBT revenues decline in municipalities that are more exposed to aggressive MNCs
- Fiscal equalization and political economy factors help explain why municipalities continue raising LBT rates

### Research question
What are the consequences of MNC tax avoidance practices for the tax revenues of subnational governments?

### Setting
- Germany, 2008–2019
- Over 11,000 municipalities with variation in LBT (Gewerbesteuer) multipliers
- Firm-level financial data from Orbis, combined with German municipal tax revenue data

### Definition of aggressive MNCs
Defined as MNCs with at least one affiliate located in a tax haven in their ownership structure. This follows a large literature: Hines and Rice (1994), Gumpert et al. (2016), Bilicka and Scur (2024), Davies et al. (2018).

### Context of paper in broader literature
- Panama Papers (2016) and Paradise Papers (2017) renewed interest in profit shifting and revenue losses
- Prior literature (Bilicka 2019; Tørsløv et al. 2023; Garcia-Bernardo and Janský 2024; Fuest et al. 2025) documents large national-level revenue losses
- This paper shifts focus to subnational (municipal) government revenues — a gap in the literature

### LBT basics
- Effective LBT rate varies between 7% and 21% during 2008–2019
- Municipalities set their own multiplier, but the tax base is set federally — important for identification (rate changes are cleanly separable from base changes)



## Page 2 – Introduction (continued) and beginning of results overview

### Firm-level findings
- After LBT rate increase, subsidiaries of more aggressive MNCs reduce reported profits and tax liability in Germany relative to other firms
- No change in real activities (proxied by assets and employment) — rules out mobility or real reallocation as driver
- At the consolidated MNC level: effective tax rates decrease when aggressive MNCs face LBT increases — consistent with profit reallocation to low-tax subsidiaries (plausibly tax havens)
- Note (fn. 2): the paper does not consistently observe profits reported in tax havens directly from financial data, so this cannot be verified directly

### Municipal-level identification strategy
- Triple difference-in-differences (DDD): comparing municipalities that raised LBT rates vs. those that did not, interacted with whether municipalities are more or less exposed to aggressive MNCs
- Exposure defined as share of subsidiaries belonging to aggressive MNCs relative to total firm count in each municipality
- Note (fn. 3): 94% of all LBT rate changes at municipal level during the sample period were increases (not decreases)
- Note (fn. 4): authors also use count and shares of assets, turnover, and employment as alternative exposure measures

### Municipal-level findings
- Following LBT rate increase, LBT revenues decline in municipalities more exposed to aggressive MNCs relative to less exposed municipalities
- Both more and less exposed municipalities show similar pre-reform revenue trends (parallel trends), but diverge post-reform
- Robust to: modified DID estimator by De Chaisemartin and d'Haultfoeuille (2020), multiple observable controls, staggered treatment correction

### IV approach
- Instrument: dummy for municipal majority coalition seat swing from left to right (or vice versa) following a close election (vote margin within 100 voters)
- Note (fn. 5): left-wing coalition = SPD and Greens; right-wing = CDU/CSU and FDP (following Foremny and Riedel 2014)
- IV result: LBT revenues in more exposed municipalities decline even in absolute terms (not just relative), suggesting these municipalities are on the downward-sloping portion of the Laffer curve

### Why do municipalities keep raising LBT rates despite revenue losses?
Three explanations explored:
1. Fiscal equalization system (Finanzausgleich): transfers compensate municipalities for revenue losses, reducing incentive to stop raising rates. Empirically, transfers to more exposed municipalities increase after LBT rate increase; when transfers are added to LBT revenues, the decline disappears. Also: since profits are shifted out of Germany entirely (not just across municipalities), this also puts stress on federal CIT revenues.
2. Political economy: LBT viewed as more voter-friendly than property tax. Business owners (especially foreign MNC owners) represent fewer votes than homeowners. Revenue losses from LBT only occur when municipalities increase just LBT rates, not when they increase both LBT and property taxes simultaneously.
3. Tax policy uncertainty: municipalities that raise LBT rates multiple times within the sample experience even larger absolute LBT revenue declines. Evidence from Devereux (2022) survey of large MNCs: frequent statutory rate changes are a key driver of tax uncertainty in high-income countries.

### Contribution to literature
- Extends US literature (Suárez Serrato and Zidar 2018; Fajgelbaum et al. 2019) on state-level tax rates and bases to subnational setting with explicit profit-shifting mechanism
- Unlike US, German municipalities do not control the tax base — allows clean isolation of rate effect from base effect
- Relates to Canada (Mintz and Smart 2004): income-shifting firms show higher elasticities, but that paper looks at cross-provincial shifting, not international
- Relates to Germany-specific papers: Fossen and Steiner (2018), Buettner (2003), Dwenger and Steiner (2012) — these estimate LBT base elasticities of −0.3 to −1.5 but cannot identify which firms drive this
- Note (fn. 6): Dwenger and Steiner (2012) show larger firms, manufacturing firms, and those with higher FDI/equity ratios are more responsive, but do not observe MNC tax haven membership



## Page 3 – Contribution and German institutional context

### Own contribution relative to earlier German literature (continued)
Three advances over prior German work:
1. Provides empirical evidence on the mechanism behind negative LBT base elasticities
2. Uncovers heterogeneity across municipalities in how tax revenues respond to LBT rate changes
3. Updates old elasticity estimates using post-2008 data (after a major federal CIT rate cut in 2008)

### Own estimates
- IV firm-level: tax base elasticity of −0.51 on average; −1.45 for aggressive MNC subsidiaries; −0.3 for other firms
- Municipal-level average tax base elasticity: −4.8 (substantially larger than prior literature)
- Marginal value of public funds (MVPF) for high-exposure municipalities: −0.11 (using Hendren and Sprung-Keyser 2020 framework) — large behavioural response more than offsets mechanical revenue gain

### Contribution to profit-shifting literature
Builds on: Grubert and Mutti (1991), Hines and Rice (1994), Huizinga and Laeven (2008), Weichenrieder (2009), Dharmapala and Riedel (2013), Dowd et al. (2017), Wier and Erasmus (2022)
- Recent aggregate work: Tørsløv et al. (2023) and Garcia-Bernardo and Janský (2024) estimate revenue losses in developed and developing countries; Garcia-Bernardo et al. (2022) extends to personal and capital gains tax losses
- Bilicka (2019): MNC vs. domestic firm profit disparity in UK using micro data; Fuest et al. (2025): similar for Germany using CbCR data
- Real consequences of profit shifting: Bilicka et al. (2022), Suárez Serrato (2018), Becker and Riedel (2012), Egger and Wamser (2015)
- Role of local agents: Bustos et al. (2022), Bilicka and Scur (2024)
- Macro consequences: Guvenen et al. (2022) — profit shifting reduces US GDP and productivity estimates; Coppola et al. (2021) — offshore issuance reduces portfolio investment estimates
- None of these study subnational revenues — this paper fills that gap

### German fiscal structure
- Three levels: federal, state (Länder), municipal
- Federal: exclusive power over customs and fiscal monopolies
- Income tax and CIT revenues: shared between federal and state
- 75% of VAT redistributed across states
- Municipalities collect: LBT and property tax, plus a share of income tax and CIT from federal transfers
- Share of total tax revenue: 15% municipal, 43% state, 39% federal, 4% EU (European Committee of the Regions, 2022)
- Municipal revenue breakdown: 50% LBT, 11% property tax, rest from fiscal transfers (Finanzausgleich)
- Fiscal equalization based on: fiscal capacity (Finanzkraft, ability to raise own revenues) and fiscal need (Finanzbedarf, per-capita budget per resident)
- Municipalities receive: share of wage and assessed income tax and final withholding tax (€41bn in 2022, 33%) and share of VAT (€7.5bn, 6%) from federal government
- In return, municipalities apportion ~10% of their LBT revenue back to state and federal (€5.8bn out of €63.5bn total LBT revenue in 2022)

### LBT mechanics
- LBT (Gewerbesteuer) = tax on companies' profits
- Tax rate formula: t_i = m_i × f, where f = base rate of 3.5% (uniform nationally) and m_i = municipal multiplier (Hebesatz)
- Municipalities vote annually on next year's multiplier; once changed, valid for at least one full budget year
- No ceiling on multiplier; floor of 200 introduced in 2004 (before sample starts)
- In 2019: effective LBT rate ranged from 7% (m=200) to 21% (m=600), average 12.3%
- Municipalities control multiplier but have no control over deductions or tax base (base set federally)
- Tax base = operating profits earned within municipal boundaries; slightly different from federal CIT base
- For multi-establishment firms: taxable profits apportioned across municipalities according to each municipality's wage bill share (formula apportionment)
- Federal CIT on corporate profits: uniform 15.825% (including solidarity surcharge)

### Data: municipal level
- German Office of Statistics: data for all 11,000 municipalities, 2008–2019
- Variables: total tax revenue, LBT revenue, property tax revenue, and transfers
- Main outcome variables: log(LBT revenues), log(total tax revenues), share of LBT revenues in total tax revenues

### Data: firm level
- Source: Orbis dataset
- Coverage: over 400,000 firms per year, 2008–2019 = 34–43% of all firms registered to pay CIT in Germany (see Table B2 in appendix for detail)
- Information available: address, postcode, city (as of 2019)
- Firm addresses matched to municipality using GIS software: 97% match rate
- Note (fn. 8): 98% of unmatched firms are domestic standalones with no ownership or financial info in Orbis
- Ownership data from Orbis as of 2019 (static): used to classify firms as domestic standalones, domestic groups, foreign multinationals, domestic multinationals
- Note (fn. 9): historical ownership data not used because coverage is limited and biased towards larger MNCs; assumption is that ownership did not change during 2008–2019 (acknowledged as a limitation, but validated in Appendix C using historical data showing no changes in likelihood of using new tax haven subsidiaries)



## Page 4 – Data construction and empirical strategy

### Classification of MNCs
- Foreign multinationals: headquarters outside Germany
- Domestic multinationals: headquartered in Germany but with at least one foreign affiliate owned >50% — ensures focus on directly controlled subsidiaries, more likely used for profit shifting
- Coverage of controlled foreign companies (CFCs): German Statistical Office reports 36,187 CFCs employing 3.5 million people in Germany (2017); Orbis covers over 31,000 of those firms, with employment info for 2.1 million people — so Orbis covers >85% of CFCs and ~60% of CFC employees
- Note (fn. 10): Table B3 in appendix for detailed statistics; financial info coverage is limited
- For each MNC operating in Germany: entire global subsidiary network downloaded; aggressive MNCs = those with at least one affiliate in a tax haven (Hines and Rice 1994; Gumpert et al. 2016; Bilicka and Scur 2024)
- Note (fn. 11): Orbis ownership coverage validated against public CbCR disclosures by Dutt et al. (2019); Orbis has poor coverage for financial info in tax havens, but firms do report presence in tax havens — which is all this paper requires; for list of tax haven subsidiaries in Orbis, see Table B5

### Scale of aggressive MNC presence
- Annually: over 30,000 unique affiliates belonging to more aggressive MNCs; ~15,000–20,000 from foreign MNCs; ~10,000–12,000 from domestic MNCs
- Data collected: unconsolidated balance sheet (total assets, fixed assets, employment, profits, turnover); consolidated parent-level financials for limited sample (used in Appendix C)

### Unit of analysis
- Firm-level: 95,000+ unique firms in Germany reporting unconsolidated financials in Orbis in at least one year 2008–2019; ~430,000 observations; annually 40,000–75,000 firms of which 15,000 belong to MNCs and 10,000 to aggressive MNCs
- Municipality-year level: firm-level data collapsed by municipality; municipalities that merged during sample period excluded
- Result: 110,000+ observations across 9,725 municipalities, 2008–2019
- Note (fn. 12): some municipalities missing due to inability to match shapefiles or no Orbis firm observations
- Average municipality: 563 firms in Orbis, 13 domestic MNC affiliates, 14 foreign affiliates; 15 of those belong to aggressive MNCs
- Average share of aggressive MNC subsidiaries in total firm count per municipality: 0.1%, ranging from 0 to 11%+
- High exposure indicator: binary variable based on median share across municipalities (above or below median)
- Note (fn. 13): Orbis does not have info on presence of establishments per reporting subsidiary; formula apportionment requires profit allocation by municipality-level wage bill; Link et al. (2024) argue only 7% of German manufacturing firms operate across multiple municipalities, so bias from this is likely small
- Descriptive statistics: Table B1 in appendix

### Identifying variation
- 12,062 events of municipal LBT rate increases identified during 2008–2019
- Average municipality changed its rate 1.26 times in the sample period
- ~4,000 municipalities: one LBT rate increase; ~2,000: two; ~900: three; ~500: four or more; 2,606 municipalities: no LBT rate change at all
- Variation shown in Figure B2 and Table B1
- Note (fn. 14): only 6% of LBT rate changes in sample are decreases

### Firm-level empirical model (eq. 1)
T_it = α + β1 post_it + β2 (post_it × MNC_i) + β3 (post_it × aggMNC_i) + γ_i + ψ_tj + ε_it

- T_it = ROA (profit before tax / total assets), ETR (tax liability / profit before tax), or log(tax liability)
- post_it = 1 in all years after LBT rate increase in municipality where firm is located (also zero for all control municipalities)
- MNC_i = 1 if subsidiary belongs to an MNC
- aggMNC_i = 1 if that MNC also has a tax haven subsidiary
- γ_i = firm fixed effects; ψ_tj = state × year fixed effects
- Standard errors clustered at municipality level
- Coefficient of interest: β3 = differential response of aggressive MNC subsidiaries relative to non-aggressive MNC subsidiaries



## Page 5 – Empirical strategy continued: municipal-level model and identification

### Municipal-level model (eq. 2) — simple DID
T_it = α + β1 post_it + β2 (post_it × hMNC_i) + η_i + φ_jt + ε_it

- T_it = log(LBT revenues), log(total tax revenues), or share of LBT in total tax revenues
- hMNC_i = 1 when share of aggressive MNC subsidiaries in municipality is above median = "high exposure"
- η_i = municipality fixed effects; φ_jt = state × year fixed effects (controls for state-level variation in equalization rules)
- Standard errors clustered at municipality level
- β1 = effect of LBT rate increase in low-exposure municipalities relative to control (no rate change) municipalities
- β2 = differential response in high-exposure municipalities (coefficient of interest)
- Control group: 2,606 municipalities with no LBT rate change during sample period

### Event study model (eq. 3)
T_it = α + Σ_κ δ_κ D_iκ + Σ_κ β_κ (D_iκ × hMNC_i) + η_i + φ_jt + ε_it

- D_iκ = binned treatment adoption indicator (following Schmidheiny and Siegloch 2023; McCrary 2007)
- κ = −3 to +3 (binned at endpoints, so end dummies include all years beyond window)
- κ = −1 is omitted category (normalisation)
- E_i = year of LBT rate increase in municipality i
- Coefficients of interest: β_κ = differential response in high vs. low exposure municipalities κ years before/after reform, relative to never-treated control group
- Same dummies equal zero for municipalities with no LBT rate changes

### Identifying assumptions
Two assumptions required for causal interpretation:

1. Location of aggressive MNC subsidiaries is not correlated with LBT rate changes or levels. Ownership data is fixed at 2019, so changes in location in response to rate changes are not allowed. Authors note: Becker et al. (2012) show German municipalities use LBT rates to attract foreign MNCs generally, but the required assumption is only that municipalities don't specifically attract more aggressive MNCs.
- Empirical validation: correlations between aggressive MNC shares and (1) LBT rates, (2) number of LBT rate changes, and (3) size of LBT rate changes are all close to zero (see note fn. 15: coefficients of 0.0001, 0.0001, −0.001)

2. Parallel trends: LBT revenues in more and less exposed municipalities would have evolved similarly absent LBT rate increases. Main threat: reverse causality (municipalities raise rates because of revenue changes). Blesse et al. (2019) argue this is unlikely in Germany because municipalities set multiplier for year t before knowing revenues for year t−1. Empirically: no significant pre-trends in event studies.

### Endogeneity concern and IV
LBT rates may be endogenous due to political cycle (Foremny and Riedel 2014; Blesse et al. 2019), firm size (Riedel and Simmler 2021), firm mobility (Langenmayr and Simmler 2021), or agglomeration. Since aggressive MNCs are larger and more mobile, more exposed municipalities may be differentially affected.

### Instrument construction
- Hand-collected data: number of eligible voters, votes per party, municipal council elections 2008–2019
- Note (fn. 16): federal/state/EU election data readily available from German Statistical Office, but no such data found for municipal elections after 2008; earlier papers (Freier and Odendahl 2015; Riedel et al. 2021) compiled dataset up to 2008
- Combined with: proportional representation seat allocation formulas per state and year; mandated number of seats (based on population thresholds)
- Note (fn. 17): Freier and Odendahl (2015) use algorithm for Bavaria pre-2008; formulas vary by state and year
- Left-wing coalition: SPD and Greens; right-wing: CDU/CSU and FDP (note: experiments with Freie Wähler in right block and Linke in left block did not change results — not reported)
- Swing dummy: 1 three years after a close election where majority coalition swings from left to right or right to left; 0 otherwise (both three years before and in municipalities where no such swings occur)
- Close election: vote difference within 100-voters margin at baseline
- Restriction to ±3 years ensures instrument captures variation from specific election, not later political changes. Relaxing this restriction yields qualitatively similar results (Figures A3 and A4)

### IV second stage (eq. 4)
T_it = α + β1 post_hat_it + β2 (post_hat_it × hMNC_i) + η_i + φ_jt + ε_it

- post_hat = predicted value from first stage
- Coefficient of interest: β2 = differential effect of LBT rate change in more vs. less exposed municipalities

### Instrument validity
- First stage results shown in Table A3: all significant
- Kleibergen–Paap LM test p-value = uniformly zero across specifications (instrument is informative)
- Kleibergen–Paap F-stats: well above 100 for firm-level; above 28 for municipal level — well above Stock-Yogo critical values, so null of weak instruments rejected
- Figure A2 shows no strategic manipulation of discontinuity

### Relevance of instrument
Political economics literature: Pettersson-Lidbom (2008) shows left governments tax 2–3% more than right; Fiva et al. (2018) shows more left representation increases property taxes in Norway. German context: Freier and Odendahl (2015) use election outcomes as IV for tax policies in Bavarian municipalities. Other papers using majority coalition swings: Folke (2014), Lee (2008).
- Appendix A.1: seat majority swing towards left = sharp increase in LBT rates; towards right = significant decline in LBT rates

### Exclusion restriction threats
Two potential threats:

1. Political power shifts may also affect property tax rates and municipal expenditures (Table A2 shows discontinuous changes at cutoff). However: no changes in firm real outcomes or entry/exit (Tables A7 and C5), suggesting property tax or spending changes do not affect LBT base through firm behaviour. Over 3-year horizon: enforcement intensity, business location, and property assessment bases are largely predetermined. Note: if political power affects LBT revenues through additional fiscal channels beyond LBT rate, IV estimates should be interpreted as local average effect of political-fiscal adjustment rather than pure LBT rate effect. This is especially relevant when using total tax revenues as outcome.

2. Swing may result from declining LBT revenues (reverse causality at instrument level). But this would have to be systematically related to aggressive MNC exposure specifically. Empirically: no significant pre-trends in LBT revenues, so limited concern.

### LATE interpretation
IV identifies local average treatment effect for politically competitive municipalities whose LBT policies respond to coalition control changes. Close elections are less frequent than all LBT rate changes, so IV is identified off narrower variation than OLS. OLS and IV are complementary: IV addresses endogeneity, OLS reflects average response across full institutional environment.



## Page 6 – Identification continued, and staggered DID

### Additional identification checks
- IV strategy relies on time variation in politically competitive municipalities — could mean estimates only reflect politically competitive places. Controlled by including municipality-specific election-year fixed effects across all IV specifications (absorb time-varying factors within election cycle)
- Potential threat from municipality-level economic trends: baseline includes state × year fixed effects (attenuates concern since German states are economically homogeneous). No significant pre-trends controlling for these FEs rules out concurrent economic shocks.
- Empirically: no changes in total firm-level employment and assets at municipal level before or after LBT rate changes — consistent with Fuest et al. (2018), Lichter et al. (2025), Link et al. (2024) who also show no changes in population or local GDP using same identifying variation
- Rambachan and Roth (2023) pre-trend sensitivity analysis applied to show robustness to potential violations of parallel trends

### Staggered DID (Section 3.4)
Traditional TWFE approach may be contaminated when already-treated observations act as controls (negative weights in regression). Solution: De Chaisemartin and d'Haultfoeuille (2020) modified dynamic DID estimator, using not-yet-treated municipalities as control group only. This also provides robustness check against potential differences between municipalities that ever change rates and those that never do.

### Mechanisms ruling out real operations reallocation
Two possible explanations for lower reported profits after LBT rate increase:
1. Profit shifting to foreign locations
2. Real operations (physical relocation of business activity) away from higher-tax municipalities

To disentangle: authors test effects of LBT increases on log(total assets), log(fixed assets), log(turnover), log(employment) — no evidence of real operations reallocation across any of the four proxies (Table C5 in appendix, similar to Lichter et al. 2025)

### Consolidated MNC-level evidence for profit shifting mechanism
Three pieces of evidence (Table C1, C2, C4 in appendix):
1. After LBT rate increase, MNCs exposed to the change do not change overall reported profitability, but ETR and total tax liability decrease — consistent with profit reallocation abroad
- Note (fn. 23): for MNCs with many subsidiaries across German municipalities, LBT rate increase is defined as first one affecting any of their subsidiaries in the sample period
2. No significant changes in real operations at consolidated MNC level (Table C2)
3. Historical Orbis ownership data (2010–2019, excluding 2008–2009 due to low coverage — fn. 24: only 109 and 129 MNCs in 2008 and 2009 respectively): MNCs in municipalities that increased LBT rates increase their presence in tax havens at the intensive margin (more use of existing tax haven subsidiaries), but no change at extensive margin (no new tax haven subsidiaries formed) — validates fixed 2019 ownership approach

### Robustness to MNC characteristics
Table C7: results qualitatively robust when adding MNC-level controls interacted with post dummy: log(employees), number of subsidiaries, share of intangibles — proxies for MNC size and mobility. Note: smaller sample due to Orbis financial coverage limitations, so magnitudes differ

### Pre-trend sensitivity testing (Rambachan and Roth 2023)
- Tests significance when allowing per-period deviations from linear trend up to M (varies 0 to 0.5 × slope of coefficient of interest)
- LBT revenues: significant even for M up to 30% deviation from linear trend (OLS) and M up to 70% deviation (De Chaisemartin/d'Haultfoeuille) — see Figure A7
- Total tax revenues: Figure A8
- Indicates that pre-trend violations would have to be economically large to generate null effects

### Ruling out real operations at municipal level
- Panels c and d of Fig. 1 show no differential changes in aggregated firm employment or assets in high-exposure municipalities around LBT rate increases (Table A7, DID coefficients in cols. 1 and 2)
- LBT subject to formula apportionment based on payroll → no payroll shifting
- Extensive margin: Orbis incorporation date data used to count firms per municipality per year → no change in number of incorporated firms in more exposed municipalities following LBT rate increase (Table A7, col. 3)
- Combined with firm-level results: findings strongly interpreted as driven by profit shifting, not real behaviour

(Table D1 reports both OLS and IV elasticities)

- Outcome re-estimated as log(profit and loss before tax); divided by corresponding LBT rate change

### Why are municipal-level elasticities so much larger than firm-level?
Four possible explanations (authors note caution and call for more research):
1. Firm-level estimates use log(profit before tax) → only intensive margin; municipal estimates capture both intensive and extensive margins. Bilicka et al. (2024) argue accounting for extensive margin profit shifting almost doubles tax base response — could explain significant part of difference
2. Orbis financial coverage: only 14% of all firms and 27% of MNC subsidiaries report profits (Table B4). Firm-level elasticities reflect only larger firms; smaller firms may be more responsive
- Note (fn. 30): Zwick and Mahon (2017) and Coles et al. (2022) suggest smaller firms are more responsive to CIT changes
3. Municipal elasticity also captures non-corporate firms (sole proprietorships, partnerships) subject to LBT that are not in Orbis
4. Firm fixed effects in firm-level estimation capture only within-firm profit reporting changes; municipal level also captures reallocation of profits and business between treated firms

Also: IV municipal estimates larger than OLS municipal estimates — partly attenuation bias, but also LATE for politically competitive municipalities where tax base may be especially sensitive

### MVPF calculations (Hendren and Sprung-Keyser 2020 framework)
Formula: MVPF = 1 / (1 − τ/(1−τ) × α × e_B,1−τ), with α = 1 and τ = 0.12; e_B,1−τ = tax base elasticity w.r.t. net-of-LBT rate (fn. 31)

- MVPF interpreted as private welfare loss per euro of additional revenue raised (since LBT increase raises revenue rather than costs)
- Low-exposure municipalities: MVPF = 0.52 (OLS) or 0.5 (IV) — each euro of revenue raised costs €0.50–0.52 in private welfare; more efficient than income tax increases (MVPF 0.9–2.0) and fuel taxes (MVPF 0.7) per Hahn et al. (2024) and Hendren and Sprung-Keyser (2020)
- High-exposure municipalities: MVPF = −0.11 — large behavioural response more than offsets mechanical gain; higher LBT rate both reduces revenues and lowers private surplus = downward Laffer curve
- Average firm-level MVPF: 0.77 (efficiency cost of LBT = €0.77 per euro raised)
- Aggressive MNC subsidiaries: large negative MVPFs; other firms: positive MVPFs

### Section 5: Why do municipalities keep increasing LBT rates?

#### Explanation 1: Fiscal equalization system (Table 4)
- Fiscal equalization (Finanzausgleich) provides transfers from federal and state based on fiscal capacity and need
- Reduction in LBT revenues triggers increase in equalization transfers → municipalities are "insured" against tax policy mistakes
- Buettner (2007) shows German municipalities receive far more from fiscal equalization than US local governments, creating perverse incentives
- Buettner and Holm-Hadulla (2008) and Blesse et al. (2019): system incentivises LBT rates above optimal and makes municipalities more reliant on volatile LBT over stable property tax revenues
- Note (fn. 35): transfers variable used is "allgem. Zuweisung, Umlagen v. Bund, Land, Gemeinden/GV" (general transfers/allocations from federal government, state, and municipalities) which includes equalization transfers among others

Table 4 results:
- Col. 1: log(transfers) — high agg share × post = +0.035** → more exposed municipalities receive more transfers after LBT rate increase
- Col. 2: transfers share — high agg share × post = +0.023*** → transfers become larger share of revenues
- Col. 3: log(LBT rev) controlling for transfers — note (fn. 36): coefficient magnitude differs due to smaller sample; triple interaction on full sample is −0.0385 (p=0.0058)
- Col. 4: log(LBT rev + transfers) — high agg share × post = +0.012, not significant → revenue decline disappears when transfers are included
- Col. 5: log(total tax rev + transfers) — slight positive effect; revenue decline essentially eliminated
- Col. 6: including triple interaction with noequaliz dummy (=1 for Berlin, Bremen, Hamburg — city-states that do not receive state-level equalization) → noequaliz interaction = −0.150*** → for city-states, even OLS shows absolute total tax revenue losses; this is consistent with Fig. 2 (results unchanged when city-states excluded)

#### Explanation 2: Political economy (Table 5)
- LBT viewed as more voter-friendly than property tax (only other municipal instrument): business owners (especially foreign MNC owners) vote less than homeowners
- Municipalities unaware whether subsidiaries in their territory belong to aggressive MNCs a priori
- Blesse et al. (2019): LBT rate growth is lower in election years, and even lower for property taxes — confirming LBT viewed as more voter-friendly

## Page 15 – Discussion (Section 6)

### Main conclusions
- Novel estimates of how local tax revenues are affected by MNC profit shifting
- Causal evidence: exposure to more aggressive MNCs reduces local capacity to collect CIT-based revenues, affecting overall municipal tax revenues

### Policy implications

For local governments:
- Increasing LBT rates in municipalities with large aggressive MNC presence may produce opposite of intended effect — reducing rather than increasing LBT revenues
- Since profits are shifted abroad (not across German municipalities), federal and state CIT revenues are also affected, not just municipal LBT — additional stress on federal revenues because the declining MNC tax base also shrinks federal revenues

For tax revenue structure and distribution:
- Profit-shifting ability affects municipal tax revenue structure and has distributional consequences
- Municipalities more exposed to profit-shifting MNCs may be forced to rely more on property taxes
- If corporate tax incidence is more progressive than property tax incidence, shifting to property taxes in more exposed municipalities could increase income inequality
- This is identified as a promising area for future research

### Corporate tax avoidance and fiscal autonomy
- Aggressive MNCs may constrain types of fiscal policies available to municipalities
- By shifting profits abroad after LBT increases, they may induce municipalities to use other instruments or reduce spending
- This represents a "previously unrecognised cost of corporate tax avoidance" that may be important to local governments

### Implications for GMT / Pillar Two (explicitly stated in paper)
- Paper explicitly mentions that policies aimed at reducing profit-shifting opportunities, such as a global minimum tax, could provide additional benefits for municipal governments beyond the global-level benefits (lower effective tax rate differences between avoiding and non-avoiding firms)
- A global minimum tax could bring "greater autonomy in tax revenue structure for local governments"
