# Boadway & Keen (2026) - "A Global Minimum Wealth Tax: Issues of Economics and Implementation"
Intertax, 54(1), 49–54

## General framing

The paper is a response/debate piece reacting to Zucman (2025) "The Billionaire Tax: A (Modest) Proposal for the 21st Century" (also in Intertax, same issue) and the more detailed Zucman (2024) "A Blueprint for a Coordinated Minimum Effective Tax Standard for Ultra-High Wealth Individuals" (commissioned by the Brazilian G20 Presidency). The authors say they share the basic objective, i.e. making ultra-high net worth (UHNW) individuals pay substantially more tax, justified on equity grounds and because of the political influence that massive wealth brings. But they raise important conceptual and implementation problems.

The core proposal of Zucman: those with wealth above USD 1 billion should pay personal taxes (income taxes, wealth taxes, and equivalent charges) of at least 2% of their worldwide wealth, with a top-up mechanism if they fall below that threshold.

The paper is organised around three questions: (1) Is this scheme a good way to achieve the objective? (2) Is it practically feasible? (3) Are there better alternatives?

## p. 49–50 – Section 1: Fish or Fowl?

Zucman presents the scheme as a "presumptive income tax", with wealth serving as a proxy for income that is hard to measure directly. Boadway & Keen argue this framing is misleading: it would be more accurately described as "a wealth tax with non-refundable credit for income, wealth and equivalent taxes", since it is the effective rate on wealth, not income, that is ultimately the standard of adequacy. They suspect the "presumptive income tax" label is chosen to avoid constitutional challenges, particularly in the US where a direct wealth tax would likely face legal obstacles. They note, however, that this choice of words probably would not eliminate legal risk. Footnote 4 is interesting here: they refer to the Dutch "Box 3" experience, where the Dutch government presented a tax on assumed returns on assets as a presumptive income tax, but the Dutch Supreme Court ruled against it (Hoge Raad 24 Dec. 2021, n° 21/01243) because it generated "inequities disproportionate to its benefits" and violated property rights.

Key critique: rather than a hybrid that is "neither fish nor fowl", why not simply add a standalone wealth tax on top of the income tax, or fix the income tax itself?

### 1.1 Assessing the Impact

Distributional reach of the proposal is limited:
- For UHNWs who already pay enough income tax to exceed the 2% threshold, their wealth accumulation is unaffected. The proposal therefore "lacks the wider distributional appeal" of a standalone wealth tax.
- The authors note the proposal could be seen as a steppingstone toward a generally applicable wealth tax, for better or worse.

On efficiency and equity (for those on whom it binds):

- If imposed unexpectedly and only once, a wealth tax would be a "lump sum" tax: it does not distort behaviour because the base was fixed by past decisions. This is attractive in efficiency terms (no deadweight loss) and can be justified on equity grounds to the extent wealth reflects "rents" (returns in excess of the minimum required). Others would see it as unjust retrospective taxation.
- But this is not a one-off levy. It is a recurrent tax. Then the base in each period includes (1) wealth already accumulated at introduction (which retains lump-sum character) and (2) cumulative savings since introduction (where distortions can arise, since future returns on new savings generate a higher future wealth tax liability).

Capital income under a recurrent wealth tax has three components:
- Rents: no distortionary effect from taxing these; UHNWs may be especially well-placed to harvest rents.
- Risk component: largely untaxed under a wealth tax in the sense that negative returns reduce the base symmetrically. This avoids the anti-risk-taking bias of income taxes without full loss offset. Over the long run only the risk premium is taxed.
- Risk-free return (reward from deferring consumption): taxing this creates a bias against savings and investment, which many consider especially harmful (citing Straub & Werning, 2020, "Positive Long-Run Capital Taxation: Chamley-Judd Revisited", 110 Am. Econ. Rev. 86–119). How serious this is for UHNWs is unclear: their alternatives to saving are consuming wealth (limited at such scale) or giving it away (which would at least disperse wealth but raises inheritance issues). At Zucman's assumed average return of 7.5% for billionaires since the 1980s, a 2% wealth tax is equivalent to a "27% tax on that return", so distortionary costs are "not inherently trivial".

## p. 51 – Section 1.2: An Alternative Route?

The authors ask what a wealth tax adds beyond what capital income taxes already do. On implementation difficulties: some standard problems (liquidity) apply less to UHNWs who can borrow against assets (as Zucman notes). Others remain, e.g. dealing with large year-to-year fluctuations in wealth.

Weaknesses in current capital income taxation (acknowledged as real and widespread):
- Exclusion of imputed rents on owner-occupied housing
- Preferential treatment of family farms and businesses
- Light taxation of dividends (as rough corporate-personal integration)
- Most importantly: favourable treatment of capital gains, both explicitly (inflation adjustment) and implicitly (realisation basis rather than accrual). Under realisation-basis CGT, tax can be deferred indefinitely, even beyond death in the US. Zucman notes about 60% of wealth for those above USD 100 million consists of unrealised capital gains not yet taxed personally (footnote 10).

A wealth tax base would include such unrealised gains, and this is presented as a key merit. But:
- About half of global billionaires' wealth is in publicly listed shares (valuations available), but the other half is not: private businesses, private equity, art, and crypto assets may be hard to value or even identify. "Shifting to a wealth-based assessment does not inherently resolve the current difficulties in assessing income."
- More fundamentally: it is technically possible to tax gains on realisation in a way that closely replicates the revenue and incentive effects of taxing them on accrual:
  - For traded assets: marked-to-market taxation with cumulation of liability until realisation.
  - For non-traded assets: apportioning gains over the holding period and calculating the tax that would have been due in each year, cumulated with interest to realisation (see Auerbach 1991, "Retrospective Capital Gains Taxation", 81 Am. Econ. Rev. 167–178; and Auerbach & Bradford 2004, "Generalized Cash-Flow Taxation", 88 J. Pub. Econ. 957–980).
  - These methods require an actual or deemed disposal, e.g. at death.

These schemes are "unfamiliar, complex" and involve high compliance and administrative costs. There was a short-lived Italian experiment (described in Alworth, Arachi & Hamaui, 2003, 56 Nat'l Tax J. 197–219). But advances in technology have made such approaches increasingly feasible. The authors conclude: "failure to tax capital gains on accrual or its equivalent thus looks like a political choice rather than a technical necessity."

On equivalence and the hybrid structure:
- The proposal credits "individual income taxes, wealth taxes and economically equivalent levies" against the minimum. But payroll taxes, consumption taxes, property taxes, and corporate taxes are excluded.
- The authors argue this is conceptually arbitrary:
  - Payroll and consumption taxes act similarly to labour income taxes.
  - Property taxes are a form of wealth tax.
  - Corporate taxes can be seen as withholding taxes for capital gains accrued in corporations that escape personal taxation indefinitely. Zucman himself stresses corporate taxes in taxing the wealthy, so excluding them is hard to justify.
  - Inheritance and gift taxes? Their status under the scheme is not clarified.
- The wider the creditability, the less top-up revenue unless the minimum rate is raised. But narrower creditability seems "ultimately to be somewhat arbitrary and so potentially unjust."
- A practical consequence: excluding property taxes gives countries an incentive to reduce property taxes on assets likely to be held by UHNWs, to limit their burden. This distorts policy choices.

On inheritance as an alternative:
- Much wealth is passed on to heirs, perpetuating dynastic inequality. A "cumulative and possibly progressive tax on inheritances, such as that proposed by the Mirrlees Review" (Adam et al., Tax by Design, IFS 2011) would be more targeted at this source of inequality.
- Conclusion of this section mirrors Hebous, Klemm, Michielse & Osorio-Buitron (2024), "How to Tax Wealth", IMF Note/2024/001: a minimum wealth tax could serve as a backstop to the personal income tax, but inheritance/gift taxes address a more fundamental source of wealth inequality.

## p. 52–53 – Section 2: Coordination

The risk of UHNW mobility is real: wealth taxes have partly disappeared in recent years because of this concern. Evidence does not always show large location effects (as Zucman notes), but:
- The proposed rate is relatively high.
- The number of people involved is small, making individual mobility impactful.
- The increase in US citizenship renunciations after FATCA (from ~500/year in 2005–2009 to ~3,400/year in 2010–2020) is suggestive. Eduardo Saverin (Facebook co-founder, then estimated wealth USD 2–3 billion) is given as a prominent example. Organ (2024), "Citizenship and Taxes", 31 Int. Tax Pub. Fin. 404–453, formally finds that taxation significantly affects renunciation decisions.

Zucman draws the coordination mechanism by analogy with Pillar Two of the OECD/G20 project. The authors work through this analogy carefully.

Analogy:
- UHNW individual ≈ ultimate parent entity of Pillar Two
- Assets in each country ≈ constituent entities

But the analogy is imperfect in a crucial way:
- Pillar Two operates on a jurisdictional basis: it tops up the effective tax rate to 15% in each jurisdiction separately.
- The proposed minimum wealth tax operates on a worldwide basis: it aims only to ensure the UHNW's total global tax is at least 2% of worldwide wealth, not that each country-level asset is taxed at 2%.
- In this sense the proposal is closer to the US NCTI (formerly GILTI) than to Pillar Two.

Available domestic instruments (by analogy):
- Analogue to the Income Inclusion Rule (IIR): the residence country of the UHNW can tax their worldwide assets.
- Analogue to a Domestic Minimum Top-up Tax (DMTT): countries where the UHNW holds assets could tax those assets at up to 2% and expect a credit in the residence country.

But this topping up at the jurisdictional level requires information on income, wealth and equivalent taxes paid across all countries where the UHNW holds assets. That information is "not readily available", which makes the DMTT analogue far less practical than under Pillar Two.

Analogue to the Undertaxed Profit Rule (UTPR):
- Under Pillar Two, the UTPR allows country A (which adopted the minimum) to collect top-up taxes on profits in country C (which has not taxed them to the minimum) when the MNE has some presence in A, even if it is resident in country B (which also has not adopted).
- The wealth tax analogue: country A could top up taxes on UHNW assets in country C, if the UHNW is resident in B (non-adopting) and has some assets in A, if C has not taxed those assets to 2%.
- This would give B an incentive to adopt the minimum (to avoid A taxing its residents' assets elsewhere), which is exactly the propagation logic of Pillar Two's UTPR.
- However, the informational requirements are "even more daunting" under the worldwide wealth approach: country A would need to know all income and equivalent taxes the UHNW paid in all other countries, not just in C.
- Furthermore, the UTPR of Pillar Two itself has become "extremely contentious", its fate now "uncertain." The extra-territorial nature makes it politically fragile.

Without a functioning UTPR analogue, countries that host UHNW residents (or wish to attract them) have little incentive to adopt the scheme.

Political feasibility: Just as Pillar Two required commitment from major capital exporters, a global minimum wealth tax requires commitment from the countries where UHNWs primarily reside. Currently "the G20 does not appear in hurry to pursue the idea."

On critical mass: Zucman (2024) notes but does not elaborate on whether the scheme requires truly global participation or just a critical mass. The authors observe:
- A high proportion of UHNWs reside in a few high-income countries, so a small coalition could in principle cover most.
- But mobility pressures increase if only few countries participate.
- Lower-income countries would gain little revenue but face additional administrative burden, another iteration of the same imbalance seen in Pillar Two discussions.
