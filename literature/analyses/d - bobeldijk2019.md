# Bobeldijk & Klaassen (2019) Country-by-Country Reporting and the Effective Tax Rate
Intertax, 47(12)


The article argues that the ETR (effective tax rate) as derivable from the CbCR (country-by-country report) cannot be accurately used in high-level risk analyses. The two definitions that are the focus are "profit (loss) before income tax" and "income tax accrued - current year." Both definitions suffer from structural flaws that make a deviating ETR an unreliable risk signal.



## p. 1057 - Introduction (section 1)

- The political context: tax avoidance has become a major public and political issue. The Greens/EFA Group in the European Parliament (Jansky, 22 Jan 2019) reported that the unweighted average ETR of EU companies is 15%, against a 23% average statutory rate, concluding that "many MNEs do not pay much tax in many countries."
- Studies show a small number of "profit-haven" jurisdictions capture a disproportionate share of total profits (Cobham & Jansky, Dev. Pol'y Rev. 2019, pp. 91-110).
- IRS CbCR data for US companies for reporting year 2016: in Bermuda, total profit of USD 24.9 billion was reported with a total workforce of only 551 (so profit per employee of over EUR 45 million). 229 of 1,093 US filers were active in Bermuda.
- MNEs can use international structures to erode tax bases (Base Erosion) or shift profits to low-tax countries (Profit Shifting), leading to unfair competition between international and domestic companies (OECD, Addressing Base Erosion and Profit Shifting, 2013).
- In September 2013, G20 leaders together with the OECD launched the BEPS project. One of its results: Action 13 on Country-by-Country Reporting (CbCR) (OECD, Action 13 - 2015 Final Report).
- Under BEPS Action 13: all large MNEs with consolidated group revenue of at least EUR 750 million must prepare a CbC report with aggregate data on global allocation of income, profit, taxes paid, and economic activity per tax jurisdiction.
- The CbC report is shared with tax administrations for use in "high level transfer pricing and BEPS risk assessments" (OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines 2017, para. 5.25). Reduces information asymmetry between companies and tax authorities.
- BEPS Action 11 identifies six indicators of BEPS, including high profitability in combination with a low ETR. OECD quote: "where BEPS is present, it may be expected that the profit rate (...) will be higher in jurisdictions where an MNE group has a lower effective tax rate compared with jurisdictions where the MNE group has a higher effective tax rate." (OECD, Country-by-Country Reporting: Handbook on Effective Tax Risk Assessment, 2017, pp. 10-11.)
- The OECD Handbook on Effective Tax Risk Assessment also identifies a low ETR in combination with a high profit as a risk indicator (ibid., p. 10).
- Article focus: critically analysing the definitions of "profit (loss) before income tax" and "income tax accrued" in CbCR, since ETR = income tax accrued / profit (loss) before income tax.
- The OECD was (at time of writing) in the process of evaluating BEPS Action 13 (a review was mandated "no later than the end of 2020"). Growing trend towards public CbCR increases the importance of definitional clarity and comparability.



## p. 1058-1059 - Country-by-Country Reporting (section 2)

### Section 2.1 - Reason and Objective of CbCR

- Aggressive tax planning of large MNEs has been the subject of intense public debate.
- OECD estimates global CIT revenue losses from BEPS at 4%-10% of global CIT revenues (OECD, Explanatory Statement, 2015).
- Causes identified by OECD: aggressive tax planning, lack of transparency, limited country enforcement resources.
- BEPS Action Plan comprises 15 Actions. Action 13 "Transfer Pricing Documentation and Country-by-Country Reporting" proposes standardisation of TP documentation.
- The "three-tiered standardised approach" was introduced: master file, local file, and country-by-country report.
- OECD: this documentation will help jurisdictions "ascertain the economic activity of multinationals beyond each country's own territorial limits, regardless of each country's individual political power" (Brauner, Intertax 2015, pp. 72-84).
- OECD states the documentation obligation "will require taxpayers to articulate consistent transfer pricing positions and will provide tax administrations with useful information to assess transfer pricing risks, make determinations about where audit resources can most effectively be deployed, and, in the event audits are called for, provide information to commence and target audit enquiries." (OECD Action 13 Final Report, p. 9.)
- Coverage threshold: MNEs with group turnover of EUR 750 million or more. The OECD calculated this covers approximately 90% of global MNE turnover, while requiring only 10-15% of MNEs to prepare a report (OECD Action 13 Final Report, p. 21).

### Section 2.2 - Directive (EU) 2016/881 (DAC 4)

- The EU followed the OECD and made CbCR preparation and automatic exchange among EU Member States mandatory via Directive (EU) 2016/881 (DAC 4). This is an amendment to the original Directive (EU) 2011/16 (which already required automatic exchange of cross-border rulings).
- DAC 4 is "nearly identical" to the OECD model legislation in the 2015 Action 13 Final Report.
- Recital 17 to the Directive: Member States should use the 2015 OECD Final Report "as a source of illustration or interpretation for this Directive."

### Section 2.3 - Public CbCR

- On 12 April 2016, the European Commission submitted a proposal to amend Directive (EU) 2013/34 to lead to (a form of) public CbCR (COM/2016/0198 final). No unanimity among Member States at time of writing.
- Directive 2013/34 and the EU Capital Requirements Directive IV: European businesses in extractive industry and financial institutions respectively are already required to include some form of CbCR in financial statements. CRD IV requires country-specific revenues, profit and tax payment disclosure.
- Voluntary public CbCR is possible: Shell announced (at a public hearing in 2019) it would voluntarily engage in public CbCR.
- The Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) prepared an exposure draft on "Tax and Payments to Governments" (March 2019), proposing an extensive CbC report in the annual report as a requirement (mandatory). GRI Standards classify instructions as requirements, recommendations, or guidance - the CbCR is classified as a requirement.
- The definitions in this GRI exposure draft are "largely in line with Action 13," making scrutiny of the Action 13 definitions all the more relevant.

### Section 2.4 - The Country-by-Country Report

- The CbC report comprises three tables.
- Table 1: (related party) revenues, profit (loss) before income tax, income tax paid, and number of FTEs per country.
- Important: an early discussion draft (OECD Discussion Draft, 30 Jan 2014) had proposed entity-level reporting, but this was not upheld. Therefore, if an MNE has multiple constituent entities in a country, only the total figures for that country appear in Table 1. These totals are determined by aggregation - intra-country transactions are not eliminated, leading to double counting.
- Table 2: entity-level information; the "main activity" must be identified per constituent entity.
- Table 3: free text / additional information.
- Accounting figures are used in the CbC report. MNEs can choose which data source to use (consolidation reporting packages, separate entity statutory financial statements, or internal management accounts) as long as a consistent method is used and explained in Table 3. No adjustments for differences in accounting standards across jurisdictions are required (Rasch, Mank & Tomson, Transfer Pricing Report 2015: "it is unclear why the OECD did not follow established international accounting standards. In addition, the OECD leaves a number of aspects - such as the source of the reported information - to a taxpayer's discretion. This will result in inconsistent implementation, a further limitation of the report's validity, and a considerable risk of double taxation if tax authorities use country-by-country reported data to make an assessment.")
- Practical for businesses, but impairs comparability across CbCR reports. Also risk of cherry-picking (businesses can choose which figures to use per jurisdiction).
- Currently impossible to reconcile the CbCR with an MNE's financial statements, mainly because of aggregation and different possible data sources. Authors argue reconciliation should be made mandatory. The GRI exposure draft already requires use of figures from financial statements and explanation of any differences.



## p. 1060-1063 - Definitions in the CbC Report (section 3)

### Section 3.1 - Introduction

Table 1 includes columns for "profit (loss) before income tax" and "income tax accrued." Both are relevant for ETR calculation. Section 3 analyses these terms in detail, including the consequences of aggregation.

### Section 3.2 - Profit (Loss) Before Income Tax

- DAC 4 definition (mirroring Action 13): "the reporting MNE shall report the sum of the profit (loss) before income tax for all the Constituent Entities resident for tax purposes in the relevant tax jurisdiction. The profit (loss) before income tax shall include all extraordinary income and expense items."
- This definition does not clarify how dividends and share of earnings of other constituent entities (including revaluations and translation results) should be treated.
- Definition of "revenues" in DAC 4: "Revenues shall include revenues from sales of inventory and properties, services, royalties, interest, premiums and any other amounts. Revenues shall exclude payments received from other Constituent Entities that are treated as dividends in the payer's tax jurisdiction."
- Two conflicting interpretations:
  - Because dividends are expressly excluded from revenues (profit = income minus costs), they should also be excluded from profit (loss) before income tax.
  - Alternatively, since dividends are expressly excluded from "revenues" but not from "profit (loss) before income tax," the silence on dividends in the latter definition could mean they are included in profit.
- OECD only addressed this question in September 2018 (OECD, Guidance on the Implementation of CbCR - BEPS Action 13, 2018, p. 13). The authors describe this "guidance" as barely guidance: jurisdictions are allowed flexibility in how to treat dividends received from other constituent entities in profit (loss) before income tax. Jurisdictions are merely encouraged to ask taxpayers to indicate in Table 3 whether dividends/share of earnings are included, and if so for which jurisdictions. The guidance does not require disclosure of the amount of dividend per jurisdiction. This means tax authorities still cannot determine whether a deviating ETR is caused by (exempt) dividends/share of earnings, or by other causes.
- In many countries, dividends and share of earnings of constituent entities are exempt or the underlying tax is credited. Including them in profit (loss) before income tax without a corresponding tax expense lowers the ETR without indicating tax avoidance.
- Even Table 2 (which shows main activities and can reveal holding company status) is insufficient, because where multiple companies with different activities exist in one country, only one profit figure appears in Table 1 (country-level, not entity-level). Impossible to estimate whether the lower ETR is caused by dividend income.
- GRI exposure draft: does not expressly exclude dividends from profit (loss) before income tax, but requires reconciliation with consolidated financial statements (which eliminate intra-group dividends). Authors infer that dividends are not included in the GRI approach, supported by the note that "intra-group transactions within the same tax jurisdiction are not included in the disclosure" in the GRI exposure draft (to avoid double counting and misleading impressions about scale of activity).

### Section 3.3 - Income Tax Accrued - Current Year

- In the 2014 draft CbCR template, only "income tax paid (on cash basis)" and "total withholding tax paid" were proposed. In the final template, these were combined, and a separate column for "income tax accrued" was added.
- DAC 4 definition (mirroring Action 13): "the reporting MNE shall report the sum of the accrued current tax expense recorded on taxable profits or losses of the year of reporting of all the Constituent Entities resident for tax purposes in the relevant tax jurisdiction. The current tax expense shall reflect only operations in the current year and shall not include deferred taxes or provisions for uncertain tax liabilities."
- Translations issue: Dutch, Belgian, and Austrian translations refer to "income tax payables" (Dutch: "nog te betalen belastinglasten"; Belgian: "nog te betalen belastingen"; Austrian: "Noch zu zahlende Ertragsteuer (laufendes Jahr)"). This could be misread as referring only to the balance sheet item, rather than the P&L item "accrued current tax expense."
- OECD further guidance (ibid., p. 10) clarified that the amount in income tax accrued is independent of whether tax was actually paid in that financial year (e.g., via provisional assessment). This raises the question of what "accrued" adds. Also, "accrued current tax expense" is not a term used in generally accepted accounting standards, creating interpretation risk.
- Carry-forward losses: unclear whether income tax accrued should be nil where taxable profit is offset against carry-forward losses, or whether it should be based on taxable profit before loss compensation. The authors find the OECD's intentions unclear. If income tax accrued is nil due to loss offset, this produces an ETR of nil, which might incorrectly trigger a "red flag" since the CbCR contains no indication of loss compensation (Rasch, Mank & Tomson, 2015, also flag this).
- Deliberate exclusion of deferred taxes: neither the discussion draft nor the final report explains why. Possible reason: deferred taxes are forward-looking while CbCR is backward-looking (Evers, Meier & Spengel, 2014).
- During the Action 13 consultation, BIAC (Business At OECD) had no unequivocal view on deferred taxes and suggested allowing businesses to choose between tax paid, current tax expense, or total tax expense (with consistent use and explanation). The authors agree the OECD was right to reject this, since it would allow businesses to present whatever suited them, undermining comparability.
- Volvo AB commented in the consultation (OECD, Public Comments Volume I, 2014, pp. 120-121) that because revenues and profit before income tax are based on accounting figures, it would not make sense to report only the fiscal tax expense. Especially in loss-making years (possible carryback) or profit-making years with loss carryforward (no tax paid), this creates a distorted picture. Volvo argued for reporting total tax expense (current + deferred).
- Key problem identified by authors: a narrow definition of "income tax accrued" means a deviating ETR may incorrectly be flagged as a risk indicator when the deviation is actually due to temporary differences (deferred tax positions). A low ETR does not necessarily mean tax avoidance; it can also be explained by temporary differences between taxable and accounting profit.
- The column "income tax paid (on cash basis)" helps show whether tax is actually being paid, but if an amount appears in "income tax accrued" for multiple years without any cash payment, questions can be raised.
- GRI exposure draft: still links to the OECD definition (deferred taxes and provisions for uncertain tax positions are excluded from income tax accrued), but requires an explanation of the difference between corporate tax accrued on profit/loss and the tax due if the statutory rate is applied to profit/loss before tax. Authors see this as recognition by GRI that comparing accounting profit with the fiscal tax expense produces differences requiring explanation.

### Section 3.4 - Aggregation vs. Consolidation

- BEPS Action 13 requires CbCR on an aggregated level: all companies in a country are added up. Intra-group transactions within a country cannot be eliminated, unlike in consolidated financial statements, leading to potential double counting.
- Same problem applies to dividends if they are included in profit before tax and multiple holding companies pass on dividends within the same country.
- Businesses not always aware of the concept of aggregation, causing errors in CbCR (Hanlon, Bull. for Int'l Tax'n 2018; van Werkhoven, Weekblad Fiscaal Recht 2017).
- The 2014 OECD discussion draft did not expressly exclude dividends from revenues. In the consultation, BIAC (OECD Public Comments Volume I, p. 108) wrote: "When a 'bottom-up' approach is adopted, dividends received from MNE group members will need to be excluded from revenues and from income before taxes to avoid counting the same income multiple times where holding companies are used." The adjusted definition of "revenues" may be based on this comment.
- Authors argue that by the same logic, dividends and share of earnings (including revaluations) of constituent entities should also have been expressly excluded from "profit (loss) before income tax," and the current definition is an oversight.
- Alternative: consolidation on a country level (as opposed to aggregation). Advantages: transactions between related parties within the same country are eliminated, while cross-border related-party transactions remain visible. No double counting, while high-level TP risk analysis is still possible. The GRI exposure draft supports this approach ("Intra-group transactions within the same tax jurisdiction are not included in this disclosure, but the organization can report this information separately").



## p. 1064-1066 - Calculation of the Effective Tax Rate (section 4)

### Section 4.1 - Introduction

- The ETR is the counterpart of the statutory tax rate. ETR = accounting tax expense / profit before tax. A low ETR (especially if deviating from the statutory rate) is an indicator of possible tax avoidance.
- This section discusses differences between ETR based on CbCR and ETR under IAS 12 (part of IFRS, mandatory for European listed companies).

### Section 4.2 - ETR Based on the CbC Report

- CbCR-based ETR = income tax accrued (current year) / profit (loss) before income tax.
- The OECD Handbook on Effective Tax Risk Assessment (2017, p. 37) does not define the ETR precisely, but states that the ETR "can - for example - be calculated as 'income tax accrued (current year)'/'profit (loss) before income tax'." The "for example" indicates other calculations are possible. The only other plausible variant the authors identify is: income tax paid (on cash basis) / profit (loss) before income tax. They argue this cash-based calculation is not effective for risk analysis, because tax paid is independent of the profit generated in that year (there may be prepayments or refunds relating to other years), making the ratio meaningless.

### Section 4.3 - ETR According to IAS 12

- IAS 12.86: "The average effective tax rate is the tax expense (income) divided by the accounting profit."
- IAS 12.5 defines "tax expense (income)": "the aggregate amount included in the determination of profit or loss for the period in respect of current tax and deferred tax." So the tax expense comprises both current and deferred tax.
- IAS 12.5 definitions:
  - Current tax: "the amount of income taxes payable (recoverable) in respect of the taxable profit (tax loss) for a period."
  - Deferred tax liabilities: "the amounts of income taxes payable in future periods in respect of taxable temporary differences."
- Under IAS 12: temporary differences do not affect the ETR for the period (because deferred taxes offset them). Permanent differences (non-deductible interest, exempt dividends, patent box, etc.) do affect the ETR vs. the statutory rate, since no deferred tax can be made for permanent differences.
- IAS 12.80: the tax expense includes not only the current tax for the year but also "any adjustments recognised in the period for current tax of prior periods."
- IAS 12 does not expressly address provisions for uncertain tax liabilities, except that IAS 37 must be followed where applicable (IAS 12.88). Costs from provisions for uncertain tax liabilities are recognised as "income tax expense" in the financial statements.
- IAS 12.58: tax expenses/income relating to items recognised outside profit or loss (in Other Comprehensive Income or directly in equity) are also recognised outside profit or loss.
- Withholding taxes: IAS 12 does not expressly include/exclude them from tax expense. IAS 12.65A: withholding tax on dividends must be charged to shareholders' equity (it is a cost for the recipient, not for the distributor). Whether withholding tax is part of the recipient's tax expense depends on IAS 12.2: "income taxes include all domestic and foreign taxes which are based on taxable profits."

### Section 4.4 - Differences Between ETR (IAS 12) and ETR (CbCR)

Summary comparison (Figure 2 in article):

| Component | ETR (IAS 12) | ETR (CbCR) |
||||
| Current tax expense | Yes | Yes |
| Deferred tax expense | Yes | No |
| Changes in provisions for uncertain tax liabilities | Yes | No |
| Adjustments for current tax of prior periods | Yes | No |
| Tax effects on items recognised outside P&L (OCI/equity) | No | Not mentioned |
| Withholding taxes | Yes, if based on taxable profits | Not mentioned |

- The main difference: the IAS 12 ETR includes deferred taxes, capturing what the business will ultimately pay or receive on the current period's result. Without permanent differences, the IAS 12 ETR should align with the statutory rate.
- Because CbCR uses only current taxes, the CbCR ETR can deviate from the statutory rate due to temporary differences (e.g., accelerated tax depreciation, loss carryforward). Allowing deferred taxes in "income tax accrued" would, in the authors' view, provide better insight into permanent differences.
- Second major difference: changes in provisions for uncertain tax liabilities are excluded from "income tax accrued" in CbCR, but included in "income tax expense" under IAS 12. Consequence: "income tax accrued" links only to the actual taxable profit as reported. If a tax dispute is resolved in a later year with an additional assessment, this does not appear in "income tax accrued" for that year (though it may increase "income tax paid (on cash basis)").
- Authors' view: excluding provisions for uncertain tax liabilities from "income tax accrued" has no added value. Including them would reduce errors (no manual removal needed), and information on such provisions can itself be a risk indicator (an MNE taking aggressive tax positions that may ultimately pay more tax). Subjectivity risk is limited because provisions require the taxpayer to assess that the chance of having to pay tax exceeds the chance of not having to, and this is typically verified by an external auditor.



## p. 1067-1068 - Detection of Possible Tax Avoidance Based on a Deviating ETR (section 5)

### Section 5.1 - Introduction

Section analyses the extent to which possible tax avoidance can be detected based on a deviating ETR, illustrated through a fictitious CbCR (Figure 3). Focus is on transfer pricing risks, since studies show that TP (alongside thin capitalisation) is a "primary driver of tax avoidance" (Taylor & Richardson, The Int'l J. of Accounting, 2012; Evers, Meier & Spengel, 2014). TP in general and IP licensing in particular are the most important profit-shifting channels.

OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines (2017), para. 5.25 expressly identifies assessing substantial TP risks as one of the objectives of CbCR.

### Section 5.2 - CbCR in Relation to the Arm's Length Principle

- Hanlon (Bull. for Int'l Tax'n, 2018) argued CbCR creates a "clear and well-acknowledged disconnect" with the arm's length principle, since TP rules are not based on formulary apportionment or degree of economic activity. Hanlon wrote: "MNEs are taxed using arm's length transfer pricing principles. However, CbyC reporting will require them to gather and report data based on where the underlying economic activity is located. There is clearly a disconnect between the CbyC data and the arm's length principle transfer pricing rules: CbyC data will not provide direct information about whether an arm's length price was employed. Requiring CbyC reporting while taxing using the arm's length principle will potentially lead to a great deal of uncertainty and exposure to tax claims for MNEs."
- Klaassen & Bobeldijk disagree with Hanlon. The arm's length principle is based on value creation, and a high or low profit relative to turnover/assets/employees may be eye-catching but can still be arm's-length. They argue Hanlon underestimates Table 2 (main activities per entity), that CbCR must be interpreted alongside the master file and local file, and that CbCR does not determine whether there is tax avoidance, but is merely a risk detection tool.
- OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines (2017), para. 5.25: the high-level CbCR data "on its own does not constitute conclusive evidence that transfer prices are or are not appropriate."

### Section 5.3 - Detection of Transfer Pricing Risks in the CbCR

Based on the fictitious CbCR (Figure 3 - figures in EUR millions, average ETR across countries only 12%), the authors make the following observations:

- Country F: large profit, zero taxes (country imposes no CIT). Profit entirely from related-party transactions (no unrelated-party revenues at all). This is identified as a potential artificial profit transfer to a low-tax jurisdiction, consistent with the Handbook risk: "There are jurisdictions with significant profits but little substantial activity" (Handbook on Effective Tax Risk Assessment, pp. 38-39).
- Country A (ultimate parent entity): ETR of 15% against a statutory rate of 25%. Cannot tell from CbCR whether this is caused by a permanent difference (patent box, exempt dividends) or by temporary differences with deferred tax positions. CbCR does not make this transparent.
- Country D: ETR of 10% - same analytical problem.
- Country E: ETR of 5% against a statutory rate of 12.5%. Could be deferred taxes (e.g., accounting vs. tax depreciation differences) or offsettable losses.
- Countries B and C: ETRs of 27% and 26% respectively (both close to their statutory rates), but different operating margins. Country B: operating margin = 100/615 = 16.2%. Country C: operating margin = 15/165 = 9.1%. Whether Country B receives a too-high (non-arm's-length) intragroup payment, at the expense of Country A, cannot be determined from the ETR alone.
- Key conclusion from analysis: a low ETR does not necessarily indicate tax avoidance from incorrect TP application. The OECD acknowledges this in the Handbook (para. 61: "The simple fact that a group has a low effective tax rate in a particular jurisdiction does not mean that the group is engaged in BEPS. (...) a number of jurisdictions have tax regimes which provide for a lower rate of tax on certain forms of income but which are not harmful.") However, the OECD fails to recognise that a deviating ETR may also result from the narrow definition of "income tax accrued." Including deferred taxes in "income tax accrued" would at least rule out temporary differences as a cause of ETR deviations, enabling better detection of permanent differences. The ETR is also unreliable if it is unclear whether dividends are included in "profit (loss) before income tax." Overall conclusion: "the value of the ETR, based on the existing definitions in the country-by-country report, for detecting incorrect application of transfer pricing is only of minor importance."
- Additional structural limitation of CbCR: no column for related-party costs. No insight into individual related-party transactions. The CbCR shows where profits end up, but cannot identify which jurisdictions made payments leading to disproportionate profits elsewhere. This limits the risk analysis further (Brauner, Intertax 2015, p. 77: "current arm's length based transfer pricing rules apply exclusively to transactions, and therefore only in rare circumstances would the information from the CbC report be useful under the current rules"). Authors suggest expanding the CbCR template.



## p. 1069 - Conclusions (section 6)

- The existence of CbCR is "a major step towards curbing tax avoidance." Useful tool for detecting possible tax avoidance and providing insight into global profit allocation. The reporting obligation itself may also deter MNEs from setting up aggressive structures.
- However, the design is not beyond criticism. The definitions of "profit (loss) before income tax" and "income tax accrued" make the CbCR "inadequate for the objective set."

Four concrete proposals:

1. Switch from aggregation to country-level consolidation. This eliminates intra-country intragroup transactions, removes the double-counting problem for dividends/share of earnings, and aligns with the GRI exposure draft approach.

2. Explicitly exclude dividends and share of earnings of constituent entities from "profit (loss) before income tax." Authors argue this is a limited administrative exercise for MNEs because the same elimination is already performed in consolidated financial statements.

3. Replace "income tax accrued (current year)" with "income tax expense" as defined in IAS 12. This means including deferred taxes and changes in provisions for uncertain tax liabilities. Benefits: (a) removes possibility that ETR deviations are caused by temporary differences, improving detection of permanent differences; (b) resolves the ambiguity about carry-forward losses (deferred tax assets on loss carryforwards are part of IAS 12 income tax expense); (c) enables direct reconciliation between financial statements and CbCR; (d) material prior-year adjustments can still be explained in Table 3. The "income tax paid (on cash basis)" column remains sufficient to show actual cash tax payments.

4. Make reconciliation between the CbCR and the consolidated financial statements mandatory. Currently impossible because of aggregation and different permitted data sources. This would also help tax authorities verify completeness of filed CbCRs. Consistent with the GRI exposure draft.

- General note on public CbCR: growing public pressure may push more MNEs towards public CbCR (voluntarily or involuntarily). In that context, accurate definitions and linkage to audited consolidated financial statements become even more important, to avoid "naming and shaming" based on incomparable or misleading figures.
