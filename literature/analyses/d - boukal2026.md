# Boukal, Janský & Palanský (2026) Decomposing the Revenue Implications of the Global Minimum Tax
International Tax and Public Finance


## Abstract (p. 1)

- Develop a methodology to decompose the tax revenue impact of the GMT into several components and quantify its potential impact on profit shifting
- Apply methodology to a dataset of 34 thousand country-multinational observations combined from corporate tax returns, financial statements, and global country-by-country reports of all MNEs active in Slovakia in 2020
- Find that the GMT has potential to decrease profit shifting by most MNEs, which are on average likely to pay higher ETRs in most countries worldwide post-reform
- Slovak corporate tax revenues estimated to increase by 4%, with half due to minimum top-up taxes and half due to CIT on profits no longer shifted out of the country
- Expect GMT to target 49% of previously shifted profits

JEL: H25, H26

## 1 Introduction (p. 2)

- The GMT reform was agreed in 2021 by more than 135 countries (90%+ of world GDP), effective in the EU and several other countries since January 2024
- Described as "the most significant change in the taxation of multinationals in decades, if not a century"
- Context: sizeable and increasing tax avoidance (Clausing 2016; Alvarez-Martinez et al. 2021; Garcia-Bernardo & Jansky 2024; Riedel 2018) and a decade of unsuccessful government attempts to reduce it (Clausing 2020; Wier & Zucman 2022; Garcia-Bernardo et al. 2022)
- Effects of GMT on firms and governments are far from clear due to: (i) reform complexity, (ii) uncertainty about firm and government responses, (iii) unavailability of suitable data
- Paper addresses these by: (i) component-by-component decomposition separating top-up taxes from CIT changes induced by profit shifting responses; (ii) transparent assumptions about firm and government behaviour based on best evidence; (iii) rich administrative data combining tax returns, financial statements, and CbCR

Three research questions:
- By how much will Slovakia's tax revenues increase?
- How much through new top-up taxes vs existing CIT?
- How much of currently shifted profits will the reform target?

Methodology to decompose the impact into seven components, differing along three dimensions: (i) whether from existing CIT or (ii) from new minimum top-up taxes (and which specific top-up), and (iii) in which country

Dataset: 34 thousand country-multinational observations from corporate tax returns, financial statements, and global CbCR of all MNEs active in Slovakia in 2020

Important clarification: the study is not limited to Slovak context narrowly; it analyses the universe of MNEs with operations in Slovakia, almost exclusively headquartered abroad, making results "reflective of global profit shifting dynamics rather than purely domestic developments"

## 1 Introduction, continued (p. 3)

First main finding: Slovak corporate tax revenues will increase by 4.3 to 5.3%, or EUR 117 to 144 million
- Estimated by aggregating from bottom up: for each affected MNE, how profits and taxes likely affected in each country by each top-up tax
- Absent implementation by the US and China (and assuming suspension of top-up taxation vis-a-vis those jurisdictions), estimated revenue declines by 7-8% (EUR 8-12 million) relative to baseline
- Revenue gain is higher than previous estimates for Slovakia by Barake et al. (2022) and Brun et al. (2025), who report no or comparatively smaller estimates using OECD aggregate CbCR data
- Replicating Barake et al. (2022) method with 2020 aggregated data yields only EUR 16 million
- Comparison of assumptions suggests "superior nature of administrative micro-level data and country-specific approaches"

Second main finding: an accounting identity decomposes revenue effects into seven components (three top-up channels and four CIT channels)
- Extends and complements prior estimates (Hugger et al. 2024; Clemens et al. 2022) with component-by-component breakdown from micro-linked administrative data
- Data allow approximation of key GloBE ETR adjustments (substance-based carve-outs, loss carry-forwards) more precisely, rather than assuming uniform 15%

Revenue increase composition:
- 45% (EUR 53 million) from two types of top-up taxes (domestic and third country) on undertaxed profits
  - 38 pp (EUR 44 million) is tax on profits currently reported in Slovakia, newly taxed at 15%
  - 7 pp (EUR 9 million) is tax on profits reported in countries not likely to implement GMT but participating, allowing Slovakia to top-up
- 73 MNEs likely to pay domestic top-up in Slovakia
- Just 3 companies likely to pay 75% of estimated top-up tax revenue; top 13 companies pay 94%
- Consistent with concentration findings (Martin et al. 2023; Wier & Hayley 2022)
- Largest contributors headquartered in Austria, UK, South Korea; active in manufacturing, financial/insurance services, real estate, retail

## (p. 4)

- The other 55% to 70% (EUR 64-101 million) of tax revenue increase from CIT collected due to reduced profit shifting out of Slovakia
- Decomposed into three parts:
  - 34-41% from taxing profit no longer shifted out because other countries apply domestic top-up
  - 14-29% from countries introducing headquarter top-up taxes (IIR) on their MNEs
  - 7-10% from countries applying third-country top-up taxes on non-implementing countries
- Tax loss from reduced profit shifting into Slovakia (due to Slovakia's own top-up taxes) estimated as negligible, mainly affecting top-up tax amounts, not CIT

Third main finding: GMT will target 38-51% of previously shifted profits
- Extends literature which has not accounted for these effects (Bachas et al. 2025)
- Majority of profits shifted out of Slovakia to several EU member states, both before and after reform
- Profit shifting likely to continue after reform mainly driven by "those few European Union member states in spite of them implementing the reform"
- For countries with higher ETRs, impact comparatively lower
- GMT targets only 31% of previously shifted profits to Slovakia itself

Important caveat: "it is too soon to provide a definitive forecast of the global minimum tax's impact"
- Approach does not address that firm behaviour is likely to change due to changing incentive landscape
- Conceptual framework reflects only potential revenue gains, assuming MNEs report profits in same jurisdictions as before and pay new minimum ETRs
- Ultimate impact depends on additional firm-level behavioural responses not captured

Literature contributions:
- Expanding body of research on GMT (empirical: Barake et al. 2022; Alex et al. 2022; Gomez et al. 2023; OECD 2020; theoretical: Devereux 2023; Hebous et al. 2024; Niels 2022; Guttorm & Stahler 2024)
- Heterogeneity of MNE profit shifting (Bachas et al. 2025; Garcia-Bernardo et al. 2022; Wier & Hayley 2022)
- Among first to use administrative CbCR at firm-level, complementing Germany (Fuest et al. 2025; Fuest et al. 2022), Italy (Bratta et al. 2024), US (Nessa et al. 2022)

## 2 Institutional context: global minimum tax (p. 5-6)

Key rules of the reform (OECD Pillar II), focused on three top-up taxes imposing minimum 15% on MNE profits:

1. Domestic top-up tax (QDMTT): allows a country to impose top-up on subsidiary's profits taxed below 15% minimum
2. Headquarter top-up tax (IIR): allows headquarter country to impose top-up on parent in respect of low-taxed affiliate income
3. Third-country top-up tax (UTPR): allows a country to impose tax on local entity by reference to low-taxed profits of affiliate in another jurisdiction

These apply in the listed order (priority cascade)

Footnote 1: There is a fourth top-up tax, the subject-to-tax rule (STTR), applying a 9% minimum tax on certain payments; not analysed as unlikely to be significant for Slovakia

Figure 1 illustrates with example: MNE headquartered in Germany, low-tax subsidiary in Luxembourg, high-tax subsidiary in Slovakia; depending on which rules implemented, each of three countries can potentially receive top-up tax

Table on p. 6 shows conditions for each top-up tax to apply across Luxembourg, Germany, Slovakia

## 3 Conceptual framework and methodology (p. 6-8)

Overall decomposition in terms of tax liabilities:

Delta_T_corporate,j = T_TopUp,j + Delta_T_CIT,j  ... (Equation 1)

where T_TopUp,j is revenue from minimum top-up taxes and Delta_T_CIT,j captures change in CIT revenue from behavioural responses of MNEs

Pre-reform profit reporting (Equation 2):
pi_reported,j,before = pi_real,j,before - pi_out,j,before + pi_in,j,before
(following Bilicka et al. 2024)

Negative correlation between CIT rate and outward profit shifting established since Hines and Rice (1994)

Pre-reform tax revenue (Equation 3):
T_j,before = pi_reported,j,before x tau_income,j

Post-reform: MNEs likely respond with changes in profit shifting behaviour
- pi_out,j,after reduced due to top-up taxes in other countries k of all three types (domestic, headquarter, third country)
- pi_into,j,after reduced because Slovak top-up taxes increase effective taxation in Slovakia
- Overall effect on reported profits ambiguous

Seven-component decomposition (Equation 4):
Delta_T_corporate,j =
+ domestic top-up tax (undertaxed profits in j, not HQ'd there)
+ headquarter top-up tax (undertaxed profits in j, HQ'd there)
+ third country top-up tax (share of undertaxed profits in k)
+ income tax domestic (less shifting out due to domestic top-ups elsewhere)
+ income tax headquarter (less shifting out due to HQ top-ups elsewhere)
+ income tax third country (less shifting out due to third-country top-ups elsewhere)
- income tax decrease (less shifting into Slovakia)

## 3.1 Computation of the seven revenue components (p. 8-10)

Step-by-step explanation:

(i) Baseline profits and taxes
- For every MNE-country pair: observed profits, accrued taxes, assets, employees
- Compute ETR and identify undertaxed profits whenever ETR < 15%
- ETR_i,j = Tax Accrued_i,j / GloBE Income_i,j  ... (Equation 5)
- In Slovakia: tax accrued from CbCR enriched by corporate tax returns; outside Slovakia: CbCR accrued income taxes

(ii) Constructing the GloBE income
- Start from profit before tax, adjust at jurisdiction level:
  (a) subtract loss carry-forwards
  (b) subtract R&D and patent-box provisions (treated as qualifying items in baseline)
  (c) apply substance-based carve-outs equal to 5% of payroll and 5% of tangible assets
- This yields the tax base for top-up taxation
- Details in Appendix A.3, cross-referenced to Slovak Tax Code in Appendix A.4

(iii) Top-up taxes (Components 1-3)
- Top-up rate = (15% - ETR), applied to GloBE income of undertaxed profits
- Allocation depends on taxing rights per each top-up tax
- Domestic top-up computation illustrated in Equation 6:
  T_corporate&domestic,i,j = pi_reported&undertaxed&domestic x (15% - tau_i,topup,j)
- For foreign-jurisdiction components, GloBE base approximated by profit before tax reduced by substance-based carve-outs (payroll and tangible assets) because tax-return items not available for all countries

(iv) Profit shifting adjustments (Components 4-7)
- GMT changes incentives to shift profits; shifting does not disappear but is reduced
- Responsiveness estimated using semi-elasticity specification
- Quadratic specification of Hines and Rice (1994), highlighted by Dowd et al. (2017), recently applied by Garcia-Bernardo & Jansky (2024):
  log(pi_ij) = beta_0 + beta_1 log(K_ij) + beta_2 log(L_ij) + beta_3(1-tau_j) + beta_4(1-tau_j)^2 + beta_chi x chi + epsilon  ... (Equation 7)
- K_ij and L_ij are capital and labour from Cobb-Douglas; operationalised by tangible assets from CbCR and wages (employees x country wage from ILO)
- tau_j proxied by ETRs
- "Profit shifting" denotes excess profits in a jurisdiction beyond what is explained by assets and employment, attributable to tax-rate differentials; not restricted to classic tax havens, applies to any jurisdiction where ETR < 15% or profits appear in excess of local substance

(v) Top-up taxes affected by change in profit shifting (Components 1-2, effect on 7)
- Timing important: for domestic and HQ top-ups, top-up revenue calculated after profit shifting adjustment, since taxable base in Slovakia directly affected

(vi) Aggregation
- Each component converted to revenue using Slovakia's CIT rate of 21%, then aggregated across all MNEs
- Sum of seven components = total revenue effect

## 3.2 Assumptions on GMT implementation and behavioural responses (p. 10-12)

Implementing countries: reform already legislated and in force 2024; all EU member states (including Slovakia), Australia, Canada, Japan, Norway, South Korea, UK
- Assume all three rules apply for these countries

UTPR debate: scrutiny about potential violation of international law (Avi-Yonah 2023); concerns about US position and retaliatory measures
- "Side-by-side" solution agreed by G7 in June 2025 effectively exempted US and US-HQ'd MNEs from UTPR (U.S. Department of the Treasury, 2025)

Third-country top-up (Component 3): non-extraterritorial interpretation adopted (Cui 2024)
- Not all undertaxed profits in third countries taxable; only share corresponding to wages and assets in implementing countries
- Conservatively assume only 25% of potential revenue from Component 3 realised
- Components 3 and 6 are mutually exclusive; remaining 75% shifted into Component 6

Scenario excluding US and China: US and Chinese MNEs, plus undertaxed profits booked in either country, excluded from Slovak revenue base; but domestic top-up implemented in other jurisdictions still applies to US and Chinese MNE profits in those implementing countries
- GILTI not treated as qualifying HQ top-up tax (conservative)

Later adoption by Switzerland and Singapore: "unlikely to materially change our results" because mainly reallocates taxing rights; due to non-extraterritorial interpretation and 25% cap, scope for reallocation already small

Unobservable GloBE reductions (tax-exempt dividends, accelerated depreciation via deferred-tax adjustments): not implemented in baseline denominator; omission can lower measured ETR and increase top-ups, especially for domestic component
- But loss carry-forwards and R&D/patent-box adjustments are implemented

Footnote 2: Slovakia deferred UTPR application until 31 December 2029

Key assumptions:
- Governments have not changed statutory CIT rates in response (estimates focus on 2024)
- No change in other provisions (tax credits, investment subsidies) that might serve as loopholes
- MNEs will not exploit existing loopholes more intensively
- Reform changes profit shifting incentives; both shifting out and into Slovakia likely affected
- Countries implementing reform become less attractive as destination for profit shifting

## 4 Data (p. 12-15)

Three datasets combined for MNEs active in Slovakia:
(i) Country-by-country reporting data
(ii) Tax returns
(iii) Financial statements
Complemented with business and beneficial ownership registers

CbCR: filed by all large MNEs with activities in Slovakia; result of 2016 regulation requiring MNEs with consolidated global group revenue >= EUR 750 million to report activities in all countries of operation
- Prepared at level of ultimate parent entity, filed with HQ tax authority, shared with other countries
- Financial data aggregated at MNE-jurisdiction level (not subsidiary level)
- Non-financial information (addresses, TINs) disclosed for all subsidiaries, enabling enrichment with tax returns and financial statements at subsidiary level in Slovakia

Merging: link Slovak subsidiaries to groups using register IDs/TINs and CbCR subsidiary listings; where tax-return and financial-statement values differ, covered taxes and loss-related items taken from tax returns (precedence), payroll and tangible assets from financial statements; outside Slovakia, rely on CbCR; negative or anomalous accrued-tax entries flagged and reconciled using Slovak tax returns
- Important because "using negative values directly from CbCR can lead to a significant overestimation of the global minimum tax effect" (Appendix Table 5)

Domestic components computed from Slovak microdata rather than CbCR aggregates
- GloBE base at subsidiary level: profit before tax (financial statements) minus loss carry-forwards and qualifying items (R&D, patent-box) from tax returns, minus substance-based carve-outs (payroll and tangible assets from financial statements)

Coverage: year 2020, 787 MNEs active in Slovakia, paired with Slovak tax returns and financial statements
- Total: 34,494 MNE-jurisdiction pairs and 1,827 Slovak subsidiaries
- Some items incomplete: employment annex present for 67% of subsidiaries; payroll, tangible assets occasionally missing
- Financial statements matched to tax returns for 95% of subsidiaries

CbCR double-counting issue (Blouin & Robinson 2020): three channels (equity income, intra-company dividends, partnership income)
- Because large part of double-counting relates to HQ country profits (not affiliates), and OECD guidelines updated for 2020 data, no adjustments made
- Primary exposure: upward bias to HQ profits, overstating HQ top-up; but HQ top-up not reported (confidentiality) and treated as zero, so does not inflate results
- Domestic top-up and outward-shifting results built from matched subsidiary-level admin data, where parent-level double counting does not arise

Slovakia as case study: CIT rate of 21% since 2017, closely mirrors OECD average of EU members (median 21%, non-weighted mean 20.9% in 2024) (OECD 2024)
- Footnote 3: as of 1 January 2025, CIT rate increased to 24% for large corporations with revenues above EUR 5 million

Year 2020 context:
- First full year of EU Anti-Tax Avoidance Directive (ATAD) I/II in Slovakia
- COVID-19 pandemic
- ATAD channels tend to increase reported profitability and raise ETRs; pandemic forces may have reduced profitability
- Net bias for 2020 is ambiguous; authors interpret baseline as "reflective of the underlying relationships relevant for global minimum tax"

Descriptive statistics (Table 1, p. 14-15): detailed statistics across all three datasets, split by all observations and MNE activities in Slovakia

- Close to 30% of profits globally reported below 5% ETR, disproportionate to real activity (assets, employees, revenues)
- 52% of global profits taxed below 15%; only 22% in Slovakia taxed below 15%
- Slovak provisions allowing ETR below statutory 21%: accelerated depreciation, R&D super-deduction, patent box, loss carry-forward

## 5 Results (p. 21-25)

Slovak corporate tax revenues estimated to increase by 4.3 to 5.3%, or EUR 117 to 144 million

Using most conservative estimate: roughly half of 4.3% increase due to top-up taxes levied by Slovakia, other half due to decreased profit shifting out of Slovakia
- Slovakia collects EUR 53 million (45% of total net effect) from top-up taxes
- EUR 64 million (55%) from CIT on profits newly reported in Slovakia rather than being shifted
- Tax loss from reduced inward shifting negligible, mainly affects top-up amounts not CIT

Footnote 4: preferred estimate of domestic top-up tax impact is EUR 47.3 million (Table 5, panel c), assuming substance-based carve-outs of 5%/5% (post-transition); transitional 8%/10% carve-outs decrease estimate by 7% to EUR 44 million; without carve-outs, top-up would raise EUR 64.6 million

Domestic top-up tax and Income tax domestic are the two most important components

Component 1 (Domestic top-up tax): EUR 44 million
- Significantly larger than Barake et al. (2022) estimate of zero (using 2017 aggregated OECD CbCR)
- Replicating their method with 2020 aggregated data: EUR 16 million (just over a third of the authors' estimate)
- Around 200 MNE subsidiaries in Slovakia with ETR < 15%, of which 73 MNEs would pay domestic top-up
- Highly concentrated: 3 MNEs pay EUR 35 million (75%); top 13 pay 95% of total
- Largest share paid by companies HQ'd in Austria and UK
- Activities in financial/insurance services, real estate, manufacturing

Footnote 5: without behavioural response, static domestic top-up estimate would be EUR 47 million; with profit shifting adjustment, reduced to EUR 44 million; majority of companies shift profits out of Slovakia (top-up unaffected at EUR 24 million); remaining EUR 23 million attributed to companies shifting profits into Slovakia, reduced by EUR 3 million post-reform to EUR 20 million

Component 2 (HQ top-up tax): cannot estimate due to confidentiality; likely only few Slovakia-HQ'd MNEs; assumed zero

Component 3 (Third-country top-up): EUR 9 million

Profit shifting: estimated EUR 760 million shifted out of Slovakia in 2020; GMT potential to reduce by 49% to EUR 386 million
- Considered lower bound of longer-term impact as only 2024 adopters included
- 49% decomposed into three components corresponding to the three types of top-up taxes applied elsewhere
- Largest share from Income tax domestic, only EUR 5 million smaller than domestic top-up introduction effect
- Other two effects comparatively lower; attributed to majority of MNEs having affiliates in EU member states that adopted reform

Changes in global distribution of profit shifting (Figure 6, Table 8 in appendix): larger share stops after reform mainly due to domestic top-up responses; but opposite trend for HQ and third-country components

Sensitivity: 38% to 51% range for reduction in profit shifting

Footnote 6: three profit shifting methods compared (semi-elasticity quadratic baseline, semi-elasticity logarithmic, misalignment); misalignment shows large amount shifted to high-ETR countries (Germany, Austria, France, US); suggests MNEs shift profits "for other motivations than achieving a lower overall effective tax rate"

EU significance in profit shifting: particularly Germany, Belgium, Luxembourg, Netherlands, Ireland; also substantial outflows to US, Switzerland, Hong Kong, Singapore, BVI
- "profit shifting is unlikely to be completely eliminated by the reform"
- Particularly true for outflows to jurisdictions with relatively high CIT rates (e.g. Germany)

US and China exclusion scenario (Table 2): reduces Slovak tax revenues by EUR 8-12 million (7-8% of overall effect)

Inward profit shifting: reform targets 31% of shifting into Slovakia (vs 49% out); reduction in CIT revenue negligible (EUR 0.002 million); robustness shows up to EUR 13.9 million possible reduction, but compensated by higher amounts in other components

Robustness over time: using Czech administrative data (Boukal et al. 2025) for comparison across 2020-2022; for quadratic semi-elasticity, reduction of 54-58% between 2020-2022; other methods suggest 66-67% and 41-50%

## 5.1 Investment responses to the GMT (p. 25-26)

- Direct effect on Slovak government revenue positive, but broader evaluation requires second-round responses by firms and governments
- Higher effective taxation may dampen investment incentives at margin, even if employment responses could be neutral or positive longer run (Brun et al. 2025)
- GMT disables some tax deductions from base, raising forward-looking ETRs (Hebous et al. 2024; Eckhard and Schjelderup 2023)

Investment effect methodology (following Hebous et al. 2024):
Delta_I / I approximately equal to -epsilon_I x (Delta_tau / (1 - tau))
- Elasticity range epsilon_I in [0.5, 1.5] (benchmark 1.0), from meta-analyses of de Mooij and Ederveen (2008) and Feld and Heckemeyer (2011)

Table 3 illustrative scenarios:
- Statutory increase 19% to 22% (no GMT): benchmark investment decline 3.70%
- No qualifying deductions, ETR 10% to 15%: benchmark decline 5.56%
- Partial deductions (R&D/patent box), 10% to 13%: benchmark decline 3.33%
- Up to 8% under more extreme assumptions

- "economically meaningful, they remain smaller in magnitude than the static revenue gains from top-up taxes and reduced profit shifting"
- Abstract from outward investment relocation; illustrative sensitivity ranges, not forecasts

Government side: if governments respond by cutting statutory rates, expanding preferential regimes, or offering new credits, tax competition pressures may re-emerge (Eckhard and Schjelderup 2023)
- G7 June 2025 discussions illustrate this dynamic (debates over treatment of non-refundable tax credits)
- Substance-based carve-outs can also encourage firms to increase local activity (Guttorm & Stahler 2024)

## 6 Conclusion (p. 26-27)

- GMT could increase Slovakia's CIT revenues by 4%
- Main contribution: decomposing this number and quantifying role of profit shifting
- Slightly over half from minimum top-up taxes in Slovakia; other half from newly collected CIT on profits currently shifted out
- Less than half of currently shifted profits affected; most shifting continues to several European countries that implemented reform
- "profit shifting will be far from eliminated by the reform"
- Results depend on implementation across countries, including non-application by US or China

- "too soon for a final verdict"; only being implemented as analysis carried out in 2024; will take years before medium- and long-term effects and data available
- Conceptual decomposition can serve as "springboard for future re-estimation using data on other countries and when data become available for 2024 onward"

## Appendix A.1: Estimation of tax revenue changes (p. 27-30)

Detailed formulas for each component:

Income tax domestic (Component 4): Equation 8
- Share of profits shifted from Slovakia to countries k calculated as ratio of profits shifted from Slovakia to total shifted profits from all countries j (Equation 9)
- tau_income,SK uses ratio of post- to pre-reform tax differential multiplied by constrained ETR in [0.15, 0.21] (Equation 10)
- Linear response deliberately assumed (conservative; convex costs of shifting would imply even less shifting in reality)

Income tax headquarter (Component 5): Equation 11
- Same approach, different group of country-subsidiaries k; only if top-up not already collected by domestic top-up

Third country top-up (Component 3): Equations 12-14
- Slovakia's share from undertaxed profits in country k calculated as ratio of real economic activity (50% employees, 50% assets) in Slovakia to total across all countries

Inward shifting (Components 1-2 and 7): Equations 15-19
- Same behavioural responses proposed for shifting into Slovakia
- Reduction of undertaxed profits reported in Slovakia and reduction of CIT on shifted profits

## Appendix A.2: Estimation of profit shifting with robustness (p. 31)

Semi-elasticity estimation (Table 6):
- Linear specification: -0.89 (comparable to Beer et al. 2020 average of -1 and Heckemeyer & Overesch 2017 average of -0.8)
- Quadratic specification: substantially larger; reducing statutory rate from 5% to 4% raises reported profits by 6.1% (vs Fuest et al. 2025 minimum of -4.7 and Dowd et al. 2017 estimate of 4.7%)
- For Slovakia-relevant scenario (15% to 14%): increase in reported profits of approximately 2.1%

Quadratic preferred because: (i) consistently applied in recent literature (Dowd et al. 2017; Fuest et al. 2025; Garcia-Bernardo & Jansky 2024) ensuring comparability; (ii) provides most conservative estimates for Slovak tax rate range

Logarithmic specification (Equation 20): follows Garcia-Bernardo & Jansky (2024) with offset parameter t = 0.0014

Misalignment method (Equation 21): difference between share of profits and share of economic activity (proxied by unrelated party sales, tangible assets, employment, wages)

## Appendix A.3: Calculation of GloBE income (p. 32-33)

Tax base for GloBE ETR (Equation 22):
TaxBase_SK,i = Profit_SK,i - Tax Deductions_SK,i - Carve-outs_SK,i - Exempt Entities_SK,i

Tax deductions (Equation 23):
- 1 x R&D expenses + 0.5 x patent box deductibles + 1 x carry-loss forward
- R&D and patent box categorised as "Qualified Refundable Tax Credits" in this design (more benevolent than standard; not currently the case in Slovakia; loss carry-forward provision is applicable)

Substance-based carve-outs (Equation 24):
- 5% of assets + 5% of payroll (long-run; transitional: 8% assets and 10% payroll for 10 years)
- Assets = long-term tangible assets from financial statements (differs from CbCR "Assets" which includes inventories)
- Payroll from financial statements; for CbCR outside Slovakia, number of employees x average wage from ILO

## Appendix A.4: GloBE-relevant features of Slovak corporate tax system (p. 33)

- Loss carry-forwards: losses from 2020 onward may be carried forward up to 5 tax periods, capped at 50% of tax base per period; pre-2019 losses follow older 4-year even-share regime
- R&D super-deduction under Section 30c of the Income Tax Act: applied at 100% since 2022
- Patent box under Section 13a/13b: exempts 50% of qualifying IP income (royalties from software developed by taxpayer; under conditions, part of income from product sales using protected inventions)
