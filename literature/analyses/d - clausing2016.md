# Clausing (2016) The Effect of Profit Shifting on the Corporate Tax Base in the United States and Beyond
National Tax Journal, 69(4), 905–934


## p. 905 – Abstract & Introduction

Paper estimates the revenue cost of profit shifting for the US using BEA (Bureau of Economic Analysis) survey data on US multinationals, 1983–2012. Main finding: profit shifting costs the US government between $77 billion and $111 billion in corporate tax revenue by 2012. Revenue losses have increased substantially in recent years. Also extends the analysis to other countries, finding that corporate tax base erosion is a large problem in countries without low tax rates.

Introduction sets up the stakes:
- Reduced revenues must be offset by higher taxes elsewhere, lower spending, or increased deficits – "none of these possibilities is particularly attractive"
- Beyond revenue: BEPS also affects integrity of the tax system
- MNEs "adroitly and aggressively respond to differential tax treatment, changing the geographic location of both economic activity and profits"
- Tax competition pressures suggest that international tax system design needs updating in face of globalisation (points to Clausing 2016a and Dietsch/Rixen 2016 for broader discussion)



## p. 906 – Introduction continued + Prior Work (start)

Corporate tax base erosion has distributional consequences:
- The corporate tax falls largely on capital/shareholders, making it a more progressive tax instrument than other major revenue sources
- Much capital income goes untaxed at the individual level because "a majority of such income is held in non-taxable form" – so corporate tax plays an essential role in taxing capital income (which is far more concentrated at upper income levels than labour income)
- Rosenthal and Austin (2016): only 24% of C-Corporation equity is held in taxable accounts in 2015; Burman and Clausing (2016) find similar results
- Gravelle and Hungerford (2011): majority of individual passive income in the US is held in tax-exempt form through pensions, retirement accounts, life insurance annuities, and non-profits

Key quantitative claims from the paper's own analysis:
- Revenue cost to the US from profit shifting has been increasing steadily, reaching $77–$111 billion by 2012, over 30 percent of US corporate income tax revenues
- In dollar terms, revenue losses have increased approximately 250 percent since 2004
- For the world as a whole (including the US), revenue losses may exceed $280 billion in 2012

Paper's results are consistent with: Dowd/Landefeld/Moore (2016), OECD (2015), Crivelli/Keen/de Mooij (2015), de Mooij (2015), de Mooij and Ederveen (2008), de Mooij (2005). Clausing notes that some work using financial statement data (especially from Europe) suggests the profit shifting problem may be shrinking, but argues this work is based on incomplete data (footnote 4 elaborates on this – see below under p. 908).

Structure of the paper: Section II = prior work review; Section III = estimates for the US; Section IV = speculative extension to other countries; Section V = policy implications.



## p. 907 – Prior Work

Literature review on profit shifting:
- Foundational review: Hines (1999)
- Subsequent reviews by de Mooij and co-authors (de Mooij and Ederveen, 2003; de Mooij, 2005; de Mooij and Ederveen, 2008) confirm that income shifting is large and increasing
- Clausing's own prior work: tax sensitivity of transfer prices (Clausing 2001, 2003, 2006) and consequences for US government revenues (Clausing 2009, 2011)

OECD (2015) Action 11 findings:
- Annual net tax revenue loss from tax planning estimated at $100–$240 billion
- OECD compares its estimates with IMF, JCT (Joint Committee on Taxation), and others

Crivelli, Keen, and de Mooij (2015) – three IMF economists:
- Base erosion problems are large in developing countries and likely more important as a share of GDP there than in OECD countries
- Short-run estimates: OECD countries lose $207 billion (0.23% of GDP); developing countries lose $105 billion (0.84% of GDP)
- Long-run estimates: $509 billion for OECD countries (0.6% of GDP); $213 billion for developing countries (1.7% of GDP)

Keightley and Stupak (2015):
- Uses BEA survey data + IMF + UNCTAD FDI data
- Views the problem through several lenses, all consistent with large profit shifting

Stylized facts on the scale of the problem:
- Share of income reported in foreign countries by US MNEs has been "steadily increasing"
- Income booked in low-tax countries is "implausibly high by any reasonable metric"
- Gravelle (2015) reports: US affiliate profits were 645% of Bermuda's GDP and 547% of the Cayman Islands' GDP in 2004 – by 2010 this increased to 1,614% for Bermuda and 2,065% for the Caymans
- US MNEs have accumulated over $2 trillion in permanently reinvested earnings in low-tax locations, over $1 trillion of which is held in cash (see Kleinbard 2015; footnote 6 notes that these funds are often held in US financial institutions but firms cannot return cash to shareholders without incurring US corporate tax liabilities upon repatriation)



## p. 908 – Prior Work continued

JCT estimates the cost of deferral (i.e. the tax expenditure associated with allowing deferral of the US tax on foreign income until repatriation) at $83.4 billion for 2014; OMB estimates somewhat lower at $61.7 billion in 2014.

Zucman (2014, 2015): uses balance of payments data to conclude that profit shifting to low-tax jurisdictions is reducing US corporate taxes by approximately 20 percent, or about $130 billion annually.

The problem extends beyond US firms – analyses using Orbis data on European firms still find "substantial magnitudes of income shifting" even without full visibility into tax haven affiliates (OECD 2015 and others).

Key data limitation flagged here (important for methodology and for understanding debates in the literature):
- Only limited data are available on operations in low-tax jurisdictions
- Establishing the counterfactual level of profit absent profit shifting is inherently difficult
- OECD (2015) notes existing data sources are "far from ideal" and describes the difficulty of using financial reporting data to make inferences about profit shifting behaviour
- Orbis data in particular: "data are particularly likely to be missing for tax haven countries" – few if any observations of affiliates in tax havens that include the relevant data fields
- Footnote 8: Cobham and Loretz (2014) document that Orbis coverage is particularly weak or nonexistent for tax havens and less developed countries. Dowd, Landefeld, and Moore (2016, p. 3) note it "can be difficult to get information on subsidiaries incorporated in some tax havens, such as Bermuda and the Cayman Islands"
- Clausing's own estimates suggest 82% of profit shifting by US MNEs is destined for just seven tax haven locations – so studies that exclude these observations miss most of the action



## p. 909 – Prior Work continued + start of Section III

OECD (2015) highlights BEA data (used in this paper) and US tax return data as "examples of best practices in data collection for analyzing base erosion and profit shifting."

Dowd, Landefeld, and Moore (2016) – using US tax return data 2002–2010:
- Find a nonlinear tax response: far more responsiveness at lower tax rates than at higher ones
- Tax semi-elasticities: -4.7 at corporate tax rates of 5 percent; -0.6 at tax rates of 30 percent

Lower estimates in the literature:
- Heckemeyer and Overesch (2013): meta-analysis of income shifting studies; "best prediction" tax semi-elasticity of corporate profits = -0.8. But their meta-analysis relies on 25 studies, the "vast majority of which use financial data that are not well-suited to studying the question at hand"
- Dharmapala (2014): argues income shifting problem is likely diminishing based in part on the Heckemeyer/Overesch survey. He acknowledges Orbis data may be "solely responsible" for lower estimates in recent literature. He does note the inconsistency between these findings and the stylized facts regarding income shifting.
- Footnote 9: Dharmapala and others note that corporate tax revenues are a small share of revenue for most developed countries, and that revenues have been relatively stable despite claims of increasing BEPS. But Zucman (2014, p. 133) argues this is because corporate profits have been rising as a share of national income: "from about 9 percent in the 1980s ... to about 14 percent in 2010–2013"
- Footnote 10: Clausing notes that estimates using firm-level fixed effects "may be a lower bound on income shifting behaviour" even ignoring the substantial flaws of Orbis data

Clausing's estimates are consistent with OECD (2015), Crivelli/Keen/de Mooij (2015), Keightley and Stupak (2015), Dowd/Landefeld/Moore (2016), Zucman (2014, 2015) and many earlier studies. BEA survey data are "more suited to capturing the profit shifting problem than many other data sources."



## p. 910–911 – Section III: The Magnitude of Income Shifting – A. Data and Regression Analysis

BEA does annual surveys of US-based MNEs and their foreign affiliates. These data show a large discrepancy between the physical operations of US MNE affiliates abroad and the locations in which they report income.

Figure 1: Top Gross Income and Direct Investment Earnings Countries, Affiliates of US MNEs, 2012:
- Shows top 9 locations of US MNE affiliate gross profits in 2012
- Gross income = net income + foreign tax payments
- Seven of the top nine countries have effective tax rates under 5 percent in 2012: Netherlands, Ireland, Luxembourg, Bermuda, Switzerland, Singapore, and UK Caribbean Islands (including the Caymans)
- Effective tax rates calculated as foreign income taxes / gross income of all affiliates in a given country
- These seven countries account for 50.1% of all foreign profits and 52.3% of all direct investment earnings
- But they account for only 5% of all foreign employment of such firms
- Their combined population is less than that of Spain or California

Critique of the gross income data series:
- Includes "income from equity investments," some of which are counted more than once if there are tiers of ownership within the same country (double-counting problem)
- Alternative series: BEA direct investment earnings – excludes all income from equity investments, thereby eliminating double-counting but also excluding some income, making it incomplete
- Footnote 12: Direct investment earnings data are pro-rated by the ownership share of US parents and are after-tax

Figure 1 also shows the direct investment earnings series – same seven tax haven countries are in the top nine, accounting for 52% of all foreign direct investment earnings. Confirms the pattern.

Figure 2: Top Employment Countries, Affiliates of US MNEs, 2012 – these are all large economies with big markets; none have effective tax rates below 12 percent.

Regression analysis (Table 1, pp. 912–913):
- Data: BEA surveys 1983–2012
- Dependent variables: employment, sales, property/plant/equipment (PPE), assets, gross income, direct investment earnings
- Independent variables: ln(GDP), ln(GDP per capita), ln(distance), effective tax rate
- Panel A (pooled): employment and PPE do not show statistically significant relationship with effective tax rate; sales, assets, and especially income and earnings are negatively related to effective tax rates
- Panel B (fixed effects): also includes country-specific fixed effects – assets, income, and direct investment earnings continue to show large and statistically significant negative tax effects. "A 1 percentage point increase in the effective tax rate reduces gross income by 1.9 percent"
- Consistent with literature on "a hierarchy of behavioral responses to taxation, whereby timing and financial decisions are more tax-sensitive then real decisions about levels of economic activity" (citing Saez/Slemrod/Giertz 2012, Slemrod/Bakija 2008, Auerbach/Slemrod 1997)



## p. 914–915 – Section III-B: Estimating the Revenue Consequences of Income Shifting

Method: use regression results to calculate how profit distribution would differ absent tax rate differences among countries, then attribute a fraction of hypothetically lower foreign profits to the US tax base.

Step 1: regression of affiliate profits on tax rates of foreign countries to generate semi-elasticities.

Table 2: Regressions Estimating Gross Profits, 1983–2012:
- 8 specifications (4 pooled, 4 fixed effects)
- All yield large, negative, statistically significant relationships between gross profits and effective tax rates
- Semi-elasticities range from -1.85 to -4.61
- Average = -2.92 (this is the estimate used in the main calculations)
- Footnote 14: using effective tax rate differences between foreign and US country as the independent variable would give identical estimates if the US effective tax rate is not changing over time

Discussion of specifications:
- Country FE: capture unique country characteristics but only use within-country variation in tax rates over time (not between-country variation)
- Columns (1)–(4) include different controls: macroeconomic controls (col. 2), capital/labour use controls (col. 3), both types (col. 4)
- Including many controls may underestimate tax sensitivity if income shifting itself affects employment, investment in PPE, and even GDP figures for tax havens – "thus, including these controls may underestimate the tax-sensitivity of profits"
- Estimates are "quite similar if one instead uses data on the BEA direct investment earnings series"
- Average is in line with meta-analyses of de Mooij and Ederveen (2003, 2008) and de Mooij (2005)



## p. 916–917 – Section III-B continued + Table 3

Dowd, Landefeld, Moore (2016) argue semi-elasticities are likely non-linear: -4.7 at 5% tax rate; -0.7 at 30% tax rate. Clausing ran non-linear specifications too and found higher elasticities at lower rates. To be conservative, she uses linear elasticities, which means a conservative (lower) benchmark.

Studies using financial/Orbis data find smaller elasticities, but these data neglect "the very observations that are driving the profit shifting phenomenon, affiliates operating in tax havens."

How the revenue calculation works:
- The elasticity is used to calculate what profits would be in countries of operation absent tax rate differences between foreign countries and the US
- US statutory rate = 35 percent in most years; assumes US effective rate is 5 percentage points lower (so 30% in most years)
- This lower US effective rate applies to any increased income in the US tax base

Table 3: Key Locations of Profit Shifting, 2012:
- Shows for each major tax haven: gross income reported vs. estimated gross income without shifting vs. percent of total excess income in that location
- Netherlands: $172.3bn reported; $33.0bn without shifting; 23.0% of total excess
- Ireland: $122.3bn; $23.6bn; 16.3%
- Luxembourg: $96.1bn; $15.0bn; 13.4%
- Bermuda: $79.7bn; $9.9bn; 11.5%
- Switzerland: $57.9bn; $14.6bn; 7.2%
- Singapore: $42.4bn; $10.5bn; 5.3%
- UK (Caymans): $40.9bn; $8.7bn; 5.3%
- All others under 15%: $188.6bn; $89.8bn; 16.3%
- Total under 15%: $800bn; $205bn; 98.4%
- All others with data: $267bn; $257bn; 1.6%

In 2012: profits in high-tax countries were "too low" (due to income shifting incentives) by $26 billion; profits in medium-tax (15–30%) countries were "too high" by $36 billion; profits in lowest-tax countries (under 15%) were too high by $595 billion. "Most of the profit shifting is done with respect to the lowest-tax countries."

The seven important tax havens from Figure 1 account for 82 percent of all excess income. Without income shifting, profits would instead be: $9bn in the Caymans (down from $41bn), $33bn in the Netherlands (down from $172bn), $15bn in Switzerland, $15bn in Luxembourg, $10bn in Bermuda. For comparison: profits booked in France and Germany are currently $13bn and $17bn respectively.

Fraction attribution to US tax base:
- A fraction (38.7% in 2012) of hypothetically lower foreign profits is attributed to the US tax base
- This fraction = share of intrafirm transactions that occur between foreign affiliates and the US parent, relative to all intrafirm transactions of affiliates abroad
- "This fraction itself is just a plausible benchmark"

Final scaling up: accounts for profit shifting by foreign-based MNEs out of the US, scaled by the ratio of sales of foreign-MNE affiliates in the US to sales of US-MNE affiliates abroad.

The alternative (Panel B) estimates are lower due to both elimination of double-counting AND omission of some income types – these two effects cannot be separated with available data.

Figure 3: shows increasing trend in revenue losses, 1983–2012. The upward trend is not due to increasing tax responsiveness (elasticity assumed constant) but due to:
- Total amount of foreign profits increasing dramatically – from $525bn (2004) to $1.2 trillion (2012); direct investment earnings more than doubled in eight years
- Average foreign effective tax rate has continued to fall, also contributing to income shifting incentives

Section C: Sources of Uncertainty (begins p. 919, list of six sources):

(1) Elasticity: uses average of -2.92 from eight specifications (range -1.85 to -4.61). Allowing for non-linear responses (Dowd/Landefeld/Moore 2016) would generate even larger estimates.



## p. 920–921 – Sources of Uncertainty continued

(2) Attribution of hypothetically lower foreign profits to the US tax base: fraction based on share of intrafirm transactions between affiliates and US parent. In 2012 this was 38.7%. "There is no particularly good reason to think that exactly this fraction of the excess income would be earned in the United States." The terms and nature of intrafirm transactions provide one essential method for shifting income, so the assumption is that income would be shifted across destinations in proportion to these intrafirm transactions. Footnote 15 notes this proxy could lead to an understatement of US tax base erosion if a US parent transfers intellectual property to a foreign affiliate (since that increases foreign-to-foreign affiliate transactions).

(3) Scaling up for foreign MNE income shifting out of the US: based on ratio of sales of affiliates of foreign-based MNEs in the US to sales of affiliates of US-based MNEs abroad. In 2012: affiliates of foreign-based MNEs had $4.09 trillion in sales; foreign affiliates of US parents had $6.98 trillion in sales – so scaling up by 58.5%. This implies affiliates of foreign-parent firms operating in the US are generating "slightly over a third of the total income shifting out of the US tax base." Still, "some evidence that foreign multinationals are more able to use interest stripping to move income out of the US tax base" (citing US Department of the Treasury, 2007). No clear direction of bias.

(4) Double-counting in BEA gross income data: "income from equity investments" counted more than once if there are tiers of ownership. Cannot be corrected accurately with existing data. Alternative (direct investment earnings) series avoids this but misses some income. The two alternative estimates therefore likely bracket the truth: first may overstate, second may understate.

(5) Assumes US effective tax rate that would apply to increased tax base = 5 percentage points below statutory rate (so 30%). If statutory rate used, revenue losses would be higher; if lower effective rate used, revenue losses would be lower. Also assumes little residual tax collected by the US on foreign income earned in low-tax jurisdictions – "this assumption is consistent with the evidence on this question" (footnote 17: Altshuler and Grubert (2013) report that using 2006 data, only $32 billion is collected on all foreign source income, amounting to less than 4 percent of foreign income).

(6) The analysis does not capture methods of tax avoidance that reduce worldwide taxable income (e.g. indirect financing structures to maximise interest deductions, as in Mintz and Weichenrieder 2010; "stateless income" where firms create income not taxed in any jurisdiction, as in Kleinbard 2011a). It is unclear how much of this income would appear in the BEA data. This source of uncertainty suggests the estimates of Table 4 may be underestimates.

Summary of bias direction: item (1) = conservative (uses lower average elasticity vs. nonlinear); items (2), (3), (5) = no clear direction; item (6) = suggests underestimation; item (4) = first set may overstate, second may understate.



## p. 922–925 – Section IV: A Speculative Extension to the World

Scaling up to the world: no comparable publicly available BEA-type survey data for most countries. Instead, uses Forbes Global 2000 list (world's 2000 largest corporations) – indicates headquarters location and worldwide profits.

Table 5: Major Countries with Forbes Global 2000 Firms, 2012 Data:
- 62 countries are home to Forbes Global 2000 firms, but 25 countries account for 95% of profits
- US: 33.33% of Global 2000 profits; UK: 6.37%; Japan: 5.41%; China: 8.52%; Germany: 3.35%; France: 3.77%; etc.
- 72% of world profits headquartered in OECD countries; 92% in OECD + big economies (Brazil, China, India, Indonesia, Russia, Saudi Arabia, South Africa)
- About 4% headquartered in six major haven destinations: Bermuda, Caymans, Channel Islands, Cyprus, Hong Kong, Singapore (though Hong Kong and Singapore could also be classed as big economies)

Figure 4 shows the distribution.

Five-step methodology for global extension:

Step 1: assume all MNEs have affiliates in two types of countries – low-tax (effective rate < 15%; these are the 17 countries identified as destinations for artificial income shifting for US MNEs, accounting for 98% of estimated profit shifting) and high-tax countries.

Step 2: for non-low-tax headquarters countries, assume their share of income booked in low-tax countries is proportionate to the share of US MNE foreign income booked in low-tax countries. E.g. Germany = 3.3% of Global 2000 profits vs. US = 33.3%, so German MNEs have about 10% of the US level of profits in low-tax countries, or roughly $80 billion. Footnote 19 notes this assumption is most reasonable if foreign MNEs are similarly tax-responsive as US counterparts, but acknowledges some countries have more compliant tax cultures (e.g. Japan) or more stringent CFC laws.

Step 3: assume foreign country tax rates are 5 percentage points less than their statutory rates (inclusive of sub-federal taxation). E.g. Japan combined statutory rate = 39.5% in 2012, so assumed effective rate = 34.5%. Footnote 20 notes this is arbitrary and depends on provisions of particular country tax codes.

Step 4: model profit shifting between headquarters countries and the 17 low-tax countries (average effective tax rate = 6.6%). Apply semi-elasticity of 2.92 to calculate magnitude of profit shifting. Footnote 21 notes that non-linear elasticities from Dowd/Landefeld/Moore (2016) would suggest using a higher elasticity (since average tax rate of low-tax countries = 6.6%, suggesting semi-elasticity in excess of 4). Using smaller elasticity of 2 instead: total excess income in low-tax countries would be $739 billion, generating revenue loss for the group of $192 billion.

Step 5: estimate total excess income booked in low-tax countries globally. For the US in 2012: of the $800 billion booked in the 17 low-tax countries, about $545 billion would not be there absent the tax rate difference. For the group of big headquarters countries: total = $1,076 billion.

This $1,076 billion is then assigned to higher-tax headquarters countries based on their share of GDP within the higher-tax group. E.g. Germany has 6.3% of the higher-tax headquarters countries' total GDP, so Germany recoups 6.3% of $1,076 billion = additional ~$67.8 billion in tax base. German revenue gain = German effective tax rate × additional base = $17 billion.

Under these calculations, the US recoups 29% of excess $1,076 billion, generating a revenue loss of $94 billion (using 30% effective rate). This falls between the two estimates of $77bn and $111bn in Table 4 (note: different assumption used here – GDP shares rather than affiliate transaction shares). Footnote 22 explains this difference and notes that some of the excess income may belong in countries outside the sample, including less developed countries.

## p. 927 – Section V: Policy Options – A. The OECD/G20 BEPS Process

Both prior literature and present analysis confirm profit shifting is eroding the corporate tax base broadly. OECD undertook the BEPS project in response to concerns raised at G8 and G20 meetings. Final BEPS reports issued October 2015, totalling nearly 2,000 pages.

Positive assessment of BEPS:
- "These attempts to better connect taxable profits to economic activity are helpful, and the suggested measures are likely to incrementally curb profit shifting activity"
- The OECD/G20 process is commendable for pushing international cooperation

But significant limitations:
- Country adoption "likely to be uneven and incomplete" since OECD recommendations are not binding
- Fundamental problem: establishing the source of income for truly globally integrated firms is "an arbitrary exercise" – MNEs earn more than their component parts would have earned alone, so determining where the additional profit resides is inherently arbitrary
- Intangible nature of intellectual property makes it even harder to establish source of economic value
- MNEs have every incentive to redirect profits to low-tax locations; "The tax departments of major multinational firms are widely thought of as profit centers, and armies of accountants and lawyers work to develop innovative tax minimization strategies, often several steps ahead of government treasuries"
- Footnote 24: reference to O'Keefe and Jones (Fortune, October 22, 2015) on how Uber plays the tax shell game as an example of the intangible IP problem

Steps toward country-by-country reporting are valuable, but political will to close loopholes enabling pervasive profit shifting is uncertain.



## p. 928–930 – Section V-B: More Fundamental Reforms

1. Worldwide Consolidation:
- Discussed in JCT (2011), favoured by Kleinbard (2011b) and Avi-Yonah (2013)
- MNE would be required to consolidate all income across parent and affiliates; all income taxed currently; foreign tax credit retained
- JCT (2011, pp. 100–101) summarizes: "The U.S. group would include on its return the foreign corporation's items of income, gain, deduction and loss, the character of such items would be preserved, and the foreign tax credit would be retained ... under the consolidation approach, losses of foreign subsidiaries would be included on the U.S. return ... the consolidation regime would apply only to U.S. corporate shareholders of foreign subsidiaries"
- Benefits: less tax-motivated shifting of economic activity or book income; fewer concerns about inefficient capital allocation; no "trapped cash" problem
- Concerns: competitiveness concerns if firms face rising foreign tax burdens; stress on definition of residence (though Avi-Yonah 2013 and Kleinbard 2011b argue a residence definition based on true location of "mind and management" would be effective); need for anti-inversion measures (Clausing 2014, Kleinbard 2014, Shay 2014)
- Could be adopted unilaterally, though Avi-Yonah (2013) recommends multilateral approach
- Footnote 25: for firms with excess tax credits there would still be some incentive to earn income in low-tax countries

2. Formulary Apportionment:
- Worldwide income assigned to individual countries based on a formula reflecting real economic activities
- Common suggestion: three-factor formula (sales, assets, payroll); Avi-Yonah and Clausing (2008) suggest single-factor formula based on destination of sales
- Essential advantage: "provides a concrete way for determining the source of international income that is not sensitive to arbitrary features of corporate behavior such as a firm's declared state of residence, their organizational structure, or their transfer pricing decisions"
- Real economic activities (the formula factors) are far less tax-sensitive than financial/accounting decisions – this is clearly shown in Table 1 data
- With formulary apportionment, firms have no incentive to shift paper profits or change tax residence since liabilities are based on real activities
- Remaining concern under three-factor formula: incentive to locate real economic activity in low-tax countries (raises capital allocation concerns) – "somewhat less of a concern under a sales-based formula"
- US experience: uses formulary apportionment for state corporate taxes; formula factors (payroll, assets, sales) are "not particularly tax-sensitive" (Clausing 2016b for in-depth analysis)
- Footnote 27: formulary apportionment assumes MNE has taxable presence (nexus) in the locations where it has employment, assets, and sales
- Footnote 31: "There is a natural incentive for countries to follow suit" once some adopt FA, since remaining separate accounting (SA) countries would lose tax base to FA countries
- Formulary profit-split method also mentioned as a related approach: tax base = normal rate of return on expenses, with residual profits allocated by sales-based formula
- If all countries adopted formulary apportionment: "there would be few concerns about competitiveness" since MNEs would be taxed based on real economic activities (production and sales)



## p. 930–931 – Section VI: Conclusion

Summary of findings:
- Using BEA survey data, profit shifting likely costs the US government $77–$111 billion annually by 2012
- In 2012: foreign affiliates of US MNEs booked $800 billion of income (75% of total foreign income) in countries with effective tax rates less than 15%; average effective tax rate in those countries = 6.6%
- Revenue costs are increasing over time due to: (a) increasing magnitude of profits booked in low-tax countries; (b) continued corporate tax rate reductions abroad
- Advantage of BEA data: comprehensive survey including operations in many tax haven countries, unlike studies relying on financial data
- Author attempted to err on side of caution; provides alternative estimates

Global extension (speculative):
- Profit shifting to low-tax countries may be costing headquarters countries without low tax rates approximately $280 billion annually (including the US)
- World is larger than set of headquarters countries analysed: less developed countries are ignored, so the global scope of profit shifting is understated
- Crivelli/Keen/de Mooij (2015): profit shifting is likely especially pressing in less developed countries relative to size and affluence of their economies; they may also have insufficient institutional capacity for enforcement

Policy conclusions:
- OECD/G20 BEPS process has promising elements and is a "useful step forward"
- But fundamental difficulties remain in establishing the source of income in a globally integrated economy
- "More fundamental reforms, such as worldwide consolidation or formulary apportionment, are likely to be more successful at stemming corporate tax base erosion in an era of globally integrated business and agile taxpayers"
