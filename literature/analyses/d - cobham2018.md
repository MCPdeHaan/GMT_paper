# Cobham & Janský (2018) Global Distribution of Revenue Loss from Corporate Tax Avoidance: Re-Estimation and Country Results
Journal of International Development, 30(2), 206–232

p. 206

- Paper re-estimates the work of Crivelli, De Mooij and Keen (2016), which is described as the most comprehensive study of global revenue losses from corporate tax avoidance at the time
- Crivelli et al. (2016) used panel data for 173 countries over 33 years to explore the magnitude and nature of international fiscal externalities, specifically spillovers from tax policy decisions in individual jurisdictions onto others
- Cobham and Janský introduce higher-quality revenue data from the ICTD-WIDER Government Revenue Database (GRD)
- Whereas Crivelli et al. report results for two country groups only (OECD and non-OECD), this paper presents country-level results
- Headline finding: global revenue losses of around US$500 billion annually (compared to nearly US$650 billion in Crivelli et al.)
- "the greatest intensity of losses occurs in low-income and lower middle-income countries and across sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America and the Caribbean and South Asia"
- Published in Journal of International Development, open access under CC BY-NC-SA licence

p. 207

- Crivelli et al. (2016) developed a new method enabling a distinction between spillover effects through real investment decisions and through avoidance techniques
- Their approach: estimate an equation with corporate tax base as the dependent variable, with "tax haven" corporate tax rates as one of the independent variables
- The avoidance associated with tax havens can be assessed by "turning off" in the model the effects on tax bases operating through that channel
- Crivelli et al. estimated global revenue losses at around US$650 billion annually, of which around one-third relate to developing countries
- Intensity as share of GDP somewhat higher in developing countries compared with OECD economies
- Cobham and Gibson (2016) combined this with data on the relatively greater reliance on corporate tax revenue in developing countries: estimated losses are around 2-3% of total tax revenue in OECD countries, but 6-13% in developing countries
- Concerns over the revenue statistics: Crivelli et al. use CIT revenue and statutory tax rate data from the private data set of the IMF's Fiscal Affairs Department
- The ICTD-WIDER GRD combines data from several major international databases and a new compilation from IMF Article IV and country staff reports
- A further data issue relates to the definition and treatment of "tax havens"

Three main issues the paper addresses:
- (1) Re-estimate original findings and test robustness to higher-quality revenue data and alternative effective tax rate series. Headline estimate: ~US$500 billion globally. Majority of reduction in total estimate relates to OECD countries, meaning even greater differential in intensity of losses suffered by lower-income countries
- (2) Experiment with alternative approach to defining "tax havens"
- (3) Disaggregate results to country level to demonstrate heterogeneity within OECD/non-OECD groups

- The paper is thus "not only a re-estimation of an earlier econometric study but also an extension of global and regional comparative analysis"
- Country-level estimates may shed light on political economy of international corporate tax, e.g. why some countries may be more or less likely to support reforms

p. 208

Literature on Revenue Loss Estimates (Section 2)

- Focus on revenue loss estimates of base erosion and profit shifting (BEPS) for lower-income countries
- "persuasive quantification of the revenue at stake through cross-border tax avoidance has proved elusive" (quoting Crivelli et al. 2016)
- Fuest and Riedel (2012) provide a critique of many earlier estimates
- After the global financial crisis, increased public and policy attention on tax avoidance of multinationals
- Clausing (2016) finds profit shifting by US-headquartered multinationals likely cost the US between US$77 billion and US$111 billion by 2012, having increased substantially over time
- Cobham and Janský (2017) use the same data set to estimate global revenue losses in a range from US$130 billion to around US$200 billion
- Both papers highlight limitations of using data on activities of multinationals from one major economy only but argue this is preferable to current alternatives
- Orbis, the leading database of company balance sheets, has been shown to have "such severe and systematic limitations through the under-representation of both lower-income countries and major profit-shifting hubs that its use for global analysis cannot be supported" (finding supported by Cobham and Loretz, 2014)
- Dharmapala (2014) and Hines (2014) discuss relatively low values of estimated sensitivities of reported profits to tax rate differences, but without addressing possible bias from Orbis data
- Hines (2014) argues estimates of 2 or 4% "probably overstate the potential tax revenue to be had by eradicating BEPS," though focused on developed countries
- Zucman (2014) estimates profit-shifting to low-tax jurisdictions reduces the tax bill of US-owned companies by about 20%; US-owned companies would have paid $200 billion in additional taxes in 2013 if the effective tax rate had not fallen from 30 to 20% between 1998 and 2013
- Clausing (2009) estimates US$60 billion lost to profit shifting by US MNEs in 2004, representing 35% of US federal CIT collections

p. 209

- Some literature suggests revenue costs might be particularly high for developing countries (overview by Reuter, 2012; more recently Johannesen and Pirttilä, 2016)
- Fuest, Hebous and Riedel (2011) find evidence of larger profit shifting for developing countries: effect of host country corporate tax rate on debt ratio of multinational affiliates in developing economies is positive and larger than the same effect for affiliates in developed economies
- Johannesen, Tørsløv and Wier (2017), arguing Orbis has recently increased coverage in less-developed countries, find profit shifting is more prevalent in less-developed countries. They argue this may explain why many developing countries opt for low corporate tax rates despite urgent revenue needs
- Neither Fuest et al. (2011) nor Johannesen et al. (2017) extend their results to tax revenue loss estimates
- Reynolds and Wier (2016) do extend to revenue but only for South Africa, finding profit-shifting lowers the tax-GDP ratio by 0.05 percentage points. In contrast, Cobham and Janský find South Africa suffers losses of between 1.6 and 1.9% of GDP
- Cobham and Loretz (2014) showed a clear pattern of misalignment of profits and economic activity to benefit of a small number of profit-haven jurisdictions and to detriment of lower-income countries
- Cobham and Janský (2017) used BEA data on US-headquartered multinationals to reveal major misalignments for middle-income and other countries
- UNCTAD (2015) used national-level data on returns to FDI to estimate revenue losses from profit shifting through investment conduit jurisdictions: lower-income countries found to lose around US$100 billion a year through this one channel
- OECD (2015) using firm-level Orbis data estimated global loss of US$100 billion to US$240 billion in 2014, or 4 to 10% of all CIT revenues (up to US$2.1 trillion over 2005-2014)

The spillover equation from Crivelli et al. (2016):

b_it = λ b_{i,t-1} + φ τ_it + γ W_{-it} τ_{-it} + ξ X_it + α_i + μ_t + ε_it   (Equation 1)

where:
- b_it = corporate tax base in country i in time t
- τ_it = domestic tax rate
- W_{-it} = weighted average of tax rates in countries j ≠ i (various versions defined)
- X_it = vector of controls
- α_i = country-specific effects
- μ_t = time-specific effects

p. 210

- Equation (1) extended to consider spillovers by country size ("GDP-weighted" statutory tax rates); by tax haven list ("haven-weighted" rates, unweighted average of tax rates only in haven countries); by geographic proximity ("distance-weighted" rates); and alternatively with average effective tax rates (AETRs)

Revenue loss estimation:

Short-run revenue loss (% of GDP) for country i in period t from profit shifting through tax havens:

L_it = τ_it * φ_hat * (τ_it - W^h τ_{-it})   (Equation 2)

where φ_hat is the estimated coefficient on the tax term (imposing equality of coefficients on own and spillover effects, separately for OECD and non-OECD groups) and W^h τ_{-it} is the haven-weighted average tax rate

Long-run estimates:

LL_it = [τ_it * φ_hat * (τ_it - W^h τ_{-it})] / (1 - λ_hat)   (Equation 3)

where λ_hat is the estimated coefficient on the lagged corporate tax base

- The loss answers the question: "how much revenue would country i gain, if opportunities for profit shifting were to be eliminated by raising the average rate in tax havens to the level of its own?"
- Key assumption in the case of haven-weighted rates: "it is equally easy to use any haven for profit-shifting" but "impossible to use other countries for it"
- This highlights a tension: long haven lists give unwarranted weight to small unimportant havens; short haven lists may exclude some actual havens

Section 3: Data

- The authors received the original data set and code from the IMF researchers
- Crivelli et al. (2016) cover 173 countries over 1980-2013; with alternative data, this paper arrives at an unbalanced data set of 49 to 120 countries over the same period
- They make equivalent changes to the data, including interpolation of tax rate series for years with missing tax rates

p. 211

- Resource-rich countries are excluded as dependent variables (their tax bases have distinct drivers and reflect distinct tax design choices), but their tax rates are included in constructing average tax rates used as explanatory variables
- Resource-rich countries defined as: Bahrain, Chad, Republic of Congo, Iran, Kazakhstan, Kuwait, Libya, Mexico, Nigeria, Norway, Oman, Russia, Saudi Arabia, Syria, Trinidad and Tobago, UAE, Venezuela and Yemen
- Footnote 2 notes the grounds for this specific group are not clear; Algeria, Angola, Australia, Brunei, Ecuador, Equatorial Guinea and Mongolia are not included despite having substantial natural resources

Three areas where data differ:

(1) Revenue data from ICTD-WIDER GRD
- GRD created in response to the absence of a consistent, high-quality, public data source for revenues (Prichard, Cobham and Goodall, 2014)
- Papers at the GRD launch confirmed issues with quality of data in IMF studies and failure to replicate results of several papers by IMF Fiscal Affairs Department researchers
- GRD strengths: better coverage for some countries and years; explicitly states a hierarchy for data sources with preference for those with more years and more detailed disaggregation; explicitly informs users when estimates seem problematic

(2) Alternative data on AETRs
- Crivelli et al. use AETR data from Abbas and Klemm (2013) available only for 43 countries over 1996-2007
- Paper considers other estimates from Orbis data (Cobham and Loretz, 2014) and BEA data on US-headquartered multinationals (Cobham and Janský, 2017)

(3) Alternative "tax haven" definitions
- IMF authors use list from Gravelle (2013), based on observed phenomena from a US perspective
- Alternative: list of six major profit misalignment jurisdictions from Cobham and Janský (2017): Netherlands, Ireland, Luxembourg, Bermuda, Switzerland and Singapore

Footnote 1: CEPII data on distances used for inverse-distance-weighted average CIT rates. Some country distances unavailable; assigned neighbouring countries instead: Montenegro (Bosnia and Herzegovina), Kosovo (Macedonia), San Marino (Italy). World Bank WDI data used instead of IMF WEO for GDP when extending the sample.

p. 212

- The alternative data-driven tax haven list (Cobham and Janský, 2017) has disadvantages (e.g. excludes countries often considered tax havens such as Cayman Islands) and advantages (only six of the most important havens for multinationals, which may account for most corporate activities by tax havens globally)
- Footnote 4: Dowd, Landefeld and Moore (2017) using IRS firm-level filings identify a near-identical list for the US: Bermuda, Cayman Islands, Ireland, Luxembourg, Netherlands and Switzerland. Unfortunately Cayman data not in the BEA's public disaggregation
- The two lists (Gravelle and Cobham and Janský) are used as alternatives and robustness checks for each other
- Additional alternatives for future research could include measures based on the secrecy score component of the Tax Justice Network's Financial Secrecy Index (detailed in Cobham, Janský and Meinzer, 2015)
- The problem: there is "no accepted definition or objectively verifiable criteria for tax havenry"
- Ireland and the Netherlands demonstrate that jurisdictions with a fair degree of financial transparency can offer targeted means to achieve effective tax rates much lower than statutory levels
- Average effective rates may offer a way to determine the "haven" list, or alternatively aggregated data from multinationals' country-by-country reporting if eventually made public

- Figure 2 shows strikingly different pattern in terms of GDP shares: low-income and lower middle-income countries face consistently heaviest losses, but these are less extreme and less volatile over time

- Key methodological point: the revenue cost estimate is independent of actual CIT revenue of a country in a given year; it depends only on the CIT rate (and GDP level for absolute values). The implied change in corporate tax bases depends for each country-year on the CIT rate relative to the haven-weighted average

p. 218

- The decreasing difference between "haven" and "non-haven" tax rates (Figure 3) drives most of the results over time (decreasing cost of BEPS as share of GDP)
- In dollar terms, the steady rise of world GDP over three decades acts as counterweight, resulting in degree of stability in estimated costs over time

- This might be viewed as evidence that lowering corporate tax rates is effective against avoidance
- However, narrower studies (Cobham and Janský 2017, Clausing 2016) provide evidence that profit shifting has grown strongly even as effective tax rates have fallen sharply
- Cobham and Janský (2017) document effective tax rates for US-headquartered multinationals of 0-5% in major misalignment jurisdictions, compared with 15-20% in the USA and other economies on average
- The issue may be "an artefact of the methodology, relying on differentials in statutory rates while incentives are driven instead by effective rates"
- This is an important caveat: the methodology's reliance on statutory rates may understate the problem if effective rates are what actually drive profit shifting

Section 6: Conclusion

- Broadly confirms Crivelli et al. (2016) findings
- Establishes broad robustness to use of alternative government revenue data
- Less success with changes to "tax haven" definition and AETRs
- Paper is "an open-source robustness check of Crivelli et al. (2016), using the widely available GRD"
- Publishes full detail of country-level revenue loss estimates for preferred model

- "There is clearly space to develop a methodological approach that goes beyond statutory tax rates and responds more closely to the actual incentives that multinationals face for profit shifting"
- Estimated tax loss with preferred GRD data: around US$500 billion (vs US$650 billion in original)
- Intensity of losses substantially greater in low-income and lower middle-income countries; in sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America and the Caribbean, and South Asia
- Two important conclusions: (1) lower-income countries suffer more intense corporate tax avoidance (even before considering their greater reliance on CIT revenues); (2) substantial variations among countries by income and region

Policy implications:
- G77 policy makers should consider pursuing an internationally representative tax body for rule changes benefiting those with greatest losses
- "The real breakthrough, however, is likely to come only when multinationals' country-by-country reporting data are made public, and the full extent and nature of the misalignment between profits and the location of real economic activity are laid bare"
