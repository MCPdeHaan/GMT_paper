# Cobham, Faccio, Garcia-Bernardo, Janský, Kadet & Picciotto (2022) A Practical Proposal to End Corporate Tax Abuse: METR, a Minimum Effective Tax Rate for Multinationals
Global Policy, 13(1), 18–33

## p. 18 — Abstract and Section 1: Concerted Action to Break the Logjam

Paper context: written against the backdrop of the stalled BEPS 2.0 negotiations, published in Global Policy (2022). Authors are affiliated with Tax Justice Network, Nottingham University, Charles University Prague, University of Washington, and Lancaster University.

Core problem statement in abstract:
- Profit shifting explosion since 1990s has cost hundreds of billions in tax revenues annually
- Reform efforts have "failed to deliver measurable progress" on aligning MNEs' taxable profits with location of real economic activity
- Countries have committed to a global minimum effective tax rate but design remains contested

Proposed solution: METR (minimum effective tax rate)
- Can be applied by any willing country, whether home or host of MNEs
- Compatible with existing tax treaties and does not require treaty changes
- Non-discriminatory, so also compatible with other international obligations
- Can be introduced unilaterally
- Economic modelling shows "major revenue gains for participating countries"
- Adoption would "contribute to, rather than impede, momentum for a more comprehensive multilateral agreement"

Section 1 — the logjam framing:

Context of the international reform effort:
- Launched after the great financial crash, about a decade before writing
- Profit shifting exploded from the 1990s onwards (Cobham & Janský, 2019)
- Introduction of CbCR gave tax authorities "unprecedented insights into profit shifting"
- Limited aggregate CbCR data puts global tax losses at around US$1 trillion per year (Garcia-Bernardo & Janský, 2021; Tax Justice Network, 2020a)
- International rules were "designed a century ago"
- Logjam is due "mainly to the perceived need to achieve an illusory worldwide consensus among states"

OECD process: considerable progress within BEPS/Inclusive Framework, but process resembles "a complex game, in which participants reach partial agreements while also taking initiatives that tilt the negotiating table." Negotiating table dominated by biggest OECD member states, resulting in unconstructive bargaining rather than genuine global solutions.

In October 2020 the OECD Secretariat delivered two complex blueprints (OECD, 2020a; 2020b) approved by the Inclusive Framework. Key political issues remain unresolved, technical details remain contested.

Authors argue: the most promising avenue is a minimum international corporate tax, but it "must be formulated in a way that can be effective and widely accepted as fair." Should enable states to tax MNEs on both inbound and outbound investment, on equal footing with domestic firms.

Proposal for coalition: "a group of interested countries, supported by regional groupings and international organisations" should take the lead; the group should include lower-income countries (footnote 1: "we use this term because it is widely accepted, although in several ways unsatisfactory") as well as G20 and other major states. Fresh impetus came from Biden administration proposals in April 2021.

The METR's stated aims (reflecting already agreed objectives of the BEPS process):
1. allocation of MNE income based on "where their activities occur and value is created" (G20, 2013)
2. taxation of net profits on a basis "that all countries, companies and the general public can accept as equitable"
3. a methodology that is "easy to administer and transparent"

Further advantages the authors claim:
4. does not require changes to tax treaties
5. allocates taxation rights fairly to all states instead of the "proposed complex and inequitable 'ordering' rules"
6. can be introduced under domestic laws
7. creates "a dynamic for international convergence"

Revenue estimate: depending on the minimum rate and country coverage, the METR "would raise tens or hundreds of billions of dollars in additional revenues around the world, by curbing the incentives and the ability of MNEs to shift their profits."



## p. 19 — Section 1 continued; Section 2: The OECD Blueprints begins

Policy implications box (summary of paper's claims):
- METR could solve global corporate tax reform problems
- METR offers simpler alternative to OECD proposal and would recover more revenue
- METR could be adopted by a "coalition of the willing" without global treaty changes
- METR would "eliminate much of the unearned profits currently booked in 'tax havens'"

Section 2 — OECD Blueprints:

Historical context:
- BEPS original initiative (2013–2015) was "the last great attempt to defend the arm's length principle," which prices intra-group transactions as if between independent parties at market prices
- "The realization that a more fundamental rethink was needed led to the renewal of negotiations"
- BEPS 2.0 took two starting points that broke with its predecessor, forming two pillars:
  - Pillar 1: addressed ease of profit shifting by going beyond arm's length and permanent establishment, apportioning some global profits based on location of real economic activity
  - Pillar 2: addressed incentives for profit shifting by proposing a global minimum tax to "put a limit on the race to the bottom"

Despite promising start, process stalled. Blueprints (OECD, 2020a; 2020b) "contained positive elements and resolved many key technical problems" but were overly complex, especially for lower-income country tax authorities. Root cause: "the scheme attempts to achieve consensus amongst all governments involved by combining incompatible approaches."



## p. 20 — Section 2.1: Pillar 1; Section 2.2: Pillar 2

Section 2.1 — Pillar 1:

- Pillar 1 introduces formulaic element ("Amount A"): assigns some of each MNE's global profits to the jurisdictions where the MNE's sales take place
- The proportion is "fixed by percentages decided politically, applied as one-size-fits-all"
- Bulk of MNE income would still be allocated under "complex and flawed transactional transfer pricing methods" (footnote 2 notes that for careful analysis of remaining flaws in OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines after BEPS, see Andrus and Collier 2017)
- Blueprint proposes intricate reconciliation rules to avoid double counting and eliminate double taxation
- Relies on self-assessment filing with a "lead tax administration" (usually home country), which validates calculations and allocations (OECD, 2020b, para. 717)
- Access by other tax authorities depends on exchange system similar to CbCR arrangements
- Conflicts settled by binding "determination panels"
- Such a system could only be introduced by amending all tax treaties, primarily via "a multilateral treaty signed and ratified by all states, with no opt-outs, which would be unprecedented" (OECD, 2020a, para. 839)
- Scope limited to MNEs above EUR 750m turnover, and further limited to "automated digital services" or "consumer-facing business" — so new taxing right applies to roughly 2,300 of the 8,000 MNEs above the threshold (OECD, 2020a, p. 63)
- Problem: OECD's own Action 1 reports (2015 and 2018) showed that "digitalisation has affected the whole economy and any solution should not be ring-fenced"
- Revenue impact: OECD's own impact assessment (OECD, 2020c, p. 61) showed likely benefits from Amount A would be "less than 1 per cent of global CIT revenues," in return for locking in flawed transfer pricing methods
- US April 2021 proposals: envisaged targeting only the largest and most profitable MNEs, "perhaps as few as one hundred" (footnote 3: derived from lead US negotiator Grinberg 2020) — easier to implement through intergovernmental agreements, but "clearly be only a stop-gap"
- African group proposed alternative simplified method with broader scope (footnote 4: tax base = applying MNE's global profit margin to local sales revenues; size threshold reduced to EUR 250m, ATAF 2021), but this would face implementation problems under tax treaties

Section 2.2 — Pillar 2:

- A global minimum tax has fewer implementation problems since it can be devised to be compatible with tax treaties
- US tax reforms of 2017 already included minimum tax measures: GILTI (for US-based MNEs) and BEAT (mainly for foreign-based MNEs)
- OECD Pillar 2 proposals are along similar lines; UK and Australia also introduced diverted profits taxes unilaterally
- Pillar 2 proposal (GLOBE = Global Anti-Base Erosion Tax) "has been deeply compromised by the attempt to combine it with Pillar 1 into a single multilateral system"
- First problem: aiming for consensus weakened ambition. Some states (tax havens, conduit jurisdictions) still oppose concept
- Key claim: a "coalition of willing states could design strong measures which could also, if done well, generate a positive momentum for broader multilateral measures to follow"



## p. 21 — Section 2.2 continued; Section 3: The METR Proposal

Section 2.2 continued:

Pillar 2 is "excessively technically complex." It rightly starts from the reality that MNE groups operate as "unitary, centrally directed organisations in which each entity performs its assigned functions."

Two main provisions:
- IIR (Income Inclusion Rule): allows MNE's ultimate parent country (or intermediate parent under a "top-down" approach) to apply a top-up tax to income of foreign constituent entities to ensure minimum ETR. Applied by jurisdiction. (footnote 5: IIR based on same principles as CFC rules, first introduced by US in 1962; but unlike CFC rules, Pillar 2 would give priority to home countries rather than allowing unilateral credits for source taxes)
- UTPR (Undertaxed Payment Rule): allows source countries to apply top-up tax to undertaxed profits of an MNE group via adjustments to taxable income of an affiliate in the source country

Critical flaw: blueprint gives priority to IIR; UTPR is only a "secondary back-up rule." Justification given was "largely driven by simplicity and lower compliance costs" (OECD, 2020b, p. 17), which the authors call "unprincipled and unfair."
- Source country has "rightful claim" to tax low-taxed income since much of it results from payments that directly reduced its tax base
- "Granting priority to the IIR is a transfer of tax revenue from lower-income countries, which are generally source states, to OECD members, which are generally the home countries of MNEs"

As partial acknowledgement of this injustice, Pillar 2 also provides a STTR (Subject to Tax Rule) to protect source country tax base, but this adds complexity and requires amendment of all relevant tax treaties. A fourth provision, the SOR (Switch-Over Rule), also requires treaty changes. Authors expect that a group of willing countries would proceed with the flawed GLOBE, leaving additional components for later treaty changes.

Summary critique: instead of designing minimum tax measures that any willing state could implement, "the blueprint proposes a highly complex set of interacting rules that would give priority to the rich home countries of MNEs, with only a partial concession to host countries (the STTR), which is hard to implement and dependent on changes to all tax treaties."

Section 3 — The METR Proposal:

METR builds on both blueprints, especially Pillar 2. Aims:
- "much greater simplicity"
- eliminates unfair prioritisation of home countries
- eliminates obstacle of requiring worldwide treaty changes

Step 1: identify the MNE based on definition of a corporate group for financial accounting purposes (as in OECD, 2020b). No reason to limit to only the largest MNEs (though small/medium enterprise threshold is possible). Aim is to end unfair competitive advantage all MNEs have over domestic firms through low ETRs achieved by attributing profits to low/zero-tax jurisdictions.

Step 2: calculate the ETR for each jurisdiction: covered taxes divided by relevant profits (footnote 6: described in blueprint as "adjusted covered taxes / adjusted GLOBE income," OECD, 2020b, p. 99; determining ETR on jurisdictional basis is "far superior to the global blending adopted for the GILTI," though may still permit some avoidance due to aggregation).
- Methodology from Pillar 2 blueprint for both covered taxes and profits before tax is adopted
- Profit/loss before tax of each constituent entity (CE), prepared under same accounting standard as parent, with agreed adjustments (OECD, 2020b, chapter 3.3)
- Blueprint details for covered taxes (chapter 3.2) and attribution to jurisdictions including "stateless" entities (chapter 3.4) are followed (footnote 7: ETR ideally should be calculated per CE to reduce sheltering via profit merging; jurisdictional blending agreed is "acceptable," but CbCR standard could be aligned with METR reporting requirements; at present CbCRs are "not designed or indeed permitted to be used for tax purposes")

Divergence from blueprint: instead of carve-outs, METR identifies "undertaxed profits" by jurisdiction and calculates "non-effectively taxed profits" (NETs) — the share of profits that have not economically been taxed.

- Aggregate for each MNE = "MNE NETs"
- MNE NETs are allocated under a "formulaic apportionment rule" (FAR), a test of substance similar to the formulaic substance-based carve-out in GLOBE (OECD, 2020b, chapter 4)
- FAR uses three factors: tangible assets, employees, and sales (footnote 8: discussed in more detail in Cobham et al., 2021)
- Instead of being a carve-out from profits before ETR calculation, this becomes the principle for allocating taxing rights over MNE NETs, "eliminates the need to treat home and source countries unequally through rule prioritisation"



## p. 22 — Section 3 continued; Section 4: Economic Analysis begins

How NETs are calculated:
- Compare actual ETR to set minimum ETR
- If actual ETR >= minimum ETR: no undertaxed profit in that jurisdiction
- If actual ETR < minimum ETR: jurisdiction's NETs = jurisdiction's profits before tax multiplied by the quotient of (minimum ETR minus actual ETR) divided by minimum ETR
- These jurisdiction-level NETs are combined to arrive at MNE NETs

Allocation of taxing rights:
- Rights to tax in respect of MNE NETs allocated to all countries where MNE has a taxable presence, "irrespective of whether their ETRs are below, equal to, or above the minimum ETR" (footnote 9: throwback rule ensures all MNE NETs are allocated to countries able to tax them — any sales, employees or assets in countries where MNE has no taxable presence are omitted from allocation formula denominator)
- Countries free to maintain their own corporate tax rate and apply it to their share of MNE NETs — they can apply either the minimum ETR or their own domestic rate, whether higher or lower
- Key insight: "any country would be free to maintain a low tax rate, because the METR would ensure that it is applied only to the share of MNE profits reflecting real activities in the country"
- METR specifically targets "harmful tax competition: the offering of low tax rates for the artificial reporting of profits out of line with economic substance"

Relationship to GLOBE and international law:
- METR is "a modification of the GLOBE, dispensing with the STTR"
- Compatible with international tax rules
- "Formulated as a single non-discriminatory rule," more likely to be accepted as compatible with EU law (footnote 10: some countries may need to change domestic law to introduce a SOR; existing CFC rules can co-exist with METR since CFC taxes are counted when calculating undertaxed profits, so no double taxation results)
- Home countries: include MNE NETs in taxable profits of parent company
- Host countries: deny deductions or make other adjustments related to intra-group expenditures (OECD, 2020b, paras. 519–520, and ch. 10.4)
- Since countries can each apply the rule independently, no need for priority and coordination rules from Pillar 2 blueprint — "greatly simplifying the proposal"

Advantages of integrating IIR and UTPR into a single criterion:
1. Abandons "the increasingly outdated and politically divisive distinction between residence and source countries"
2. Applying a single rule places all countries and MNEs "on an equal footing"
3. No discrimination between countries or between inbound and outbound investment — less likely to conflict with rules of international trade and investment including EU law
4. MNEs taxed at same rate as domestic companies on profits attributable to activities in the country — level playing field, compatible with constitutional principles
5. Delivers "simplicity and ease of administration" which is especially needed by lower-income country tax authorities, "as has been stressed by the Inclusive Framework"

Section 4 — Economic Analysis:

Purpose: model potential effects of competing proposals; estimate redistribution of tax base and additional collectable taxes. Analysis focuses "only on the likely direct effects on revenue" and does not quantify speculative behavioural reactions by governments or firms (footnote 11: OECD impact assessment includes some dynamic effects evaluation; for METR, factors like employees, physical assets, place of delivery for sales are location-specific so countries would compete to attract real activities instead of paper profits).

Literature on scale of profit shifting:
- Immediate direct annual revenue gains from ending profit shifting estimated at $100–240 billion (Johansson et al., 2017) or $200–300 billion (Garcia-Bernardo & Janský, 2021)
- Total gain over time including indirect gains from reducing race-to-bottom pressure: "four to six times the size" (Cobham & Janský, 2018, following Crivelli et al., 2016) = one to one and a half trillion dollars
- Consensus finding: while high-income countries lose the greatest absolute share of revenues, "lower-income countries suffer greater losses in relation to their current tax revenues" (Cobham & Janský, 2020; Garcia-Bernardo & Janský, 2021; Johannesen et al., 2020; Tax Justice Network, 2020b)
- Therefore greater alignment of profits and activity "would likely be strongly progressive internationally"

Comparison with current blueprints:
- Pillar 1 (per OECD's own analysis, OECD, 2020c): potential global revenue gain of $5–12 billion, "just 2 per cent–5 per cent of the OECD's assessment of the scale of losses"
- Pillar 2: redistributive potential depends heavily on minimum rate chosen
  - 12.5% minimum ETR: "could disproportionally affect lower-income countries" — low floor would leave incentive to shift profits for African countries with average CIT of 28%
  - Higher minimum (Biden administration proposed 21% for GILTI, then announced international minimum of at least 15% in May 2021)
  - Agreement on immediate 15% rising to 20% and perhaps 25% "could have more powerful and, importantly, more balanced effects in reducing incentives for profit shifting"
  - Low minimum rate "could exacerbate rather than ameliorate the extant inequalities in taxing rights"
  - Higher rate such as the 25% suggested by ICRICT (2019) "might be globally progressive and reinstate the tax autonomy of governments lost due to the race to the bottom"



## p. 23 — Section 4.1: Data

OECD data used for main analysis — already combines best available data using sophisticated methodologies, but complete database not made available; published data is aggregated by regional and income level groups. "This aggregated data also has some significant limitations."

OECD assessment relies on four matrices (profit, turnover, tangible assets, payroll) created from four sources (OECD, 2020c, p. 232):
1. CbCR data (in all matrices except payroll)
2. Orbis data (all four matrices)
3. OECD Analytical AMNE data (in turnover matrix), covering 59 countries
4. OECD AMNE data (in turnover and payroll matrices)

The authors raise important concerns (Eden (2020a, 2020b) also addresses some of these issues):

First concern: matrices use only values for "entities belonging to MNE sub-groups with positive profits in the jurisdiction considered" (OECD, 2020c, p. 249). But "reporting zero or negative profits is a common result of profit shifting." Excluding assets, employees, and sales of affiliates that shifted profits to tax havens "can heavily affect the redistribution of taxing rights."

Second concern: turnover matrix includes sales to both third-party and related-party entities. This distorts the analysis of where real activity takes place because it includes "the very intragroup transactions that are used to manipulate the location of taxable profits." Scale of distortion illustrated: unrelated-party sales of US MNEs in France ($147bn) are comparable to those in the Netherlands ($121bn), but related-party sales in France ($58bn) are far below those in the Netherlands ($218bn). The turnover matrix is also used to estimate tangible assets and payroll matrices, so those are likely also distorted — "likely to mean that the modelling overstates the degree of real activity in jurisdictions that procure profit shifting."

Also, turnover data is attributed to the recipient entity rather than reflecting destination-based sourcing — which is what the METR's methodology would require (using Pillar 1 sourcing rules).

Third concern: book location of tangible assets "appears to be highly distorted." Example: value of tangible assets of US MNEs in Luxembourg = $223bn, roughly equal to combined amounts in Germany ($78bn), France ($57bn), Italy ($27bn), Spain ($21bn), Russia ($19bn), and Poland ($17bn). METR would use methodology from Pillar 2 carve-out based on "depreciation of property," disallowing leased-out assets (OECD, 2020b, para. 368).

For country-level results: alternative modelling using OECD's country-level aggregate CbCR data (OECD, 2020d), following Garcia-Bernardo & Janský (2021) approach. This dataset contains profits, employees, sales, and assets of MNEs headquartered in a country, aggregated by country of residence of subsidiary. Main strength: large coverage — data on all large MNEs (consolidated revenues above EUR 750m) headquartered in countries that adopted CbCR. 2020 edition covers 195 countries (footnote 15: only 26 of ~100 CbCR-implementing countries agreed to share data publicly; only 10 disaggregated to more than 60 countries; missing data imputed using "histogram-based gradient boosting regression — a type of machine learning algorithm offering high performance and interpretability"; aggregated totals are comparable to those from OECD's linear model imputation).

Extra field created for this study: Cc = share of global foreign profits controlled by companies in country c (profits made abroad by subsidiaries of companies headquartered in country c). Two approaches to operationalise this:
1. Share of outward FDI stock from IMF CDIS dataset
2. Direct estimation using linear model combining: number of MNEs in country (Bureau van Dijk/Orbis), bank deposits (BIS, table B4), statutory tax rate (KPMG, Deloitte, PwC), effective tax rate (average in OECD data), GDP, and population — same variables used by Garcia-Bernardo & Janský (2021) to estimate domestic profits



## p. 24 — Section 4.1 continued; Section 4.2: Core Modelling

The second approach for Cc reduces weight of major conduit countries (Luxembourg, Netherlands) and increases weight of more substantive home countries (US, Japan). Model predicts Cc for OECD-reporting countries with R² of 88%; used to estimate Cc in non-reporting countries.

Section 4.2 — Core Modelling:

Main analysis starts from OECD economic evaluation (Annex 5.D, OECD, 2020c, p. 267), which aggregates data on profits, turnover, tangible assets, and payroll into groups (see Table 1 in paper: three categories — high-income countries (A–D), low- and middle-income countries (E–K), and investment hubs (L–N)).

ETR by group calculated as average of ETRs weighted by payroll vector. ETR based on cash payments in CbCR data and sub-groups with positive profits. Where effective rate was below zero, statutory CIT rate is used instead.

Five approaches compared (footnote 16: other "residence-priority" taxes exist, e.g. Clausing et al. (2020); focus here is on OECD proposal since aggregated results are similar; note that Clausing–Saez–Zucman proposal would give priority to source country taxes):
- Model 1: straight UT-FA (Unitary Taxation with Formulary Apportionment) — all taxing rights over consolidated global MNE profits reallocated to countries of operation based on formula equally weighting shares of turnover, payroll, and tangible assets:
  - Model 1a: apportioned profits taxed at statutory CIT rate
  - Model 1b: apportioned profits taxed at effective tax rate
  - These provide upper and lower bounds for UT-FA effects
  - Note: data particularly inappropriate for modelling formulary apportionment because sales data by destination is unavailable; turnover as proxy under-estimates UT-FA effects
- Model 2: UT-FA combined with minimum tax rate (varied from 0 to 35%); extra top-up tax added to reach minimum rate
- Model 3: the METR — reallocates undertaxed profits (MNE NETs) using FAR; fraction of undertaxed profits = profits multiplied by (minimum rate minus ETR) / minimum rate; taxing rights reallocated on same formulary basis; each country applies its standard rate
- Model 4: GLOBE proposal — economic impact assessment for Pillar 2 scenario 1 (OECD, 2020c, p. 82); top-up tax = undertaxed profits multiplied by (minimum rate minus ETR); fraction redistributed to ultimate parent (footnote 17: no formulaic substance-based carve-out is modelled, but OECD suggests it has only small effect; US assumed to participate, unlike OECD's own analysis which excluded US and added GILTI revenues separately); rest allocated using turnover share

Important simplifying assumptions acknowledged: formulae do not allow for losses, whereas "in reality a substantial share of companies do have losses rather than profits." No second-order/dynamic effects modelled.



## p. 25 — Section 4.2 continued: Formal Expressions

The paper provides the formal mathematical expressions for all models.

Status quo: T_ic = π_ic × τ_ic (profits times effective tax rate)

UT-FA (Model 1): T_ic = E_ic × Σ(π_ic × τ'_ic), where E_ic = (1/3)(A/ΣA) + (1/3)(S_ic/ΣS_ic) + (1/3)(P_ic/ΣP_ic) — equal weighting of tangible assets, sales/turnover, and payroll

UT-FA with minimum tax (Model 2): T_ic = E_ic × Σ(π_ic × τ'_ic) + TU_ic, where TU_ic = E_ic × Σ(π_ic × (τ'_ic - τ_m)); described as a "maximum range" estimate of what would happen if all profits were fairly apportioned and taxed

METR (Model 3): T_ic(METR) = π_ic × τ_ic + π^R_ic × τ_ic, where π^R_ic = E_ic × Σ[π_i'd × (τ_m - τ_i'd)/τ_m], summed only over subsidiaries i' resident in countries d where τ_i'd < τ_m; the reallocation of taxing rights is the share of real activity in country c multiplied by the total sum of undertaxed profits of the MNE group

OECD Pillar 2 scenario 1 (Model 4): T_ic(OECD) = π_ic × τ_ic + TU_ic; top-up tax TU_ic has two components:
- If τ_ic > τ_m: TU_ic = Σ[π_i'd × (τ_m - τ_i'd)] (full top-up allocated to country c as parent country)
- If τ_ic ≤ τ_m: TU_ic = Σ[(τ_m - τ_ic)/τ_m × π_i'd × (τ_m - τ_i'd)] + Σ[S_ic/ΣS_ic × τ_ic/τ_m × π_i'd × (τ_m - τ_i'd)] (partial to parent, rest distributed by turnover share — "simplification of the income inclusion rule and the undertaxed payments rule")

Since data is aggregated at region-income group level, average ETRs and statutory tax rates weighted by employee location are used for calculations.



## p. 26 — Section 4.3: Core Results

Approach provides "at least a comparative evaluation" — estimates subject to "major caveats about data quality" (footnote 19: important heterogeneity hidden within groups; e.g. Puerto Rico included in same group as US, so $40–60bn profit shifting from US to Puerto Rico is completely disregarded; UK attracts inward profit shifting but sees outward profit shifting from other MNEs; regional aggregation nets out profits shifted in and out).

UT-FA results (Figure 2):
- Model 1a (apportioned profits at statutory CIT rate): ~$847bn revenue gain globally — "clearly exaggerated since many statutory rates are very high"
- Model 1b (apportioned profits at effective tax rate): ~$96bn — "likely to be reasonably accurate" as a low bar; regional aggregation "appears to have a powerful effect in distorting downwards the apparent gains" (footnote 20: Tax Justice Network (2020) estimates using more disaggregated country-level data put global revenue losses at $245bn)
- Model 2 (UT-FA + minimum tax): most powerful revenue gains, exceeding a trillion dollars globally at higher minimum rates; diverges upwards from Model 1a as minimum rates increasingly exceed current CIT rates; addresses most comprehensively both the divorce of profits from real activity and the incentive to shift

Context note: full UT-FA is "not expect[ed to have] wide political support at this time," but included as a baseline to assess ambition of other proposals.

METR vs GLOBE (Model 3 vs Model 4):
- Both deliver gains between the UT-FA lower and upper bounds (Models 1b and 1a)
- Gains from residence-priority taxes (Model 4) do not exceed those from simple UT-FA at current effective tax rates (Model 1b) at effective rates below 15%
- METR exceeds Model 1b above a 10% minimum rate
- Only at minimum rates above 30% do both METR and GLOBE deliver higher revenue gains than Model 1a
- "For all the rates we model, the returns to UT-FA with a minimum tax rate remain the highest"
- Major difference: METR generates "superior revenues at all levels of minimum tax rate up to 30 per cent, ranging from $50 billion to $140 billion additional revenue gains" over the GLOBE

Distribution results (Figure 3, three panels):

For high-income countries:
- Pattern similar to global picture
- METR delivers somewhat larger gain than GLOBE until minimum rate reaches 30%
- Reason: reallocating taxing rights over undertaxed profits and taxing at statutory rate is more beneficial than GLOBE's residence-priority top-up at minimum rate

For low- and middle-income countries:
- METR outperforms GLOBE at lower minimum rates; pattern holds until minimum rate of 32.5%
- Important difference: UT-FA with current effective tax rates (Model 1b) generates higher revenues for low- and middle-income countries up to rather high minimum rates; METR generates higher revenues only above 17.5%; GLOBE only above 22.5%

For investment hubs:
- UT-FA approaches "end the main attraction of corporate tax havenry"
- GLOBE sees large proportional gains at higher minimum rates since top-up tax is redistributed to owners
- Both METR and OECD proposal deliver revenue gain to investment hubs at any minimum rate (footnote 21: OECD assumes investment hubs would not implement GLOBE and only 50% of other countries would; authors assume all countries implement since METR allocates some undertaxed profits to almost all countries including investment hubs)
- METR provides investment hubs "proportionally somewhat smaller gains" than for lower- and high-income countries — though gains could increase over time if they rebalance to encourage real activities
- In practice METR gains to investment hubs would be even lower since it allocates undertaxed income using Pillar 1 destination-based sourcing rules for sales
- GLOBE residence-priority tax delivers larger gains to investment hubs than to lower-income countries at higher minimum rates — "increasingly so at higher minimum tax rates"

Summary assessment: both UT-FA approaches provide clearest allocation of tax base to real activity; UT-FA with minimum rate would generate major gains for all groups. METR and OECD proposals appear more feasible at present.



## p. 27 — Section 4.3 continued

METR results set it apart from OECD proposal: "It provides the strongest revenue gains at minimum tax rates of 25 per cent or below for both high- and lower-income countries (this holds true up to rates of 30 per cent for the former); it generates the smallest gains for investment hubs."

Key reason for the METR's distributional advantages: "The fairer reallocation of taxing rights in the METR proposal is especially important if profit shifting is not completely eliminated by the minimum tax rate, that is, if the minimum tax rate is not high enough."

## p. 29 — Section 4.4: Country Results: METR vs OECD

Country-level modelling using more granular CbCR dataset (footnote 22: three key differences from OECD's own model — (1) method of estimating missing data; (2) ETR used: OECD uses median of CbCR, BEA data, and Tørsløv et al. (2020); BEA data is "biased upward, especially for low-tax jurisdictions" (Garcia-Bernardo & Janský, 2020); (3) IIR modelling assumption: authors assume all countries implement, while OECD assumes 0% of no-CIT countries and 50% of others; "high number of assumptions required to model the OECD proposal results from the complexity of the details").

Formal expressions at country level:

METR for country c: T_c(METR) = π_c × τ_c + π^R_c × τ'_c, where π^R_c = E_c × Σ[π_d × (τ_m - τ_d)/τ_m] summed over countries d where τ_d < τ_m; τ'_c is statutory tax rate

OECD Pillar 2 for country c: T_c(OECD) = π_c × τ_c + TU_c, where TU_c has two sources:
- From subsidiaries in low-tax jurisdictions: (τ_c/τ_m) × (C_c/ΣC_c) × Σπ_d(τ_m - τ_d) — fraction based on ownership share, scaling if own ETR below minimum
- Plus: (E_c/ΣE_c) × Σ[(τ_m - τ_e)/τ_m × (C_e/ΣC_e) × Σπ_d(τ_m - τ_d)] — fraction based on real economic activity from undertaxed profits of other companies

Figure 4 findings (scatter plots of METR vs OECD gains by GDP per capita, four panels for different minimum rates):
- "METR proposal provides higher and more balanced tax revenue gains compared with the OECD proposal, even when our modelling choices for the OECD proposal overestimate its tax revenue gains"
- For tax rates up to 25%: METR provides higher gains; generally also true at 30%
- "Even more strikingly, the METR proposal provides higher tax revenues gain for countries with lower GDP per capita, for all tax rates modelled"
- This follows directly from METR allocating gains by share of real economic activity rather than by location of MNE parent
- Jurisdictions with zero statutory tax rate would not obtain any gains from METR; they may receive some reallocated top-up tax under OECD proposal depending on implementation

Specific country comparisons at 20% minimum rate:
- US: METR $139bn vs OECD $148bn (similar)
- UK: METR $20bn vs OECD $19bn (similar)
- Brazil: METR $14bn vs OECD $6bn (substantially higher)
- Mexico: METR $11bn vs OECD $6bn
- South Africa: METR $4.6bn vs OECD $2.6bn
- Argentina: METR $2.7bn vs OECD $1.1bn
- Malaysia: METR $2.7bn vs OECD $1.9bn

At 25% minimum rate, METR yields much higher gains for lower-income countries (footnote 23: the result of higher gains for countries with high GDP per capita under OECD would reverse if OECD's own assumptions were used):
- GDP per capita below $1,000: 60% increase (from $4.8bn to $7.7bn)
- GDP per capita $1,000–3,000: 58% increase (from $13.1bn to $20.7bn)
- GDP per capita $3,000–10,000: 31% increase (from $17.7bn to $25.7bn)
- GDP per capita $10,000–40,000: 8% increase (from $32.8bn to $35.4bn)
- Only countries with the highest GDP per capita would be losers (Table 2 shows totals: METR $784bn vs OECD $782.8bn at 25% rate — overall similar in aggregate, but very different distribution)



## p. 30 — Section 5: Conclusions; Figure 5

Figure 5: country-level estimates of tax revenue gains of METR and OECD proposals at four minimum rates (15%, 20%, 25%, 30%), in USD millions and as share of corporate income tax revenue. Complete version for 136 countries available online.

Section 5 — Conclusions:

The METR would offer key advantages over the GLOBE:
- resolve the key problem with GLOBE by "eliminating the need for priority rules"
- greatly simplify the proposal
- capable of rapid implementation by all willing countries using common methodology, without treaty changes
- allow states to apply own tax rules and rates, without discrimination between domestic and multinational enterprises or between inbound and outbound investment
- result in "greater as well as more fairly distributed revenue gains than the GLOBE"
- "point the way forward to more effective comprehensive reforms"

Acknowledged limitations: METR would "still not be a complete solution." Changes would still be needed to tax treaties to ensure a taxable nexus for significant economic presence and to allow a switch-over rule. But crucially: "progress on ensuring a minimum effective tax rate should not be dependent on securing signature and ratification by all states of a multilateral treaty." Such a requirement "would in practice give all states a veto on implementation, which would be fatal."

Final statement: "The METR provides a practical and pragmatic basis for a feasible consensus of willing states to create a critical mass for progress towards effective reforms — while offering the prospect of immediate, substantial increases in revenues with a progressive global distribution (Figure 5)."



## p. 31 — Conclusions list; Endnotes 1–11

Page 31 restates the conclusions in bullet form and provides the endnotes. Key endnotes for thesis purposes:

Endnote 5: IIR is based on CFC principles (first introduced by US in 1962). Unlike CFC rules, which normally grant a unilateral credit for foreign income taxes paid (allowing prior source taxation rights), Pillar 2 would give priority to home countries.

Endnote 6: ETR on jurisdictional basis is "far superior to the global blending adopted for the GILTI."

Endnote 7: Important practical note — at present CbCRs are "not designed or indeed permitted to be used for tax purposes, even though paradoxically they are available only to tax authorities." Countries adopting METR would need to enact appropriate information requirements, coordinated among them, possibly reflected in revised CbCR or in the transfer pricing Master File (BEPS Action 13).

Endnote 8: Apportionment factors discussed in more detail in Cobham et al. (2021), including methodologies for ensuring location-specificity.

Endnote 9: Throwback rule detail — any sales, employees, or assets in countries where MNE has no taxable presence would be omitted from the FAR denominator. Also mentions Nigeria's Companies and Allied Matters Act, s.54 as an example of requiring foreign companies to incorporate locally.

Endnote 10: CFC rules can co-exist with METR since CFC taxes count toward ETR calculation; same applies to other cross-jurisdictional taxes such as withholding taxes.

Endnote 11: For METR, apportionment based on location-specific factors (employees, physical assets, place of delivery) means countries would compete to attract real activities instead of paper profits; combination of production and consumption factors (as argued by G24) would balance incentive to attract investment with revenue needs.
