# Devereux, Vella & Wardell-Burrus (2022) Pillar 2: Rule Order, Incentives, and Tax Competition
SSRN Working Paper 4009002

## Page 2 – Introduction

The paper opens by identifying two of the most controversial questions around Pillar 2:
- to what extent does Pillar 2 allow countries to engage in tax competition?
- which countries will collect the resulting tax revenues?

Both of these were largely open even after the political agreement of 1 July 2021 (OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework, "Statement on a Two-Pillar Solution", 1 July 2021). The Model Rules published by the OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework on 20 December 2021 provide "somewhat unexpected answers to both questions."

The paper focuses on two critical elements:

1. the Substance-Based Income Exclusion (SBIE), previously called the "substance-based carve-out" – a formulaic exclusion of an amount equal to 5% of the carrying value of eligible tangible assets and payroll from the minimum tax base, with more generous transitional rates (footnote 3)
2. the Qualified Domestic Minimum Top-up Tax (QDMTT) – introduced in the Model Rules and, "to our knowledge", not previously discussed in publicly available documentation

The authors define three distinct tax variables for income arising in a particular country (referred to throughout as the "source country", acknowledging the term is used loosely since it refers to the jurisdiction where profit is declared for both tax and accounting purposes, which may or may not host real economic activity – footnote 4):

- (a) Total Tax paid by the MNE on that income (i.e. to the source country and elsewhere combined)
- (b) Corporation Tax collected by the source country alone
- (c) Total Tax (Corporation Tax plus QDMTT) collected by the source country

This tripartite distinction between (a), (b), and (c) is central to the paper's argument.

Three main points are made:

i. The QDMTT effectively alters the rule order of Pillar 2, moving source countries to the head of the queue to collect the top-up tax.

ii. Pillar 2 creates a floor on source-country tax competition, but does so indirectly. Prior to the Model Rules, multiple designs of the SBIE were possible. All would have created a floor on variable (a) – total tax paid by the MNE – at 15% of "Excess Profit" (GloBE income less SBIE). However, only one design would have created a floor on variable (b) – Corporation Tax collected by the source country. The Model Rules have clarified this: countries still have an incentive to compete by reducing their Corporation Tax liability (b), potentially to zero. However, they now also have an incentive to collect a QDMTT equivalent to 15% of Excess Profit, and this does not worsen their competitive position. So the floor introduced by Pillar 2 is on variable (c) – Total Tax collected by the source country – not on (b).

iii. Pillar 2 increases the incentive for at least some countries to reduce Corporation Tax liabilities (b), possibly to zero. These countries still collect 15% of Excess Profit through the QDMTT. The potential substitution of Corporation Tax with the QDMTT is described as "a significant development" that "merits careful consideration."

Note: footnote 7 confirms that points (i) and (ii) were first published in a blog by the same authors on 23 December 2021.



## Pages 3–4 – Section 2.1: Pillar 2 Top-Up Calculation and the Distribution of Pillar 2 Revenues

The calculation of the top-up tax under Pillar 2 involves two steps (simplified):

Step 1 – Calculate the ETR:

ETR = Adjusted Covered Taxes / Adjusted GloBE Income

Step 2 – Calculate the Pillar 2 top-up:

max(15% - ETR, 0) * (Adjusted GloBE Income - SBIE) - QDMTT

Important clarification from the Model Rules (Articles 5.2 and 5.3): before publication, there was uncertainty about whether the SBIE would be deducted in step 1 (i.e. from the denominator of the ETR) as well as in step 2. The Model Rules confirmed that the SBIE is only used in step 2, when determining "Excess Profit" (defined as Adjusted GloBE Income less SBIE). This matters for the competitive dynamics (cross-reference to Devereux, Simmler, Vella, and Wardell-Burrus, "What is the Substance-Based Carve-Out under Pillar 2? And How Will it Affect Tax Competition?", EconPol Policy Brief 39, 17 November 2021 – footnote 9).

On the QDMTT: if the QDMTT equals the top-up rate multiplied by Excess Profit, it entirely offsets the top-up tax that would otherwise be collected by another country through the Income Inclusion Rule (IIR) or the Undertaxed Payment Rule (UTPR). That is, a source country introducing a QDMTT collects revenue that would otherwise flow to the parent's residence country (or another country applying the UTPR).

Crucially: a source country can introduce a QDMTT "in the safe knowledge that this would not impinge on its competitive position because the top-up tax would be collected by another country if it is not collected through the QDMTT." The authors therefore assume throughout the paper that countries will in fact adopt the QDMTT (footnote 11 notes that the EU Commission's director for direct taxation, Benjamin Angel, identified three options for EU Member States but did not include the QDMTT; the authors describe this as a fourth option – maintain a CIT rate below 15% and collect the top-up domestically via QDMTT).

The rule order under the Model Rules is therefore: QDMTT first, then IIR, then UTPR. This is a significant change from earlier versions of Pillar 2, and should substantially alter the distributional impact of Pillar 2 revenues among countries, addressing concerns that earlier versions would primarily benefit the handful of countries where large MNEs are headquartered.



## Pages 4–6 – Section 2.2: Creation of a Floor on Total Tax Collected by "Source" Countries

The analytical starting point: a country's decision to compete on tax balances the marginal cost (lost revenue) against the marginal benefit (improved competitive position, more investment, indirect benefits) of a reduction in domestic tax liability.

Without Pillar 2: a $1 reduction in domestic tax liability costs $1 in revenue and benefits the MNE by exactly $1.

Illustrative example setup:
- Countries X and Y compete for investment from MNE resident in W
- Both initially tax at 15% of financial accounting profit
- Investment generates financial profit of 1,000
- Tax of 150 in either country at 15%
- Of the 1,000 in financial profit, 400 is covered by the SBIE, leaving 600 in Excess Profit

Table 1 (p. 7) analyses four scenarios: X reduces its CIT rate to 14% or to 0%, under two Pillar 2 designs (SBIE only; and SBIE + QDMTT).

Case A – X reduces CIT to 14%, with SBIE but no QDMTT (column 2):
- ETR in X = 14%, top-up rate = 1%, applied to Excess Profit of 600, top-up = 6 paid to W
- X collects 140, MNE pays 146 total
- X forgoes 10 in revenue to give MNE a benefit of only 4
- Ratio of revenue forgone to benefit conferred: 1:0.4 (equal to SBIE/Profit ratio = 400/1,000)
- Compared to no Pillar 2 (where ratio is 1:1), the incentive to compete is weakened

Case B – X reduces CIT to 14%, with SBIE and QDMTT (column 3):
- ETR in X = 14%, QDMTT = 6 (collected by X), top-up to W = 0
- X collects 140 CIT + 6 QDMTT = 146 total; MNE still pays 146
- X forgoes only 4 in total tax to give MNE a benefit of 4: ratio is 1:1, same as without Pillar 2
- So the competitive benefit to X of reducing CIT is identical, but the revenue cost is much lower

Case C – X reduces CIT to 0%, with SBIE but no QDMTT (column 4):
- Top-up rate = 15%, applied to 600, top-up = 90 to W
- X collects 0, MNE pays 90 total
- X forgoes 150 to give MNE a benefit of 60; ratio 1:0.4

Case D – X reduces CIT to 0%, with SBIE and QDMTT (column 5):
- X collects 0 CIT + 90 QDMTT = 90 total; MNE pays 90 total
- X forgoes 60 in total tax to give MNE a benefit of 60; ratio 1:1
- Despite CIT of zero, X still collects 90 via QDMTT (= 15% of 600)

Key insight: the QDMTT does not change the total tax paid by the MNE relative to the design without QDMTT, but it increases the total tax collected by X. This is "the floor introduced by Pillar 2 – it is a floor on competition over Total Tax rather than Corporation Tax in the source country."

Without the QDMTT, Pillar 2 would have weakened (but not eliminated) the incentive to compete on CIT. With the QDMTT, the marginal cost-benefit ratio for the source country from reducing CIT is restored to 1:1 – as if Pillar 2 did not exist in terms of competitive dynamics. But total revenue is nonetheless protected at 15% of Excess Profit.

The paper also demonstrates that countries cannot replicate QDMTT outcomes through other routes:

1. Simply increasing CIT to 15%: top-up taxes may still arise if the domestic CIT base is narrower than Adjusted GloBE Income for any particular MNE.

2. Adopting Adjusted GloBE Income (P) as the CIT base with a 15% rate: this would eliminate top-up liability, but would place the country at a competitive disadvantage relative to countries with lower ETRs, because the sum of tax paid in those countries plus top-up to others equals less than 15% of P where there is Excess Profit. In the example, column 2 shows MNE paying 146 total, which is less than 15% of P (150).

3. Setting CIT to 15% of Excess Profit only (i.e. P minus SBIE): this would still generate a top-up liability. If X kept a 15% CIT rate but narrowed its base to Excess Profit, MNE would pay 90 in CIT implying an ETR of 9% (90/1,000), hence a top-up of 36 ((15%-9%) * 600 = 36). MNE's total tax = 126, compared to 90 under the zero-CIT + QDMTT approach. "Countries cannot replicate the outcomes that follow from the adoption of a QDMTT by increasing their Corporation Tax rate to 15%, even if they also replace their Corporation Tax base with Adjusted GloBE Income or Excess Profit."



## Pages 8–9 – Section 2.3: Stronger Incentive to Compete on Corporation Tax

Following Pillar 2, countries may face a stronger incentive to compete on Corporation Tax specifically.

Mechanism: countries whose MNEs face local ETRs below 15% and have Excess Profit may need to reduce their CIT liability just to maintain their current competitive position relative to rivals – and must reduce it by more than they would have had to do absent Pillar 2 in order to improve that position.

Table 2 (p. 9) illustrates this with a location decision:
- Y generates 1,020 in income, X generates only 1,000
- Y taxes at 15%, leaving MNE with after-tax income of 867
- Without Pillar 2: X needs to tax at 13% to offer 870 in after-tax income and attract investment
- With Pillar 2: X needs to offer an even lower CIT rate of 11% to achieve the same 870 after-tax income
  - At 11% CIT: CT = 110, QDMTT = (15%-11%) * 500 = 20, total tax = 130, after-tax = 870
- Crucially: this additional reduction in CIT (from 13% to 11%) comes at no additional revenue cost to X, because the QDMTT offsets the foregone CIT revenue

Conclusion for section 2.3: Pillar 2 increases the probability that some countries will reduce CIT liabilities, "perhaps even all the way to zero." But these countries retain revenue through the QDMTT.



## Page 10 – Section 3: Conclusion

The paper explicitly notes that its analysis is not exhaustive. It does not cover:
- the possibility that Pillar 2's increase in total tax liability in some (currently low-tax) jurisdictions may lead other countries to increase their CIT (cross-reference to Hebous and Keen, "Should low tax countries learn to love a minimum tax rate?", Oxford CBT Blog, 1 November 2021, and Hebous and Keen, "Pareto-Improving Minimum Corporate Taxation", IMF Working Paper WP/21/250 – footnote 14)
- other possible channels of tax competition that may still be open after Pillar 2

The two main incentives identified:
- countries are expected to retain (and indeed face stronger) incentives to reduce CIT liabilities, possibly to zero
- countries are also expected to introduce a QDMTT, which acts as "a floor to competition over the Total Tax imposed by source countries"

Both incentives point toward the substitution of Corporation Tax with the QDMTT, at least for in-scope MNEs. This substitution is described as coming as "a by-product of Pillar 2, but it is momentous and merits further thought."



## Pages 11–16 – Appendix: More Formal Analysis

This section formalises the main argument.

### Section 1: Define the top-up tax

Notation (one constituent entity in the jurisdiction, for simplicity):
- T = adjusted covered taxes
- P = net GloBE income
- ETR: e = T/P
- m = minimum rate = 15%
- C = substance-based income exclusion (SBIE)
- Overall required top-up (if m > e): J = (m - e)(P - C)
- QDMTT with rate q applied to base (P - C): Q = q(P - C)
- In practice, q = 0 or q = m - e
- Top-up collected by other jurisdictions: K = J - Q = (m - e - q)(P - C)
- Total tax paid by MNE: Z = T + Q + K = T + J

With general domestic tax T = τ(P - G) (where G is an arbitrary deduction from financial accounting profit), substituting into Z:

Z = m(P - C) + T * (C/P)

Two components:
- the minimum rate m applied to Excess Profit (P - C)
- adjusted covered taxes T = τ(P - G) multiplied by the ratio C/P

Special case where G = 0: Z = m(P - C) + τC

### Section 2.1: Choice of q (QDMTT rate)

Source country revenue: D = T + Q = τ(P - G) + q(P - C)

Constrained to m > e and q ≤ m - e.

Since Z does not depend on q (other countries give a full credit for the QDMTT), the source country's optimal choice is clearly q = m - e: this captures the full Pillar 2 top-up revenue domestically.

### Section 2.2: Marginal incentives from choice of covered taxes T

If q = m - e, total source country revenue is:

D = T + (m - T/P)(P - C) = m(P - C) + T * (C/P)

This equals Z (total MNE tax liability), since the source country captures all top-up revenue. Cutting T by $1 reduces the MNE's tax liability and the source country's net revenue by C/P – the same 1:1 trade-off as in the absence of the minimum tax. For every dollar of reduced liability to the MNE, the country gives up exactly C/P in revenue (not $1 because the SBIE portion of the income base is unaffected by the top-up mechanism).

If instead q = 0 (no QDMTT): a $1 cut in CIT reduces the country's revenue by $1, but reduces MNE's liability by only C/P, because another country captures the top-up. This is a less favourable trade-off for the source country – it gives up more revenue for a smaller competitive gain.

At the extreme, with q = m - e, the country may choose T = 0. Total tax paid by MNE = m(P - C) (minimum rate on Excess Profit only), which is not zero.

### Section 2.2.2: Location of real activity (formal)

Countries X and Y compete for real inward investment from MNE resident in M.
- Pre-tax profit higher in Y: π_Y > π_X, difference D = π_Y - π_X > 0
- Y taxes at rate v ≥ 15% (no Pillar 2 charge in Y)

Without Pillar 2: X's maximum CIT rate to attract investment, w*_1, satisfies:

(1 - w*_1)π_X = (1 - v)π_Y

→ w*_1 = 1 - (1 - v)π_Y / π_X

Maximum revenue to X = w*_1 * π_X = vπ_Y - D (i.e. revenue in Y less the pre-tax profit difference)

With Pillar 2 + QDMTT, assuming q = m - e and tax base in X = P: total tax on activity in X = m(π_X - C) + w*_2 * C

To match after-tax income in Y:

w*_2 = [(1 - v)π_Y - m(π_X - C)] / C

Maximum revenue to X = m(π_X - C) + w*_2 * C = vπ_Y - D (identical to without Pillar 2)

Comparing w*_1 and w*_2: since π_X ≥ C, it follows that w*_2 ≤ w*_1. Country X needs to offer a lower CIT rate under Pillar 2 to attract the same investment – confirming the stronger competition result.

### Section 3: Profit Shifting

Without Pillar 2: shifting $1 of profit from a high-tax country (rate s) to X saves $(s - τ) in tax.

With Pillar 2 + QDMTT (q = m - e): total tax on income in X is:

Z = D = m(P - C) + τ(P - G) * (C/P) = m(P - C) + τC - τG * (C/P)

Marginal tax rate on income arising in X (∂Z/∂P):

∂Z/∂P = m + τG * (C/P²)

This is company-specific (depends on P, C, G).

Overall gain from shifting $1:

R = s - (m + τG * C/P²)

If τ is low (e.g. after competing CIT to zero), this simplifies to approximately s - m: the gain from shifting is the difference between the high-tax country's rate and the Pillar 2 minimum rate of 15%. "Unless the original tax rate was close to m, this would represent a reduction in the incentive to shift profit to the source country." The paper notes this reduction in profit-shifting incentives is possible but may not be the dominant case, since MNEs would not have shifted profit to X absent Pillar 2 if there existed other lower-rate countries at the time.
