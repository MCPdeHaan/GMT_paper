# What Do We Know about Base Erosion and Profit Shifting? A Review of the Empirical Literature dharmapala2014

### Page 421: Abstract and Title Info

* Core Subject: The paper focuses on tax-motivated income shifting within multinational firms, commonly known as "base erosion and profit shifting (BEPS)".  
* Objective: To survey the empirical literature, evaluate different methodological approaches, and clarify the known magnitude of BEPS.  
* Main Conclusion: Recent empirical literature leveraging new, richer data sources estimates the magnitude of BEPS to be "much smaller than that found in earlier studies".  
* Framework: The paper seeks to contextualize this magnitude for policy implications, specifically emphasizing the role of "existing legal and economic frictions as constraints on BEPS".  

### Page 422

Introduction 

* Policy Context: BEPS is a highly relevant topic, heavily tied to the OECD's ongoing initiatives.  
* Consensus Estimate: The literature's current representative estimate is a "semi-elasticity of reported income with respect to the tax rate differential across countries of 0.8".  

### Page 423

* Methodological Focus: The paper heavily reviews the dominant economic framework, termed the "'Hines-Rice' approach". It also incorporates recent accounting and economic methods proposed by Dharmapala and Riedel (2013) and Dyreng and Markle (2013).  
* Data Evolution: The shift from "aggregate country-level data sets to firm-level microdata" has improved the credibility of empirical estimates.  
* Size Contrast: Early 1990s studies produced tax sensitivity estimates roughly three times larger than currently accepted numbers.  
* Magnitude Interpretation: Using the 0.8 semi-elasticity (from a Heckemeyer and Overesch meta-regression), a 10 percentage point tax rate increase difference between parent and affiliate would "increase the pre-tax income reported by the affiliate by 8 per cent".  

### Page 424

Approaches used in the empirical literature:  
* Foundational Model: The primary framework derives from Grubert and Mutti (1991) and Hines and Rice (1994).  
* Theoretical Premise: An affiliate's pre-tax income is the sum of "'true' income and 'shifted' income". "True income is generated by the affiliate using capital and labour inputs," typically proxied by tangible assets and employment compensation.  
* Shifted Income Mechanism: This is determined by tax incentives, generally the tax rate differential between the parent and the affiliate. Any reported income not explained by local labor/capital inputs is attributed to BEPS.  
* Equation 1 Formulation: The Hines-Rice baseline uses the log-linear equation:  

$$log \pi_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \tau_i + \beta_2 \log K_i + \beta_3 \log L_i + X_i \gamma + epsilon_i$$


* Footnote Detail: Loss-making affiliates are typically excluded because their incentives for BEPS are "attenuated" by limitations on loss offsets.

### Page 425

* Coefficient Interpretation: The parameter $\beta_1$ represents a "marginal effect" (the change in reported profits for a small tax rate change).  
* Statutory vs. Effective Rates: The empirical literature standardly uses statutory tax rates to construct the tax incentive $\tau_i$. "Effective tax rates in part reflect endogenous choices made by the firm" (e.g., debt usage), whereas statutory rates are determined externally by governments, offering cleaner identification despite potential mismeasurement.  
* Value of Microdata: Early models like Hines and Rice (1994) used aggregate country-level data from the US Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA). Using longitudinal affiliate-level panel data allows for the control of "unobservable determinants of income," correcting biases.  
* Example of Bias: Without fixed effects, an affiliate with high unobserved intangible assets in a low-tax area might report high profits that are misattributed to BEPS rather than asset mismeasurement.

### Page 426

* Equation 2 Formulation: Implementing panel data modifies the model:  


$$\log \pi_{it} = \beta_1 \tau_{it} + \beta_2 \log K_{it} + \beta_3 \log L_{it} + X_{it} \gamma + \mu_i + \delta_t + \epsilon_{it}$$

* Fixed Effects: The model includes $\mu_i$ (affiliate fixed effects to control for time-invariant traits) and $\delta_t$ (year fixed effects for common profitability changes).  
* Exogeneity: Changes in $\tau_{it}$ typically arise from legislative tax reforms, not affiliate behavior, thereby isolating exogenous variation.  
* Footnote Detail: If researchers use tax data encompassing all global affiliates within a group, they can apply "country-pair-year fixed effects" to account for specific unobserved trends between two jurisdictions.

### Page 427

* Tax Accounting Literature: Another method utilizes US firm data from Compustat, assessing the "ratio of foreign pre-tax income to foreign sales (FRoS)" against foreign tax rate (FTR) variables.  
* Core Challenge: Both the amount of income shifted and the firm's operational mix are "endogenous choices of the firm," creating a potential upward bias in the BEPS estimate.  
* Accounting Findings: Despite potential upward bias, older studies like Collins, Kemsley and Lang (1998) found no evidence of outbound US income shifting. Later studies (Klassen and Laplante, 2012\) improved this with multi-year variables and IV strategies.  
* Sales Location Model: Dyreng and Markle (2013) posited that the location of an MNC's sales is "non-manipulable" due to fixed consumer locations, using the discrepancy between sales location and earnings location as direct evidence of BEPS.

### Page 428

* Dharmapala and Riedel (2013): Proposes a thought experiment: if unexpected "manna from heaven" (an exogenous income shock) appeared in a parent firm, a fraction should theoretically be shifted to low-tax affiliates, but not to high-tax affiliates.  

$$log \pi_{it} = \beta_1 log \hat \pi_{it}+ \beta_2(d_{it}log \hat \pi_{it})+X_{it}\gamma + \mu_i + \delta_t + \epsilon_{it}$$

where $\hat \pi_{it}$ is income shock

* Control Group: High-tax affiliates serve as a control group to rule out non-tax explanations for income transfers, such as "internal capital markets" or risk-sharing.  
* Footnote Detail: To ensure observed effects represent shifting rather than real capital investments, the authors focus strictly on "contemporaneous changes" in the same year as the shock.  
* Identifying Shocks: Exogenous shocks are isolated using earnings from independent firms operating in the "same industry and the same country as the parent firm".

### Page 429

*  The magnitude of BEPS:  
* Interpreting Results: The core parameter, $\beta_1$, denotes the "'semi-elasticity' of pre-tax income" regarding the tax differential.  
* Practical Example: A semi-elasticity of 0.8 means a 10 percentage point tax difference translates to an 8% increase in reported pre-tax income. These figures are evaluated at the "sample mean" and cannot necessarily be extrapolated to extreme tax changes.  
* Footnote Detail: Depending on whether the independent variable is defined as a tax differential or a local statutory rate, the expected sign of the $\beta$ coefficient will flip (positive for differentials, negative for local rates).

### Page 430

* Databases: Recent studies rely heavily on Bureau van Dijk's commercial databases—Orbis (global) and Amadeus (European)—which provide "unconsolidated" financial and ownership microdata.  
* Huizinga and Laeven (2008): Using a 1999 cross-section from Amadeus, they estimated an overall semi-elasticity across Europe of 1.31.  
* Footnote Detail: Amadeus and Orbis only report ownership structures for the final year of data, risking "misclassification of ownership structures" in historical panels, which generally creates a bias against finding significant results. Crucially, they report "financial statement information rather than tax return information".

### Page 431

* Magnitude Reduction: Huizinga and Laeven's estimate (1.31) is markedly lower than early aggregate estimates, like Hines and Rice's 2.25.  
* Panel Data Estimates: Introducing affiliate fixed effects lowers the magnitude further: Dischinger (2010) found an overall semi-elasticity of 0.7, while Lohse and Riedel (2013) found 0.4.  
* Meta-Regression Consensus: Heckemeyer and Overesch (2013) synthesized 238 estimates from 25 studies to trace why magnitudes vary, confirming that methodological innovations shrink the BEPS estimates.

### Page 433

* Return on Assets (ROA): Weichenrieder (2009) used MiDi to show a 10 percentage point tax increase for a foreign parent lifts its German affiliate's ROA by about half a percentage point.  
* Debt Shifting: Büttner et al. (2012) found a modest tax impact on inter-affiliate debt: a 10 percentage point local tax increase raises the "ratio of internal debt to total capital" by 8%.  
* Legal Constraints: "Thin capitalisation rules," which deny interest deductibility over a specific threshold, are highly effective. When tightened, the tax sensitivity of the debt ratio falls by half.  
* Dharmapala and Riedel (2013) Baseline: Evaluated affiliates in different industries than their parents to isolate shocks. They found a 10% increase in parent profit only yields a 0.4% profit increase for low-tax affiliates.

### Page 434

* Absolute Shifted Value: Under Dharmapala and Riedel's model, of a hypothetical $22 million parent profit increase, only about $420,000 (roughly 2%) is shifted to low-tax affiliates.  
* Reconciling Estimates: Extrapolating the Hines-Rice approach to similar Amadeus data yields a semi-elasticity of 0.5, implying that just under 4% of a parent's income is shifted.  
* Conclusion on Size: Dharmapala and Riedel's shock approach yields a slightly smaller metric than Hines-Rice (2% vs. 4%), but the absolute discrepancy is not dramatically large.  
* Footnote Detail: Standard statistics note an average parent profit of $220 million and average low-tax affiliate profit of $7.7 million.

### Page 435

* Methodological Limitations: Dharmapala and Riedel restrict their sample to distinct industries, which inadvertently suppresses observations of "strategic transfer pricing". Consequently, their approach primarily detects debt shifting, meaning it might underestimate total BEPS.  
* Sales Approach: Dyreng and Markle (2013) estimate that 10% of US MNC domestic income is shifted abroad, but this likely overestimates BEPS because legitimate direct sales from foreign affiliates to US consumers are conflated with income shifting.  
* Primary BEPS Channels: The literature identifies two main vectors: "strategic transfer pricing" and the "strategic use of inter-affiliate debt".

### Page 436

* Measuring Channels: Researchers differentiate channels by comparing regressions on pre-tax profit (captures both debt and transfer pricing) versus Earnings Before Interest and Taxes (EBIT, which isolates transfer pricing).  
* Ratio: Heckemeyer and Overesch (2013) estimate that 70% of BEPS magnitude originates from strategic transfer pricing, with 30% from debt.  
* 2\. Other issues related to BEPS:  
  * a) Parent-to-foreign vs. foreign-to-foreign: Dischinger, Knoll and Riedel (2014) established that shifting income from parents to affiliates is significantly lower than shifting between sibling affiliates. Disincentives out of parents may stem from agency costs or repatriation taxes.

### Page 437

* US Tax Rules: The 1997 US "check-the-box" (CTB) regulations provided latitude in entity tax status and are widely believed to have increased foreign-to-foreign shifting.  
* b) Real economic activity, intangible assets and BEPS: BEPS opportunities shape where real capital is deployed.  
* Role of R\&D: Grubert (2003) matched US tax returns to Compustat to prove that affiliates with highly R\&D-intensive parents are exceptionally sensitive to local tax rates. These firms polarize their physical locations, heavily utilizing both very low-tax and very high-tax jurisdictions.

### Page 438

* Efficiency Debate: Some literature theorizes that BEPS might paradoxically "reduce distortions to the location of real activity," enhancing overall efficiency.  
* Intangibles Location: Dischinger and Riedel (2011) proved a 1 percentage point drop in relative tax rates raises a subsidiary's intangible assets by 2.2%. Karkinsky and Riedel (2012) analyzed EU patent applications, generating an implied semi-elasticity of \-3.5 (a 1 percentage point tax rate increase cuts patent applications by 3.5%).  
* c) Territorial vs. Worldwide systems: Markle (2012) found that MNCs headquartered in "worldwide" tax systems tend to shift *less* income overall compared to those in "territorial" systems.

### Page 439

* Repatriation Constraints: Worldwide and territorial firms show no difference in foreign-to-foreign shifting, but worldwide firms shift less *out* of the parent, likely due to future repatriation taxes.  
* d) Has BEPS grown over time?: While researchers like Grubert (2012) and Klassen and Laplante (2012) claim it has grown, panel studies using the Hines-Rice approach suggest the opposite. Lohse and Riedel (2013) formally tested a time trend and found tax sensitivity actually fell significantly between 1999–2009, likely due to stricter global transfer pricing and thin capitalization laws.  
* e) BEPS and tax revenue: Huizinga and Laeven originally calculated massive revenue effects, but recent literature's smaller BEPS estimates imply correspondingly smaller revenue impacts.

### Page 440

* Outlier Estimates: Clausing (2009) used aggregate BEA data to assert the US lost one-third of its corporate tax revenue to BEPS in 2004, relying on a massive implied semi-elasticity of 3.3.  
* Macro Perspective: Corporate tax is a relatively minor revenue component for major economies (e.g., just 7.4% of UK revenue in 2012).  
* Revenue Stability: Despite BEPS and generally falling statutory rates, corporate tax revenue measured as a fraction of GDP has been remarkably "robust in recent times".

### Page 441

* Overall Impact: Given corporate tax forms less than 10% of revenue in most OECD countries, BEPS is unlikely to cause a dramatic fiscal crisis.  
* IV. Interpreting the magnitude of BEPS:  
* 1\. Is the estimated magnitude of BEPS large or small?: There is no universally accepted baseline for evaluating if the 0.8 consensus semi-elasticity is practically large.  
* Public Discourse: Politicians frequently point to "the fraction of the income of MNCs that is reported in tax havens" as *ipso facto* proof that BEPS is enormous. For example, 42.6% of US MNC foreign net income lands in tax havens.

### Page 442

* Table 2 Highlights: The table displays physical footprint vs. income. While havens hold 42.6% of net income, they only hold 11.1% of "Net plant, property and equipment" and 4.9% of employees.  
* Empirical Disconnect: Academic literature identifies BEPS *at the margin* (small changes), not the baseline absolute level.  
* Table 3 Illustration: A tax cut generates the exact same marginal income shift whether the initial profit allocation between a high-tax and zero-tax state was 90/10 or 60/40.  
* The Gap: A 0.8 semi-elasticity would theoretically only explain shifting 10-20% of income to havens. The observed 40% represents an "inframarginal" phenomenon that basic elasticities fail to explain.

### Page 443

* Mechanical Artefact: The high net book income in havens may simply be a "mechanical artefact of how the use by MNCs of holding companies located in havens is reflected in government statistics".  
* Example: If a US firm invests in France via a haven holding company, interest payments from France generate book income in the haven and zero it out in France. This happens whether the structure is motivated by tax avoidance or legitimate corporate structuring.  
* The Netherlands Test: The Netherlands is not a traditional haven, yet it hosts a disproportionate share of net book income (13.4%), proving that holding company locales inherently skew these statistics.

### Page 444

* Value Added: Hines (2010) asserts that "value added" is a more reliable metric than net book income. Value added by US MNCs in havens is a much more modest 14.5%.  
* Taxable Income Proxy: Future research must determine if net book income or value added is a closer proxy for actual taxable income. If value added is better, it aligns perfectly with the small BEPS magnitudes found in academia.  
* 2\. Some directions for future research: How to reconcile small empirical BEPS estimates, stable tax revenues, and rampant anecdotal stories of aggressive MNC tax evasion?.  
* Firm Heterogeneity: A major key is the "considerable heterogeneity in the apparent tax sophistication of MNCs". Data shows only 58–59% of large MNCs even possess tax haven affiliates.

### Page 445

* Fixed Costs of Tax Planning: This behavioral split suggests "significant fixed costs of tax planning". Large entities easily absorb the fixed cost and shift heavily (making headlines), while smaller firms skip obvious tax-planning avenues because the fixed cost outweighs the benefit.  
* Validation: Mills, Erickson and Maydew (1998) proved tax-planning expenditures decrease proportionally as firm size drops, despite generating high rates of return.  
* The Next Frontier: Future analysis must model existing "legal and economic frictions as constraints on BEPS" to properly assess international tax reform proposals.  
* Footnote Detail: Mentions the "undersheltering puzzle"—the mystery of why some firms inexplicably fail to utilize perfectly lawful tax avoidance strategies.
