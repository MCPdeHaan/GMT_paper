## Page 33

Good implementation of QDMTT, reduces the top-up tax charged by the UPE jurisdiction to nil, making sure that the tax revenue stays in the source jurisdiction.

Even countries with statutory rates above 15 per cent cannot be certain their ETR will not fall below the minimum. Differences in calculating the tax base under GloBE rules versus domestic rules (e.g., different treatment of incentives or covered taxes) can result in an ETR below 15 per cent. Therefore, implementing a QDMTT is desirable even for high-tax jurisdictions as a "safety valve" to prevent other countries from taxing their resident entities.

### Page 34

Tax rules often allow faster expensing of assets than financial accounting rules (which typically use straight-line depreciation) like accelerated depreciation and immediate expensing. This creates a timing difference: taxable profits are lower in early years, reducing taxes paid. Without adjustment, this would lower the GloBE ETR below 15 per cent, triggering a top-up tax and eliminating the incentive's benefit. "result in a liability under the IIR and in “significant and frequent IIR tax paid and ultimately IIR tax credits" para 220, this is risk for capital intensive businesses and could lead to over-taxation. 

The Inclusive Framework recognises these as "low-risk incentives" tied to substantive activity. To prevent the top-up tax from negating these incentives, the rules use **deferred tax accounting**. The "deferred tax liability" (the tax payable in the future when the accounting depreciation exceeds tax depreciation) is added to the "adjusted covered taxes" in the ETR calculation. This increases the ETR for the current year, neutralizing the temporary drop caused by the incentive.

The deferred tax adjustment amount is capped: it is calculated at the lower of the applicable domestic tax rate or 15 per cent.

### Page 35

Loss carry-forwards allow companies to offset current losses against future profits. In financial statements, this creates a "deferred tax asset". The GloBE rules allow adjustments for losses to smooth out volatility.
Two mechanisms are available:

1. **Deferred tax accounting:** Similar to depreciation, using Article 4.4.
2. **GloBE loss election:** A constituent entity can elect to create a "GloBE loss deferred tax asset" in the year of the loss. This asset is equal to the net GloBE loss multiplied by the minimum rate (15%) and is carried forward to be used in years with net GloBE income.

Countries may refund negative tax liabilities via tax credits, which can also be used as incentives (e.g., for R&D).

### Page 36

The GloBE rules distinguish between:

1. **Qualified Refundable Tax Credits:** Refundable within four years. These are treated as **income** (GloBE income) in the denominator. They function similarly to government grants.
2. **Non-qualified Refundable Tax Credits:** These are treated as a reduction in **tax expenses** (covered taxes) in the numerator.

Both affect the ETR, but non-qualified credits generally lower the ETR more directly (by reducing the numerator) than qualified credits (which increase the denominator). However, the text notes that because qualified credits are treated as income, they are not "entirely re-collected as taxes," meaning the incentive is partially preserved.

The GloBE tax base starts with financial accounting profit (profit before tax). This measure includes all operating expenses but disregards "special local tax exclusions" or super-deductions that don't exist in financial accounts.

### Page 37

While implicit permanent differences exist between local tax bases and the GloBE base, the rules do not allow comprehensive adjustments for all local deductions. Adjustments are limited to exclude only "material items that are commonly excluded from the tax base of Inclusive Framework jurisdictions," such as excluded dividends and equity gains. This implies that unique local super-deductions might lower the ETR and risk top-up taxation.

Tax holidays (temporary elimination of tax) and specific exemptions (e.g., for certain locations or sectors) are "direct cuts from the tax bill". Because GloBE focuses on effective taxation, exemptions that eliminate income tax will drastically lower the ETR. These are "largely to be affected" by the top-up tax, meaning the tax savings offered by the holiday will simply be collected by the UPE jurisdiction instead.

### Page 38

To avoid double taxation, many jurisdictions exempt dividends from taxable income (participation exemption). GloBE rules align with this by allowing dividends to be excluded from GloBE Income (the denominator). This prevents the ETR from dropping artificially.
*Limitation:* This exclusion does *not* apply to "short-term portfolio shareholding" (ownership below 10 per cent held for less than one year).

Gains/losses from the disposition of ownership interests (except portfolio shareholdings) excluded from GloBE income. This matches common domestic exemptions. However, exemptions for **foreign currency exchange gains or losses (FXGL)** are a risk area. If domestic law exempts FXGL due to currency differences, but GloBE rules do not adjust for it (which they don't if accounting and tax functional currencies are the same), it creates a permanent difference that lowers the ETR and is "likely to be impacted" by the top-up tax.

### Page 39

These regimes prevent economic double taxation by crediting shareholders for tax paid by the company.

1. **Qualified Imputation Tax Regimes:** Refunds go to shareholders who are subject to tax or are excluded entities. These refunds do *not* reduce the company's "covered taxes" (numerator), so the ETR remains higher, preserving the regime.
2. **Disqualified Refundable Imputation Tax Regimes:** Refunds go to shareholders not subject to tax (e.g., non-residents). These refunds *must* be added back to GloBE income, reducing the ETR and potentially triggering top-up tax.

### Page 40

Withholding tax (WHT) on passive income (dividends, interest, royalties) is included in "covered taxes" calculated for ETR, explained in art 4.2.1.(c) of model rules. If a country offers a WHT incentive (e.g., 0% WHT on outbound royalties), it fails to increase the covered taxes amount. If the recipient jurisdiction also has a low CIT, the aggregate tax on that income might drop below 15 per cent. 

### Page 41

STTR (subject to tax rule) is a treaty-based rule allowing source jurisdictions to impose additional tax on certain intragroup payments (interest, royalties, etc.) if they are taxed below 9 per cent in the recipient's jurisdiction. It allows the source state to "top up" the tax to the agreed minimum rate. this makes double tax-treaty benefits conditional upon taxation of corresponding income in country of resicdence. covered payments include interest, royalties, other payments for mobile factors (capital, assets, risks owned or assumed). STTR has nominal rate cap of 9%, applies for covered payments between connected persons. 

IP boxes offer lower rates on intellectual property income. Even if "substance-based" (BEPS Action 5 compatible), GloBE rules do not automatically exempt them. If the IP box rate is low enough to drag the jurisdictional ETR below 15 per cent, it will be impacted. Solutions for this is if MNE has other high tax income in jurisdiction to bring ETR again to 15% or substance carve-outs where the R&D activity is heavy on payroll and tangible assets, the "excess profit" subject to top-up tax is reduced.

GloBE rules, according to the authors, will reduce impact of IP boxed, although the effects will still continue (if top up tax is collected by the jurisdiction itself)
