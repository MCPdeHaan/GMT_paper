# Garcia-Bernardo, Janský & Tørsløv (2021) Multinational Corporations and Tax Havens: Evidence from Country-by-Country Reporting
International Tax and Public Finance, 28(6), 1519–1561


## p. 1519 (Abstract)

- Paper contributes to the evidence on MNC profit shifting to tax havens by comparing a range of available data sets focusing on US MNCs
- Key novelty: uses country-by-country reporting (CBCR) data, a full sample of which was released in December 2019 for the first time
- Using CBCR data, they establish that "lower effective corporate tax rates are associated with higher levels of reported profits when compared with different indicators of real economic activity"
- This corresponds to MNCs shifting profits to low-ETR countries without shifting substantive economic activity
- They identify the most important tax havens for US MNCs as countries with both low ETRs and high profits misaligned with economic activity

Keywords: Effective tax rate, Profit shifting, Tax haven, Country-by-country reporting, Multinational corporation, Foreign direct investment, Tax competition
JEL: C81, F21, F23, H25, H26


## p. 1520 (Section 1: Introduction)

- Profit shifting from production/retail countries to tax havens has been extensively discussed in economics literature
- US-headquartered MNCs are a good example
- Prior estimates (Clausing 2009, Zucman 2014, US Joint Committee on Taxation 2014, Clausing 2016, Tørsløv et al. 2020) using various methods find that government revenue foregone due to profit shifting amounts to "approximately one fifth of the US corporate tax base"
- Guvenen et al. (2017) find profit shifting leads to undercalculation of US GDP; argue missing corporate profits should be reattributed from a small group of tax havens
- All these studies rely on BEA survey data
- Dowd et al. (2017) use confidential corporate tax returns and confirm the importance of profit shifting; show reported profits are "much more sensitive to tax rate changes in low tax countries than in high tax countries"
- Data sets other than BEA and combinations thereof have been seldom used for US MNCs

- The paper uses the newly available CBCR data set released for the first time in December 2018 by the IRS
- IRS CBCR advantages over other sources:
  - most reliable country-level information about MNCs' tax payments and profits to date
  - covers an extensive range of countries, including tax havens often omitted from existing data sources
  - by design, does not suffer from selection bias in country coverage (MNCs required to report for all countries worldwide where they have any economic activity)
- CBCR data are not as detailed as previously discussed (Murphy 2009; Sikka and Murphy 2015; Wójcik 2015), but constitute an improvement in some aspects (quality of tax payment info, country coverage)
- De Simone and Olbert (2019) show that the requirement for large MNCs to share CBCR data with governments already had an effect on MNC transparency even before the data was published
- This paper aims to provide "the first evidence of the effective tax rates of MNCs using the newly published IRS CBCR data set itself"

Two interrelated research questions:
1. Primary: Which tax havens are most important for US-headquartered MNCs?
   - Defined as countries where US MNCs pay low ETRs while reporting large profits and little economic activity (misaligned profit)
   - Answered in three steps: (i) estimate ETRs, (ii) quantify misaligned profit, (iii) examine relationship between ETRs and misaligned profits


## p. 1521

- ETRs calculated as ratios of taxes and gross income
- Misaligned profit measured as profits lacking proportionate economic activity in terms of assets, revenue or employees
- The third step tests whether more misaligned profits are reported in countries with lower ETRs, indicating key tax havens

- Authors argue ETRs and misaligned profits are "two sides of the same coin":
  - Countries compete to attract MNCs' reported profits by lowering their ETRs; a country's ETR can be a proxy of its tendency to attract foreign profits
  - A country's success in attracting profits can be measured by misaligned profits reported there
  - Enabling low ETRs is "primarily at the governments' discretion" while reporting profits without proportionate economic activity is "ultimately due to the MNCs' actions"
- Both have been studied before, but few studies focused on their interrelationship
- Profit shifting studies typically use only one data source (e.g. BEA); opportunity to combine several suitable data sets has been "significantly underexploited"

Secondary research objective: identify how the new IRS CBCR data set differs from existing data sets
- Answered both conceptually and empirically
- Compared data sources: BEA's and Eurostat's foreign affiliate statistics, controlled foreign corporation data (IRS), Bureau van Dijk's Orbis, Standard & Poor's Compustat

- Generally find strong correlation between data sets, but also important conceptual and statistical differences (e.g. extent of correlation differs across economic variables and country groups)
- Find a negative correlation between ETRs and profit misaligned with economic activity across all data sets
- Several countries identified with low ETRs and high shares of profits contrasting with much lower shares of employee numbers and other indicators


## p. 1522

Various kinds of ETRs:
- Forward-looking (ex ante) law-based ETRs: derived from hypothetical business models, heavily used in literature (Devereux and Griffith 2003 methodology applied by Spengel et al. 2014, European Commission 2018, Congressional Budget Office 2017, Hanappi 2018)
- Backward-looking (ex post) MNC data-based ETRs: what this paper uses
- Forward-looking ETRs are "less effective than backward-looking ETRs in the case of MNCs" because backward-looking ETRs "depict the actual taxation of MNCs" and provide "a good indication of the extent to which countries use low taxes to attract MNCs"
- More detailed comparative discussion of the two in Janský (2019)

- Common approach: estimating ETRs as ratios of corporate income tax and gross income
- Prior ETR studies using BEA: Bosworth et al. (2007), Stewart (2014), Clausing (2016), Cobham and Janský (2019), Wright and Zucman (2018), Tørsløv et al. (2020)
- Desai et al. (2004): used BEA data; ETR = ratio of foreign income taxes paid to foreign pre-tax income for each affiliate; used medians as country-level observations
- Mutti and Grubert (2004): estimated country average effective CIT rates using US Treasury Form 5471 by US-controlled foreign corporations in manufacturing
- Confidential tax returns: used by Government Accountability Office (2008) and Dowd et al. (2017) for the USA (latter uses averages weighted by positive profits)

- Misaligned profits: second key indicator
- Estimated as profits lacking proportionate economic activity (assets, revenue or employees)
- "Similar but broader measure than profit shifting indicators commonly found in literature"
- Profit shifting by US MNCs well recognized: Hines and Rice (1994), Grubert (2012), Dowd et al. (2017), Guvenen et al. (2017)
- Transfer mispricing channel: Clausing (2003), Bernard et al. (2006), Flaaen (2017)
- Both US-centred and cross-country estimates agree profit shifting is costly in terms of foregone revenue, "in particular for the US government"

- Relationship between profit shifting and ETRs less intensively studied:
  - Government Accountability Office (2008): using IRS data, ETRs on US MNCs' foreign operations vary considerably by country and correlate with where income is reported
  - Wright and Zucman (2018): using BEA data, effective foreign tax rate fell by half since late 1990s in sectors other than oil; "almost half of this decline results from the rise of profit shifting to tax havens"
  - Cobham and Janský (2019): BEA data, show rough correlation between ETRs and misaligned profits


## p. 1523

- In contrast with prior studies, this paper uses newly available and (in some respects) superior CBCR data to investigate the ETR-misaligned profit relationship

- Secondary question: how useful is IRS CBCR for studying ETRs and profits in tax havens?
- Want to investigate whether CBCR data re-establish appropriate disclosure and accountability of MNCs (as Cobham et al. 2018 argued)
- OECD (2018) envisions that BEPS Action 11's recommendation to collect aggregated and anonymized CBCR data will support OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework's ongoing work on BEPS measurement and monitoring
- Paper's aim: "to provide researchers and the public with a more complete view of the global activities of the largest MNCs" (paraphrasing OECD 2018, p. 42)
- "One of the major challenges associated with measuring profit shifting is that only limited information is available on the location of MNCs' income, taxes, and business activities and that CBCR represents a step forward" (following OECD 2018, p. 42)

Policy relevance:
- Governments focused on how taxing rights on income from cross-border activities in the digital age should be allocated (OECD 2019, p. 5)
- OECD proposals divided into two groups, paper relevant to both:
  - Pillar One: allocation of taxing rights, review of profit allocation and nexus rules (OECD 2019, p. 6); the paper's misaligned profit estimates are relevant here
  - Pillar Two: remaining BEPS issues, rules providing jurisdictions with "a right to 'tax back' where other jurisdictions have not exercised their primary taxing rights or where payment is otherwise subject to low levels of effective taxation"; the paper's ETR estimates speak directly to this
- Results confirm how "misaligned profits and ETRs, or Pillars One and Two, are interrelated"

Paper structure: data description and comparison, methodology (ETRs and misaligned profits), results (descriptive statistics, statistical comparisons, ETR and misaligned profit estimates, their relationship), conclusion and policy implications


## p. 1524 (Section 2: Data)

- Exploit a new data set and combinations with a range of previously used data sets
- BEA: recently used by Wright and Zucman (2018)
- Eurostat: Tørsløv et al. (2020)
- Compustat: Dyreng et al. (2017)
- US-controlled foreign corporations: Mutti and Grubert (2004)
- Orbis: Garcia-Bernardo et al. (2017)
- General overviews of data sources: OECD (2015) and Keightley and Stupak (2015)
- IRS CBCR has only recently been released, hardly used for any analysis until autumn 2019 (exception: social media, e.g. Setser 2019)
- By October 2019, two other papers using 2016 data emerged:
  - Cobham et al. (2019): evaluation of recent policy proposals
  - IMF's de Mooij et al. (2019): also evaluation of policy proposals
  - Both concurrent to this paper
- Subsequent papers, such as Clausing (2020), based on 2017 data

Section 2.1: Country-by-country reporting data

- Description draws primarily on IRS (2019a) for Form 8975
- Covers US persons who are ultimate parent entity of a US MNC group with annual revenue for preceding reporting period of over $850 million
- Filer must list constituent entities, indicating each entity's tax jurisdiction, country of organization, and main business activity
- Financial and employee information for each tax jurisdiction:
  - Unrelated party revenues, related party revenues, profits, income taxes paid and accrued, stated capital, accumulated earnings, tangible assets other than cash
- CBCR includes info on both:
  - income taxes paid (cash basis)
  - income taxes accrued ("total accrued current income tax expense recorded on taxable profits or losses, reflecting only operations in the relevant annual period and excluding deferred taxes or provisions for uncertain tax liabilities")

- CBCR data for 2016 and 2017 published by IRS in December 2018 and December 2019 respectively
- Paper uses 2017 data set, which covers the full population of firms
- 2016 data only a sample: "all figures are estimates based on a sample"; filing not mandatory for first reporting year; only approximately three quarters of all relevant companies submitted
- Empirical comparison of 2016 and 2017 in Appendix Fig. 6: "the 2 years are not very different"


## p. 1525

Notable aspects of IRS CBCR data:

1. Does not include foreign-controlled US corporations (these file CBCR in a foreign country); however, these are included in US Treasury Form 5471 on controlled foreign corporations; will also be in CBCR data for large MNCs headquartered in other countries (published July 2020, OECD 2018)

2. Includes a "stateless" category:
   - Entities without a tax jurisdiction of residence
   - Includes US-organized constituent entities that are fiscally transparent US business entities (perhaps partnerships)
   - Double counting of revenue and profits may occur in this category (IRS 2020)
   - "The stateless category may include any amount of pass-through income which should have been reported in the US or in another country"
   - Similarly to Clausing (2020, p. 39), stateless income is dropped from the analysis

3. Past accumulation of earnings:
   - IRS CBCR includes accumulated earnings (negative for some countries, i.e. accumulated losses)
   - Accumulated earnings overlap with permanently reinvested earnings (PRE)
   - With Public Law 115-97 (the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act), both accumulated earnings and new earnings not taxed on current basis no longer need to be designated as PRE (Joint Committee on Taxation 2018)


## p. 1526

   - PRE = "cumulative amount of foreign earnings designated as 'indefinitely reinvested' for which no accrued income tax expense is recorded on financial statements" (Laplante and Nesbitt 2017)
   - If a firm has sufficient evidence to retain unremitted earnings offshore indefinitely, it may defer recording US income tax expense until repatriated or no longer considered indefinitely reinvested

4. Option of using all MNCs or only those with positive profit before income tax:
   - CBCR (all): better comparable with BEA
   - CBCR* (only positive profits): might suffer from a practice where MNCs prefer to report losses in high-tax countries and profits in low-tax countries; but calculated ETRs are more robust to yearly fluctuations
   - Data with both profits and losses "likely understate profits (since losses are included) and overstate ETRs (since taxes are paid by companies earning profits, typically, though losses are also included in the denominator)"
   - They use both, but adopt CBCR* as standard for comparisons

5. Favourable characteristics:
   - Avoids double counting of related party dividend payments: intracompany dividends unambiguously excluded from revenues
   - Instructions somewhat ambiguous on whether intracompany dividends excluded from profit (Klaassen and Bobeldijk 2019, p. 1061); authors consider it more likely they are mostly excluded
   - Clausing (2020, p. 39) argues companies do not have incentive to overstate income in tax havens since data used for transfer pricing risk assessment
   - Check-the-box election for foreign eligible entities does not affect their tax jurisdiction of residence and thus has no impact on CBCR

- CBCR for US MNCs is a pioneering component of a worldwide effort
- OECD scheduled to publish aggregated CBCR data for large MNCs (Annex C, OECD 2018)
- OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework members agreed to provide three main data tables summarizing CBCR information
- IRS published data using a similar structure (three main tables)
- IRS CBCR "are a good proxy for what might be published for other countries" (main difference: lower number of MNCs and more suppression for confidentiality)
- Two IRS tables contain sectoral breakdowns (IRS Table 1D, IRS Table 2)
- IRS provides information by ETR of MNC sub-groups in IRS Table 3, but lacks overview based on tax rate faced by whole MNCs (as discussed by OECD 2018, expected as OECD Table 2)


## p. 1527-1529 (Table 1)

Table 1 compares data sources relevant for US MNCs with advantages and disadvantages:

CBCR (all) and CBCR* (only with positive profits):
- Advantages: includes all large US-headquartered MNCs; 138 countries (85 in CBCR*); wide range of information; no double counting in revenue and likely not in profit (except stateless); distinguishes related/unrelated party revenues; sectoral composition; option of all MNCs or positive-profit only
- Disadvantages: only one year (2017) with full sample; excludes foreign-controlled US corporations; stateless category not well defined, may include double counting (IRS 2020); might include double counting in profit (intercompany dividends) per Klaassen and Bobeldijk (2019) and Clausing (2020); no revenues by destination of sales; some countries suppressed/aggregated; concerns about how well MNCs fill in forms; data with losses included likely understate profits and overstate ETRs

BEA and BEA 2:
- Advantages: all US-headquartered MNCs; BEA profit = profit-type return minus net interest paid (and BEA 2 = sum of net income and foreign income taxes) avoids double counting; data available many years (most since 1982); sectoral composition
- Disadvantages: only 55 countries; BEA excludes some foreign-to-foreign profit shifting; BEA might not account for untaxed foreign earnings properly; BEA 2 is after tax, not directly comparable; BEA 2 based on immediate investor approach

CFC* (with positive profits):
- Advantages: stratified random sample oversampling large MNCs; includes CFCs owned by persons; foreign corporations >50% controlled by US included; option of all or positive profits
- Disadvantages: no employees or tangible assets info; only 42 countries; available only every two years 2004-2016; some countries suppressed

Eurostat:
- Advantages: all US-headquartered MNCs
- Disadvantages: no tax or tangible assets info; only foreign affiliates in some European countries (30); some suppressed

Compustat:
- Advantages: company-level data; most large publicly listed US companies
- Disadvantages: only consolidated, no unconsolidated; only foreign/domestic distinction

Orbis (all) and Orbis* (only with profits):
- Advantages: company-level data; in theory both consolidated and unconsolidated
- Disadvantages: non-random subsample; lack of unconsolidated data coverage in tax havens (Cobham and Loretz 2014; Tørsløv et al. 2020), leading to underestimates of profit shifting due to highly nonlinear elasticities (Dowd et al. 2017)

- BEA provides several data series for income
- Net income series includes double counting (discussed by Clausing 2016)
- BEA (used in paper): based on profit-type return minus net interest paid; likely underestimates profits
- BEA 2: measure developed by Clausing (2020), calculated as sum of net income and foreign income taxes
- BEA 2 could be adjusted for foreign ownership share (dividing by average parent ownership, similarly to Clausing 2016), but could overestimate profits
- Clausing (2020) prefers BEA 2 and IRS CBCR; Blouin and Robinson (2020) seem to prefer BEA and their newly created series
- Authors use both BEA and BEA 2 alongside other data

- Data sets differ conceptually in sample of companies included
- Figure 1 outlines sample coverage and overlaps:
  - BEA and Eurostat include practically all US MNCs
  - 2017 IRS CBCR includes all large US MNCs
  - IRS CFC includes stratified random sample oversampling large MNCs, of all US persons (corporations, individuals, trusts), similar to Grubert and Mutti (2000)
  - These four sources (BEA, Eurostat, IRS CBCR, IRS CFC) in principle cover all (large) MNCs and do not suffer from sample selection bias
  - Orbis does not guarantee a full or representative sample


## p. 1532

- Data sets also differ in country reporting detail:
  - Orbis reports on all countries/jurisdictions with available info
  - Government-supported data sets (BEA, Eurostat, IRS CFC, IRS CBCR) suppress or aggregate some countries for confidentiality
  - IRS CBCR reports on wider range of countries than BEA, including some small tax havens
- In theory, data sets could be combined (e.g. BEA compared with CBCR; Orbis adjusted for coverage by combining with BEA or CBCR; Orbis used for ownership info with 50% threshold for legal ownership)

- Challenge across all data sets: difference between accounting and tax concepts of profit and tax
- Data sources are based on financial accounting rather than tax accounting
- Data indicate taxes paid according to financial accounting rather than amounts actually reported to a tax authority
- Tax and accounting literature on this: Graham et al. (2012) argue accounting for income taxes is complex because rules governing accounting principles are "sometimes different from those that govern income tax reporting"


## p. 1533

- Book-tax conformity: measure of scale of alignment between tax and financial reporting
- In the US, dual system has enabled firms to simultaneously manage taxable income downwards and book income upwards
- Hanlon and Maydew (2009): under book-tax conformity, the tax base would be broadened
- Hanlon et al. (2008): increasing book-tax conformity could curtail earnings management and aggressive tax planning, but could decrease informativeness of firms' accounting earnings
- With financial reporting data limited to their sources and no tax accounting information, there is a potential bias in tax data they cannot quantify or control

- Bilicka (2019) using UK tax returns data: "the ratio of taxable profits to total assets reported by foreign multinational subsidiaries is half that of comparable domestic standalones"; majority of difference attributable to higher proportion of foreign MNC subsidiaries reporting zero taxable profits; using accounting data leads to "much smaller estimates of the difference"

- Two data sources available only to US government workers/citizens:
  - Confidential firm-level BEA data (Blonigen et al. 2014; Guvenen et al. 2017)
  - Tax returns data from IRS (Dowd et al. 2017; Wright and Zucman 2018)
  - These have advantageous characteristics (full, company-level data) but are unavailable to foreign researchers
- IMF and UNCTAD publish country-pair/country-level FDI data, but these only partly reflect MNC activities (other investments like private equity or collective investment institutions are included); considered impractical for their analysis


## p. 1534

- Two other BEA-related series considered impractical:
  1. Unadjusted net income: known to suffer from double counting (Hines 2010; Wright and Zucman 2018)
  2. Blouin and Robinson (2020) series: excludes some foreign-to-foreign profit shifting; leads to "undesirable observations such as negative income in some tax havens such as Bermuda"
     - Clausing (2020) discusses: BEA researchers hold that Blouin and Robinson series "may place too much of the income where it was earned rather than where it was taxed when shifted among foreign countries, for example, due to hybrid dividends or untaxed foreign earnings"
     - If this diminishes the difference between where income is truly earned and where reported for tax, then it "is likely to underestimate income in tax havens as well as profit shifting"
- Overall: for studying tax havens and profit shifting, net income series and Blouin and Robinson series are less suitable than BEA and BEA 2
- "Any BEA-based series suffer from similar problems and that is the CBCR series are a more suitable alternative"

Section 3.1: Effective tax rates

- Estimated at country level as indicator of taxes paid by MNCs in various countries
- Estimated consistently across data sets
- For Orbis: ETRs calculated using unconsolidated accounts of foreign entities of US MNCs (Garcia-Bernardo et al. 2020); in contrast to ETRs using consolidated company data (Garcia-Bernardo et al. 2019b)
- For each US MNC affiliate in Orbis: divide accrued corporate income tax by gross income; then use weighted average for country-level ETR

Formula:

Effective tax rate_it = Sum(Corporate income tax_it) / Sum(Gross income_it)

- Sum of corporate income tax = sum of unconsolidated taxes accounted for in balance sheets of MNC affiliates in country i
- Sum of gross income = sum of these taxes and unconsolidated net income in balance sheets of MNC affiliates in country i
- This provides weighted average of company-level ETRs of MNC affiliates in country i
- Focus on US (domestic) but also estimate ETRs for other countries
- For Orbis: aggregation from company to country-level done by authors; for BEA, IRS CFC, IRS CBCR: only access to already aggregated country-level data

- ETRs from unconsolidated data enable studying how ETRs differ across countries or from statutory CIT rates
- When compared with CIT, ETRs reveal effect of tax deductions (tax holidays, ad hoc arrangements) and other provisions
- If MNC affiliate uses tax incentives or granted advantageous tax ruling, its ETR will be lower than CIT (Hanlon and Heitzman 2010)
- Offshore leaks (LuxLeaks, ICIJ 2014; Paradise Papers, ICIJ 2017) reveal how MNCs reduce their ETRs

Footnote 1 (p. 1535): Starbucks case study: selected in 2013 by European Commission as one of first state aid cases from an advanced pricing agreement; in 2019 General Court of EU Court of Justice found Starbucks' transfer pricing analysis was reasonable based on Dutch law and OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines at the time (Byrnes 2019)

- Huesecken and Overesch (2015): using diff-in-diff on LuxLeaks cases, firms engaging in advance tax rulings benefit from lower ETRs compared to non-ruling firms

- ETRs differ depending on whether taxes are accrued or paid
- Difference = deferred taxes (accrued taxes not paid)
- US MNCs were taxed on worldwide income but could defer by keeping profits offshore (typically in conduit tax havens)
- IRS CBCR data for US MNCs with positive profits: total deferred of $14.9 billion in 2016, reducing ETR from 19.7% to 16.0%
- For other countries, differences between ETRs using tax paid vs. tax accrued are small (Appendix Figs. 7 and 8), since deferred taxes are small fraction of all taxes
- Small difference might also result from deferred liabilities and assets offsetting when aggregated across MNCs


## p. 1536 (Section 3.2: Misaligned profit)

- Misaligned profit: "shows the difference between the location of MNCs' profits and the location of those MNCs' economic activity"
- Helps shed light on "the upper bound of profits which might be artificially shifted from where economic activity takes place to other countries"
- Draws on Janský (2020) and builds on existing literature
- Tørsløv et al. (2020): ratio of pre-tax corporate profits (net of interest and depreciation) to employee compensation as main profit shifting indicator
- Misaligned profit measure as in Cobham and Janský (2019), which operationalizes OECD's BEPS aim to "better align rights to tax with economic activity" (OECD 2013: 11)

Formula:

Misaligned profit_it = Share of economic activity_it * Total global profit - Profit_it

- Profit = gross profit (sum of net income and income taxes, summed from positive values in company-level data)
- Total global profit = sum of all profit across all countries
- Share of economic activity estimated using revenues, tangible assets, number of employees, wages, or a combination; divided by that indicator's global total

- Enables simulation of profit distribution under formula apportionment / unitary taxation
- Common Consolidated Corporate Tax Base (CCCTB) proposed by European Commission (2011, 2016):
  - Economic activity = 1/3 tangible assets + 1/3 sales + 1/3 split equally between compensation costs and number of employees (i.e. 1/6 each)
- Two notes on operationalisation:
  1. For sales: use info on where sales are reported (not location of final customer), since data only available on the former; CBCR allows unrelated party sales instead of total sales
  2. No employee compensation costs for most data sets: approximate as product of number of employees and GDP per capita (rough but reasonable; see Fig. 9 for comparison with median income per capita)
- Refer to this as CCCTB formula; also provide results for individual components (sales, tangible assets, total assets, employment, compensation)


## p. 1537 (Section 4: Results, Section 4.1: Descriptive statistics, Figure 2)

- Data sets are strongly correlated with each other, but also highlight empirical differences
- Correlation between CBCR, CBCR*, BEA, BEA 2, Eurostat for employees, turnover, tangible assets: very high (71-99%) for all combinations
- Correlation between Orbis and CBCR or BEA: lower (50-77%)
- But Eurostat-Orbis* correlation is very high (97% employees, 96% turnover), indicating Orbis data is good for European countries
- Correlation for profits: lower but still high (62-96% excluding Orbis*), likely because different databases use different definitions of profits
- For taxes: excellent CBCR-BEA correlation (88-100%); Orbis moderate (66-78%)
- IRS CFC vs IRS CBCR: moderate correlation, caused by only four large outliers (Netherlands, Luxembourg, Bermuda, Cayman Islands); without these four, correlation increases to 85-93%
- Since IRS CFC includes foreign entities owned by US persons (corporations and individuals), this indicates foreign entities of non-corporations are more predominant in those four jurisdictions


## p. 1538

- Analysis of correlation between CBCR* and all other data sets (scatter plots in Fig. 10):
  - Most countries lie close to the diagonal
  - Not the case for Orbis, where several countries are covered less thoroughly
  - For tangible assets: some countries have higher coverage in Orbis due to use of fixed assets instead of tangible fixed assets (includes intangibles, overestimates assets in countries with holding companies, e.g. UK, Ireland, Netherlands, Belgium)

- Investigation at regional level (Fig. 11):
  - Orbis* has almost no coverage of most information in Africa, South-East Asia, Latin America, Middle East
  - Orbis* comparable with BEA, BEA 2, Eurostat for most variables for EU member states
  - For North-East Asia, Canada, Australia, New Zealand: Orbis* has approximately 2/3 of info (except Canada where Orbis* has poor data)

- Comparing BEA/CBCR to CBCR*: BEA and CBCR usually contain higher revenues, employees, assets, taxes (expected since both include all companies, CBCR* only those with profits)
- CBCR* uses "unrelated party sales" (not total sales as other sources), so revenues approximately 30% lower
- Increased coverage in BEA/CBCR more marked for Africa, Middle East, CA/AU/NZ, and especially offshore financial centres
- BEA coverage of profits in offshore financial centres: only 5.7% (because "profit-like returns" underestimates shifted profits); for other regions 44-93%
- BEA 2 data partially corrects this: captures 34% of profits in offshore financial centres
- IRS CFC (includes all US persons) generally exhibits higher coverage than IRS CBCR


## p. 1539 (Section 4.2: Effective tax rates)

- Two extra data sets for ETR analysis:
  1. Dowd et al. (2017): micro-data from confidential tax returns; average ETR after discarding 5% most extreme; use 2010 and 2012
  2. CIT collected by Janský and Palanský (2019): statutory CIT rates for 2016

    - Fluctuations in 2017 profits (Austria, Venezuela, Greece are outliers in many comparisons)

Section 4.3: Misaligned profits

- Calculated using CCCTB formula: 1/3 tangible assets, 1/3 revenue, 1/6 number of employees, 1/6 wages
- Figure 4: top 10 countries with highest missing (negative) and excess (positive) profits

- Countries with largest negative misalignment (i.e. more profits should be reported there): Canada, Australia, China, Brazil, consistently across data sets (Fig. 4a)
- USA has the largest negative misalignment (Fig. 13) but excluded from analysis because "2/3 of US multinationals' total activity is domestic, which greatly distorts the total revenues, assets and employee numbers"

- Excess profits more variable across databases (partly due to low data availability in BEA for e.g. Puerto Rico, and Orbis for most countries in the list)
- Clear patterns emerge: Netherlands, UK Caribbean (mainly Cayman Islands and BVI), Bermuda, Switzerland, Ireland, Luxembourg, Singapore, Puerto Rico, Jersey are countries with highest excess profits (Fig. 4b)
- Consistent across data sets except Ireland (BEA and BEA 2 provide much higher excess profits than other data sets)
- Results consistent with Zucman (2014) and Cobham and Janský (2019) which found similar set of tax havens using only BEA data

## p. 1542 (Section 4.4: Relationship between ETR and misaligned profits)

- Test hypothesis: low ETRs correlated with higher excess profits

- Figure 5a: misaligned profit as share of allocated profits using CCCTB formula
  - Countries below zero: missing profits
  - Countries above zero: excess profits
  - Size proportional to total misaligned profits
  - Excess profit in UK Caribbean larger than missing profits in any of Canada, Germany, Mexico or China, despite UK Caribbean having less than 150,000 inhabitants vs. 37-1,300 million in the latter

- Given extreme outliers, tested hypothesis using logistic regression where dependent variable is dummy for positive misalignment (Fig. 5b; Table 4, columns 2 and 4)
- Restricted analysis to countries with absolute misaligned profits above sample mean (22 countries; column 2)
- Key finding: "all (nine out of nine) countries with ETRs below 13% have positive misaligned profits"
- "Conversely, 12 out of 13 countries (all except the UK) with ETRs above 13% have negative misaligned profits"

- Relationship much weaker when regressed on CIT instead of ETR (Fig. 5c, Table 4 columns 1 and 3)
- Expected because "large shares of the missing profits are located in countries with relatively large CITs but also large tax deductions on profits derived from loans and intellectual property, products that are typically used by MNCs to shift profits"

- Also find stronger ETR effect when cube root of misalignment profit is regressed on ETR (OLS, Table 4 columns 5-8), for both 22-country and full sample

Footnote 2: cube root used because misalignment contains extreme values (positive and negative); ensures residuals approximately normally distributed while allowing both signs

- Confidence intervals for ETR coefficient: logistic [−0.67, −0.13]; CIT logistic [−0.19, −0.006]

## p. 1543-1545 (Section 5: Conclusion)

- When effects of 2017 US tax reform (TCJA) are discussed, CBCR data promise to capture effects on profit shifting well
- CBCR data "conceptually superior in some respects, such as country coverage" and "differ substantially when compared empirically"
- Using CBCR: "lower effective corporate tax rates are associated with higher levels of reported profits when compared with different indicators of real economic activity"
- Corresponds to MNCs shifting profits to low-ETR countries without shifting substantive economic activity

- Most important tax havens for US MNCs (high misaligned profits with low ETRs):
  - Bermuda, Ireland, the Netherlands: confirmed across most data sets
  - Also: UK Caribbean, Switzerland, Luxembourg, Singapore, Puerto Rico, Jersey

- Formula apportionment sensitivity demonstrated:
  - If profits allocated by unrelated party revenue: countries where MNCs book sales benefit (Switzerland, Ireland, Singapore, Hong Kong); less disadvantageous for some tax havens (Bermuda, Cyprus, UK Caribbean, Netherlands)
  - While sales measure would favour some tax havens, formula apportionment "might still be favourable to the current status quo"
  - Tangible assets preferred by some tax havens (Luxembourg, Bermuda, UK Caribbean)
  - Agreement on specific formula would be difficult; paper provides quantification of challenges

Policy implications:
- Results timely for debates on introduction of effective minimum taxes on MNCs' profits (OECD 2019)
- In view of low ETRs observed and their negative correlation with misaligned profits, "one form or another of global minimum tax, which is a part of Pillar Two proposal (OECD 2019), might be worth exploring"
- Based on presented evidence, "we have not been able to clarify what form this should take or what effects an implementation of this proposal might have"
- Research "clearly highlights a need for the determination of specific data sources and explicit definition of rates, ETRs or otherwise, as an integral part of future policy agreements"
