# Godar, Aliprandi, Faccio, Janský & Toledo Ruiz (2024) The Long Way to Tax Transparency: Lessons from the Early Publishers of Country-by-Country Reports
International Tax and Public Finance, 31(2), 593–634


## p. 593 – Abstract

- Paper analyses voluntarily published CbCRs of 35 MNEs
- Assesses value added and limitations of qualitative and quantitative CbCR data by comparing to: (1) individual MNEs' annual financial reports, (2) aggregate CbCR data from OECD
- Data quality findings:
  - inclusion of intra-company dividends and equity-accounted profits is a minor concern on average, but can be substantial for individual MNEs
- Sample MNEs seem to pay higher ETRs than global average
- Many report relatively little profit in tax havens
- Only very weak correlation between location of profits and ETRs — may indicate more tax transparent MNEs avoid taxes less aggressively
- Assessment of different tax risk indicators shows important variation between companies
- JEL: F23, H25, H26



## p. 594 – Introduction

- CbCR under OECD BEPS Action 13 seen as major breakthrough for curbing BEPS — provides global picture of MNE tax payments, profits, and economic activities per country
- OECD has made aggregated CbCR data publicly available — used by researchers e.g. to refine profit-shifting estimates (García-Bernardo & Janský, 2021) and to evaluate effects of global corporate minimum tax (Barake et al., 2021)
- Data confidentiality has so far limited firm-level analyses — references Fuest et al. (2022a, 2022b) and Bratta et al. (2021)
- EU decided to make EU-wide public CbCR mandatory starting from first financial year after 22 June 2024 (EU Directive 2021/2101) — until then most company-level CbCRs remain confidential
- Growing number of MNEs voluntarily publish CbCRs
- Two research questions:
  - What is value added and limitations of public micro CbCRs for analysis of corporate tax avoidance?
  - What do CbCRs tell us about individual MNEs' tax aggressiveness, and are there differences vs. world average in ETRs, tax haven use, other indicators?
- Evidence from other voluntary disclosures (Kays 2022, Breuer et al. 2022, Flagmeier et al. 2023, discussed by Hackett & Janský 2022) shows firms act strategically with regard to voluntary disclosure
- Two reasons why public CbCRs may differ from confidential ones:
  - MNEs face reputational risks when perceived as tax dodgers — even possible negative stock market reactions (Müller et al. 2021; Rusina 2020) — so publishers may have less to hide, or hide it too well to be discovered; Adams et al. (2022) find less tax-aggressive firms are more transparent
  - Public CbCRs address larger and less informed audience than confidential ones addressing tax auditors — may favour more careful reporting since public might misinterpret inflated profits from double-counting



## p. 595 – Introduction continued

- Comparison to consolidated financial accounts and OECD aggregate CbCR to highlight benefits and discuss limitations
- Known data limitation: double-counting of dividends — considered an issue in aggregate CbCR but less so in sample of voluntarily published reports
- Also assess to what extent profits of associates and joint ventures bias tax risk indicators — another issue flagged by OECD (2017) — some individual companies explicitly correct for this
- Explore reasons MNEs provide for low ETRs — explain frequently observed gap between financial profits and actual tax base to limited but non-systematic extent
- Qualitative information in many voluntary CbCRs helps understand MNEs' tax haven use and assess correlation between global ETRs and tax haven use
- Compute additional tax risk indicators: share of profits in tax havens; misalignment of profits and economic activity (partly explained by profit shifting)
- Overall conclusion: data quality concerns are valid, some uncertainty remains, but adjustments are gradual and do not undermine qualitative conclusions — a few percentage points ETR difference can matter for individual companies
- Early publishers seem to pay higher taxes than global average; majority reports lower share of profits in tax havens
- Weak correlation of profit location and ETRs for sample as a whole — consistent with some tax-induced profit shifting, but not robust, smaller than studies based on confidential CbCR
- Five companies stand out in terms of tax risks



## p. 595–596 – Literature context

- Best practice in tax avoidance research uses confidential tax returns data — available in USA (Dowd et al. 2017), UK (Bilicka 2019), South Africa (Reynolds & Wier 2019), Uganda (Koivisto et al. 2021)
- Researchers with wider country coverage use: Orbis (Egger et al. 2009; Fuest & Riedel 2012), Compustat (Dyreng et al. 2017; Markle & Shackelford 2012), FDI statistics (Bolwijn et al. 2018; Janský & Palanský 2019), foreign affiliate statistics (Tørsløv et al. 2022)
- No single data source solves the trade-off between quality of confidential data and comprehensive country coverage (Janský 2023)
- CbCR data are "most promising candidates" for addressing this trade-off
- Previously mandatory public CbCR existed only for specific industries:
  - Extractive industries: longest-lasting, may have had an effect (Johannesen & Larsen 2016) but data not very useful (Stausholm et al. 2022)
  - Financial industry: banks and financial institutions required to publish CbCRs since 2016 under Capital Requirements Directive IV — substantial literature (Dutt et al. 2019a, 2019b; Hugger 2019; Joshi et al. 2020; Tuinsma et al. 2023; Barake 2022; Bouvatier et al. 2017; Brown et al. 2019; Fatica & Gregori 2020; Janský 2020)
- Growing literature on voluntary tax disclosure and corporate social responsibility — GRI included country-by-country tax reporting in CSR reporting standard in 2019 (Global Reporting Initiative, 2020)
- Paper "pioneers the use" of CbCR data prepared according to OECD BEPS Action 13 minimum standards and voluntarily published by MNEs



## p. 596–597 – Section 2: Data

- Under OECD BEPS Action 13, governments collect CbCRs from large MNEs — MNEs must report profits, tax payments, and economic activity for each tax jurisdiction
- OECD has made aggregate data publicly available for 2016, 2017, and 2018 only
- Growing number of companies voluntarily publish individual CbCRs
- Data sources used:
  - Public Country-by-Country Reports Dataset of EU Tax Observatory (Aliprandi et al. 2022)
  - Matched consolidated profits from financial accounts (Aliprandi & von Zedlitz 2023)
  - Individual CbCR and financial reports of sample MNEs (for equity-accounted JV/associate profits, business activities, qualitative info)
- Dataset has 103 groups for 2017–2020, only 46 with complete information on variables of interest
- Drop one group (Teck — extremely high total profits in 2020) and one group-year (St. James's Place 2020 — zero employees globally; note: original report no longer available online at time of writing)
- Limit to groups with turnover ≥ EUR 750 million (threshold for legally required confidential CbCR filing) — ensures consistency with OECD aggregate CbCR statistics
- Exclude groups where more than 5% of total profits are reported aggregated by country group (e.g. "other Americas") rather than by individual country
- Final sample: 35 MNEs, collectively reporting activity in 147 jurisdictions
- Geographic breakdown: most headquartered in Europe, five in Australia, one in USA, one in India
- Industry breakdown: manufacturing (9), mining and quarrying (8), transportation and storage (4), information and communication (4)
- Full list of sample MNEs in Appendix Table 7
- Key variables: profit/loss before income tax, income tax accrued in current year, number of employees, tangible assets, unrelated party revenues
- Summary statistics (Table 1):
  - Largest company by employees: Telefonica (~117,000 in 2019), then Vodafone (101,000), Wesfarmers (87,000)
  - Smallest: Erg (778 employees)
  - Highest worldwide profits: Shell and Rio Tinto; lowest positive profit: Enav
  - Shell's 2018 global profits ~360 times higher than Enav's 2020 profits
- Profit distribution reflects MNE heterogeneity — some MNEs concentrate profits in HQ jurisdiction (Iberdrola, Rio Tinto), Anglo American concentrated in Australia, Telefonica in Brazil
- Vodafone: first MNE to voluntarily publish CbCR; also publishes supplementary country-by-country data because it considers OECD minimum standards unsuitable for its objectives (Faccio & FitzGerald 2018) — supplementary data excluded from analysis for consistency
  - Vodafone's argument: OECD report does not explain nature of activities per jurisdiction; their CbCR profit before tax represents "total taxable revenue less expenditure" and includes dividends received (usually tax exempt) and gains/losses on disposal — their voluntary report excludes these, being "more closely related to the amounts on which we pay tax in each jurisdiction" (Vodafone, 2018)

## p. 599–600 – Section 3: Lessons from comparing public micro CbCRs to other data sources

- CbCR data "most promising candidate" for addressing trade-off between quality of confidential tax returns and comprehensive country coverage (Janský 2023)
- Aggregate CbCR used in recent profit-shifting research (García-Bernardo & Janský 2021)
- Confidential country-specific firm-level data used for Germany (Fuest et al. 2022a) and Italy (Bratta et al. 2021)
- Challenges: small number of years available; confidentiality of company-level CbCR; quality issues detailed in OECD disclaimer (OECD 2021)
- Main finding of Section 3:
  - Most sample MNEs exclude intra-company dividends (unlike what aggregate CbCR disclaimers suggest)
  - Some MNEs correct for equity-accounted participation results, especially those with high shares of equity-accounted profits
  - Qualitative information in voluntary CbCRs helps explain low ETRs
  - Important limitations remain but some likely to become less problematic as standard evolves and longer time series become available



## p. 600–601 – Section 3.1: Double-counting of profits

- Profits in CbCR may be inflated by intra-company dividends — earlier OECD guidance did not specify treatment of intra-company dividends for pre-tax profit reporting
- Result: some MNEs include intra-group dividends both in country of origin (as profit) and in receiving country (as dividends) — e.g. dividends received from subsidiary counted as subsidiary's profit AND added to parent's pre-tax profits
- Biases ETRs because tax payments on this income are counted only once (in subsidiary's country), while dividends received by parent are usually at least partly tax-exempt at parent level
- Country-level estimates of double-counting bias (for 2016 aggregate CbCR):
  - Netherlands: ~EUR 5.8 billion or 16% of total profits (positive-profit sample)
  - Italy: dividends = 38% on average (median 28%) of Italian MNEs' reported profits (positive-profit sample)
  - UK: HMRC estimates ~25% of UK-headquartered groups included dividends in CbCR; intragroup dividends receivable = GBP 55 billion or 49% of domestic CbCR profit by UK MNEs
  - Sweden: 2017 tax-free dividends in CIT returns = SEK 266 billion; if all Swedish MNEs included dividends in CbCR profits, total profits of SEK 512 billion should be reduced by 52%
- Many early publishers explicitly state they exclude intra-company dividends (Anglo American 2019a, ENI 2019a, Repsol 2019a, Rio Tinto 2019a, Shell 2019a)
- Confirmed by comparing aggregate CbCR profits to consolidated financial accounts — if CbCR profits exceed consolidated global profit, this may indicate inclusion of intra-company dividends
- In sample: CbCR profits exceed consolidated profits in only few cases; majority of observations have ratio between 0.9 and 1.1 (Figure 1)
- Average ratio = 0.4 (driven by Vodafone large losses); median = 1
- Seven companies assumed to include relevant intra-company dividends (CbCR profits significantly inflated vs. consolidated profits): Atlantia, Orica, Evraz, Piaggio, Buzzi Unicem, Snam, Sol — corrections suggested in Section 4.2
- Low occurrence of double-counted profits in sample contrasts with magnitude in aggregate CbCR — aggregate estimates refer mostly to HQ profits only
- Note: ENI explicitly excludes intra-company dividends but highlights that inclusion would mostly affect Italy (HQ), Netherlands, and UK



## p. 601–604 – Section 3.2: Profits of equity-accounted associates and joint ventures

- Financial accounting allows net profits of JVs and associates to be included in total profits on accrual basis (equity method)
- Problem for CbCR: taxes paid by associate/JV, its employees, and other economic variables are not included in CbCR — only profits are affected
- This biases ETRs and other tax risk indicators — flagged by OECD (2017)
- Netherlands country-specific analysis estimates aggregate positive CbCR profits reported in Netherlands are biased upward by 27% "due to shares of result in associates and joint ventures, differences in accounting standards between the two reports, one-off (de)mergers, takeovers, or disposals" (OECD, n.d.(a))
- In sample: bias from inclusion of JV/associate profits potentially affects 28 out of 35 groups
- Seven groups explicitly exclude income from JVs and associates from CbCR profits: Aegon, Enel, Repsol, Rio Tinto, and South32 (exclude explicitly); Cipla and Enav (do not even include equity-accounted profits in "profit before taxes")
- Average share of equity-accounted profits in total consolidated profits ≈ 13% across sample
- 20 MNEs have equity-accounted profits below 10%; 10 even below 1%
- Groups that do NOT correct their CbCR for equity-accounted income have lower average share (9%); those that DO adjust have higher average share (38%) — suggests high equity-accounted profits incentivise better reporting to prevent misinterpretations (Figure 2)
- Note: Ferrovial has exceptionally high share (63%) among "includers" but does not adjust reported CbCR profits — only highlights this when computing its own global ETR (Ferrovial 2021)
- Country-level breakdown of JV income not always available in annual reports — correction possible only "in an exemplary and non-systematic manner"
- Companies providing breakdown for most important JVs/associates: Anglo American (2019b), Buzzi Unicem (2020), ENI (2019b), Vodafone (2020)
- Examples of distortion:
  - ENI 2017 Spain: less than 100 employees; profit would increase more than tenfold (USD 5m to 76m) if losses from JV Unión Fenosa Gas SA were excluded — but in Italy (>20,000 employees) bias is only 0.1–28%
  - Anglo American Colombia: only 1.5 employees reported; losses would look much bigger after subtracting equity-accounted income from Cerrejón; in Australia, Brazil, South Africa correction would be much smaller (0.3–37%)
  - Buzzi Unicem: only MNE with breakdown of equity-accounted profits by entity where tax haven associate identified (Luxembourg) — but amounts to less than 0.3% of profits reported there
- Overall conclusion for sub-group ETRs: should be interpreted with caution; equity-accounted entity income may bias upward or downward, especially in branches with little economic activity
- No relevant JV/associate income reported in tax havens across sample — so share of profits in tax havens would need to be corrected upward if these profits excluded, as sample MNEs do not report relevant equity-accounted income in tax haven sub-groups; global ETRs also likely need to be corrected upward



## p. 605–606 – Section 3.3: Differences between financial profits and the tax base

- Voluntarily published CbCRs mostly sourced from consolidated financial accounts — so ETRs measure corporate tax payments relative to financial profits, not taxable profits
- Financial profits ≠ taxable profits due to timing differences (different depreciation rules) and permanent differences (some payments deductible in financial accounting but not tax purposes) — Hanlon & Maydew (2009)
- ETRs based on CbCR suffer similar shortcomings as ETRs based on financial accounts
- MNEs not required to publish tax accounting — reasons for discrepancies between ETRs and statutory rates remain "somewhat opaque"
- Loss carryover (likely important share of discrepancies) will be easier to control as longer time series become available
- Netherlands country notes (OECD n.d.(a)) — based on MNEs' tax filings — provide rough estimates of how tax system features contribute to gap between financial and taxable profits:
  - After correcting for double-counting of dividends: subtract additional 19% for loss carry-overs, another 9% for commercial-fiscal differences and non-deductible interest/costs
  - Also subtract part of profit benefiting from IP tax incentives (under Dutch tax system, share of IP profits exempt from CIT base) — but this is a contested correction for inter-country ETR comparisons because taxable profits defined inconsistently across jurisdictions
- Some sample MNEs provide qualitative explanations for low ETRs (possibly linked to GRI standard requiring explanation of ETR differences from statutory rates, note 7):
  - Vodafone (2018): explains historic losses in Luxembourg allowing large amount of income to be offset; also explains UK capital allowance and debt interest relief leading to "little or no UK corporation tax"
  - Rio Tinto (2019a): explains Belgian entities qualifying for Diamond Tax Regime, resulting in ETR lower than general statutory rate
  - Repsol (2019a): computes ETRs for each jurisdiction and provides explanations for differences to statutory rates — tax deductions in Spain, accelerated amortization in Peru, previous year losses in Mexico, tax credits from previous year losses in Luxembourg, non-deductible losses in Bolivia and Netherlands
- This qualitative information is usually "not detailed enough to quantitatively adjust the ETRs"
- ETRs based on financial profits may correctly capture effect of tax incentives (they result in lower tax payments) — but ETRs based on taxable profits would not capture this; individual features like loss carryover, depreciation schemes, and tax incentives can also be used strategically as part of global tax optimisation schemes



## p. 606–608 – Section 4: Tax risk indicators

- Three main tax risk indicators analysed:
  - Activities and profits in tax havens
  - Effective tax rates
  - Misalignment of profits with reported economic activity
- For calculation of tax risk indicators: keep only profitable sub-groups



## p. 607–608 – Section 4.1: MNEs' presence in tax havens

- Aggressive tax planning channels (Ramboll & Corit 2015; Spengel et al. 2016):
  - Via interest payments
  - Via royalty payments
  - Via strategic transfer pricing (intra-group sale of goods or services)
- High-risk functions in tax havens include:
  - Intra-group finance: higher debt → lower taxable profit; intra-group lending allows interest to be received in low-tax jurisdiction
  - IP licensing: IP placed in low-tax location, licensed to high-tax entity in return for deductible royalty payment
  - Marketing hubs: supply chain elements (marketing/logistics management) located in low-tax jurisdictions; remunerated through transfer pricing (mark-up, commission, or share of gain)
  - Insurance/headquarter services: provision from low-tax to high-tax jurisdictions
  - Holding companies: not necessarily for profit shifting but can benefit from preferential tax treaty networks (reduced/exempt withholding taxes on dividends, interest, royalties, services)
- Tax haven list used: Gravelle (2015) plus Belgium, Hungary, Netherlands (added per European Parliament special tax crime committee 2019) — Gravelle's list includes: Andorra, Anguilla, Antigua and Barbuda, Aruba, Bahamas, Bahrain, Barbados, Belize, Bermuda, Cayman Islands, Cook Islands, Costa Rica, Cyprus, Dominica, Gibraltar, Grenada, Guernsey, Hong Kong, Ireland, Isle of Man, Jersey, Jordan, Lebanon, Liberia, Liechtenstein, Luxembourg, Macao, Maldives, Malta, Marshall Islands, Mauritius, Monaco, Montserrat, Nauru, Netherlands Antilles, Niue, Panama, Saint Lucia, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, Samoa, San Marino, Seychelles, Singapore, St. Kitts and Nevis, Switzerland, Tonga, Turks and Caicos Islands, Vanuatu, British Virgin Islands, U.S. Virgin Islands
- Most important tax havens in absolute terms for the sample: Netherlands, Singapore, Switzerland; then Luxembourg, Bahamas — consistent with other analyses (Bratta et al. 2021; Fuest et al. 2022a, 2022b for European MNEs; Clausing 2020 for US MNEs); role of Bahamas is specific to Shell
- 18 out of 35 groups report principal business activities per jurisdiction per OECD CbCR standard
- Groups reporting high-risk activities in top 10 tax havens: Aegon, Anglo American, Eni, Orica, Randstad, Repsol, Rio Tinto, Shell, Vodafone, Wesfarmers
- 8 groups do not report high-risk activities in any top-10 tax haven; 17 groups do not report business activities at all
- Figure 3: tax havens host higher share of high-risk functions; non-havens host higher share of core and other lower-risk activities
- Specific patterns:
  - Group insurance: 12 times in tax havens vs. 5 in non-havens
  - Marketing/trade hubs: 6 times in tax havens, zero in non-havens
  - Intra-group finance: 16 in tax havens vs. 11 in non-havens; most frequently in Luxembourg (Repsol, Rio Tinto, Shell, Vodafone, Randstad)
  - Group insurance most in Bahamas (Repsol, Aegon, Shell, Wesfarmers)
  - Hubs most in Singapore (Anglo American, Rio Tinto, Shell), also Bahamas (Shell), Luxembourg (Vodafone), Hong Kong (Vodafone)
  - IP and holding more equally distributed; Netherlands and Luxembourg most popular for holding (9 and 6 MNEs respectively)
- Company-level analysis: Randstad, Repsol, Rio Tinto, Shell, Vodafone all report more high-risk activities in top-10 tax havens than in top-10 non-havens
- Sample mean share of foreign affiliates' profits in tax havens: 12% (own list) and 8% (Gravelle's list) — slightly lower than 14% and 11% in OECD aggregate CbCR data
- Excluding extractive industries: even lower (9% own list, 7% Gravelle's) — difference not driven by extractive industry overrepresentation
- 30 out of 35 MNEs report lower tax-haven profit shares than aggregate CbCR; only Randstad, Heimstaden, Shell, Vodafone, Buzzi Unicem have higher shares
- Correction for equity-accounted profits does not make big difference on average



## p. 609–613 – Section 4.2: Effective tax rates

- ETR defined as ratio of income tax accrued to profit before income tax, either at MNE level or country level:
  - ETR MNE_i = sum of income tax accrued / sum of profit before tax across all countries j
  - ETR country_j = sum of income tax accrued / sum of profit before tax across all MNEs i in country j
- MNE-level ETRs are weighted averages giving more weight to locations with relatively more profits
- Three alternative ETRs computed:
  - ETR 1: baseline, positive profits only, no adjustments
  - ETR 2: subtract equity-accounted profits for MNEs that do not adjust themselves
  - ETR 3: for seven companies likely including intra-company dividends (Atlantia, Orica, Evraz, Piaggio, Buzzi Unicem, Snam, Sol) — subtract difference between total CbCR profits and consolidated profits from sum of positive CbCR profits
  - Also: adjusted global ETR by subtracting 14.4% from global profits (estimated double-counting in US CbCR per Horst & Curatolo 2020)
- Results:
  - Sample average global ETR = 25% — much higher than global ETR of 14% from OECD aggregate CbCR statistics
  - Only 8 groups in sample have ETR below 14%
  - Result relatively robust to adjustments: equity-accounted correction increases sample mean by 0.5 pp; dividend correction increases by 1.5 pp
- Company-level corrections more visible:
  - Equity-accounted: Astm (37% → 30%), Shell (23% → 26%), Snam (19% → 21%), Wesfarmers (35% → 38%)
  - Dividend: Buzzi Unicem (10% → 15%), Evraz (15% → 28%), Orica (7% → 36%), Piaggio (25% → 42%), Snam (19% → 27%), Sol (19% → 25%)
- Robustness: computed global ETRs for OECD aggregate data for MNEs headquartered in same jurisdictions as sample MNEs — sample ETRs higher in most cases
- Extractive industry bias: ENI and Repsol have high global ETRs partly reflecting high tax payments in resource-rich countries (Libya, Algeria, Egypt for ENI; Libya, Indonesia for Repsol) — special tax regimes (excess profits taxes) in extractive sector: Libya/Norway up to 65%/78% composite nominal rates; Algeria, Angola, Australia, Nigeria also have special oil/gas regimes (EY 2018); Indonesia: calculated based on Production Sharing Contracts
- Non-extractive sub-sample average ETR = 21% vs. 25% full sample — still visibly higher than global ETR from aggregate CbCR
- Negative correlation between share of profits in tax havens and global ETR ranging between -17% and -26% depending on measures used — "relatively high presence in tax havens seems thus to be associated with lower effective global tax rates"
- Outstanding MNEs combining high tax-haven profit share with low global ETR: Buzzi Unicem, Heimstaden, Randstad, Shell, and Vodafone



## p. 613–614 – Section 4.3: Misaligned profits in tax havens

- Misalignment measure: for company i and country j:
  - Misaligned_profit_ij = actual profit_ij − (share of economic activity_ij × global profit_i)
  - Excess profit if actual profit higher than activity-predicted profit; missing profit if lower
  - Relative misalignment = absolute misaligned profit / actual reported profit in each country
- Economic activity measured by employees or tangible assets
- Most sample MNEs report excess profits in tax havens, misaligned with economic activity — extent varies 11–100%
- Mixed picture for Atlantia, Orsted, Sol, Swiss Life, Philips, and Heimstaden
- When measured by unrelated party revenues: misalignment negative for 8 sample MNEs — illustrates sensitivity to activity measure chosen; shows tax havens attract higher share of unrelated party revenues relative to employees and tangible assets; unrelated party revenues may already be over-reported in profit-shifting destinations (Lafitte & Toubal 2022); García-Bernardo & Janský (2021) suggest even tangible assets may be strategically located — US MNEs report second highest value of tangible assets in Europe in Luxembourg
- Sample means suggest profits more aligned with economic activity than global average; individual MNEs clearly above global average
- Negative correlation of misalignment with ETRs (by group and jurisdiction) at 10% significance level for: Ferrovial, Orica, Pearson, Randstad, Sol, Vodafone, and Wesfarmers — implies more likely to report excess profits in jurisdictions where they pay less taxes — "might be a first indication of profit shifting"



## p. 614–618 – Section 4.4: Misalignment and effective tax rates

- Country-level misalignment: compare country's share in total sample profits to country's share in total economic activity (employees preferred as less likely to be strategically located than tangible assets or unrelated-party revenues)
  - Misaligned profit_j = actual profit_j − (share of economic activity_j × total global profits)
- As expected: most tax havens exhibit both excess profit and very low ETR
  - Bahamas, Bermuda, Hong Kong, Luxembourg, Malta, Singapore, Switzerland: ETRs range 0–12%; over 50% of profits seem misaligned
  - Netherlands: average ETR 11%; Austria: ETR 12%
- Resource-rich countries often have both high ETRs and relatively high excess profits (Norway, Nigeria, Libya, Oman with ETRs > 40%) — Figure 7
- Countries with important missing profits: Brazil, Germany, Italy, South Africa, Spain (ETRs 19–36%); also USA (missing profits at low ETR of 12%)
- For total sample: misalignment and ETRs do not seem correlated; however, correlation of -0.3 between relative misalignment and ETRs is significant at 5% level after excluding resource-rich countries (resource rent share > 5% of GDP); no correlation when using tangible assets
- Reconciling excess profits and high ETR in resource-rich countries: high ETR may partly reflect measurement errors (financial vs. tax accounting differences); special tax regimes (excess profits taxes) in extractive sector likely also explain high sample ETRs in resource-rich countries; profits above average per employee may reflect resource rents rather than measurable economic activity — misalignment approach identifies them as "excess profits" but this is "very unlikely to be related to tax-induced profit shifting"



## p. 615–619 – Section 4.5: Semi-elasticities

- Regression analysis to estimate semi-elasticity of reported profits with regard to ETR — as standard in related literature (Beer et al. 2021)
- ETR at country level used to operationalise tax incentive variable
- Dependent variable: log profit of group i in country j
- Specifications:
  - Linear: ln π_ij = β₀ + β₁τ_j + β₂ ln L_ij + β₃ ln K_ij + β_X X_j + Σδ_i D_i + ε
  - Quadratic: adds β₄τ²_j term
- Controls: employees (L), tangible assets (K), country-level: GDP per capita, population size, share of natural resource rents in GDP (World Bank 2022); 34 group dummies (Anglo American as reference)
- Comparison to OECD aggregate CbCR data regression (same structure but headquarter country dummies instead of group dummies)
- Results (Table 5):
  - Linear model: ETR coefficient negative but very small and not significant
  - Quadratic model: negative relationship, but coefficients "small and not very robust" — at ETR of 5%, 1 pp difference between countries explains only 0.025% of difference in reported profits; at ETR of 25%, semi-elasticity only −0.005
  - Qualitatively consistent with Dowd et al. (2017) and García-Bernardo & Janský (2021) — nonlinear relationship implies profit-shifting incentive of 1 pp difference higher at very low ETR levels and approaches zero at moderate ETR levels
  - Employees and assets positively correlated with profits; population negatively; resource rent share positive and significant
  - OECD aggregate data shows more important correlation of profits and country-level ETRs (coefficient −1.68, significant at 0.1%)
- Robustness checks:
  - Group-specific ETRs: slightly negative linear coefficient (−0.01), more negative with quadratic term (−0.03)
  - Interaction of country ETR with extractive industry dummy: not significant; results similar for non-extractive only
  - Nominal corporate tax rates instead of ETRs: no significant results
  - Top 10 biggest groups: similar results, quadratic coefficient slightly higher (−0.05)
  - Non-extractive only: similar results (Table 8)
- Small number and cross-sectional nature of observations do not allow identification of profit-shifting behaviour — "relatively weak correlation profits and ETRs is, however, in line with our previous findings"



## p. 619–620 – Section 4.6: Evaluation of tax risk indicators by company

- Criteria for a very tax-aggressive company:
  - (1) Locates high-risk functions in several tax havens
  - (2) Reports above-average share of profits in tax havens
  - (3) Has below-average global ETR
  - (4) Profits in tax havens not aligned with economic activity; profits correlate with corporate tax rates
- Sub-criteria:
  - 1a: reports high-risk activities in at least one of top 10 tax havens
  - 1b: more high-risk activities in tax havens than in non-havens
  - 2a: above-average share of profits in tax havens (own list) vs. aggregate CbCR
  - 2b: above-average (Gravelle's list)
  - 3a: global ETR below aggregate CbCR mean (unadjusted)
  - 3b: global ETR below aggregate CbCR mean (adjusted for dividends)
  - 4a: profits in tax havens misaligned with economic activity (both employees and tangible assets)
  - 4b: negative correlation of misalignment with ETRs at group level
- Note 17: due to specific extractive industry tax rules, sector-specific ETR benchmarks might be more appropriate (PWC 2015 finds average ETR of global oil & gas sector was 31.5% over 2011–2013, higher than other sectors) — but "comparable consistent and up-to-date benchmarks are not available for all industries represented in our sample"
- Results:
  - Only one sample MNE fulfils all criteria across all operationalisations (Vodafone)
  - Buzzi Unicem: above-average tax-haven profit share (both lists), below-average ETR, misalignment in tax havens — but no significant correlation of misalignment with ETRs
  - Heimstaden: above-average tax-haven profits (own list), below-average ETR, misaligned profits
  - Randstad: more high-risk activities in tax havens than non-havens; above-average profit share (both lists); misaligned profits; profits correlate with ETRs
  - Shell: more high-risk activities in tax havens than non-havens; above-average profit share; misaligned profits — but does not seem to correlate with ETRs
  - Vodafone (most outstanding): more high-risk activities in tax havens than non-havens; above-average profit share; below-average global ETR; misaligned profits; profits correlate with ETRs
  - Orica disregarded even though surpasses three thresholds: ETR only below global average before adjusting for intra-company dividends, and tax-haven profit share is below average



## p. 620–623 – Section 5: Conclusion

- Paper explores voluntarily published CbCRs by 35 MNEs
- Confirms CbCR data need to be interpreted with caution — reporting not uniform; tax risk indicators may be biased by dividends or equity-accounted entity profits
- However, many voluntary publishers appear aware of these risks:
  - Tend to avoid double-counting of dividends
  - Some correct for JV/associate profits
  - For those who do not: equity-accounted profits account for 9% of consolidated profits on average (can be significantly higher for individual companies)
  - Correction effect very moderate — gradual adjustments to individual and average risk indicators
  - Correction for intra-company dividends more important quantitatively, but concerns only 7 smaller MNEs — limited effect on average risk indicators
- Reporting problems likely to be avoided as standard improves, but some conceptual gaps between financial and taxable profits remain
- Loss carryover can partly be addressed by averaging over years; "a certain degree of uncertainty with regard to ETRs seems to be unavoidable as long as MNEs' tax accounts are kept confidential"
- Voluntary publishing of CbCRs "may in itself be regarded as a major step towards greater transparency"
- Main substantive conclusions:
  - Early publishers generally score low on typical tax risk indicators — comparably low tax-haven profits and moderate-to-high global ETRs
  - More frequently locate high-risk functions in tax havens than in other jurisdictions
  - Some degree of correlation between location of profits and ETRs — indication of profit shifting, but correlation coefficients much smaller than other studies when controlling for covariates and not robust across specifications
  - Substantial heterogeneity: only one company fulfils all proposed criteria; five MNEs stand out on at least three out of four criteria
- Two overall conclusions:
  - "Early publishers of CbCRs tend to report better than what quality assessments of aggregate CbCR statistics suggest"
  - "Early publishers of CbCR seem to avoid taxes less aggressively on average than the global average" — plausible because less tax-aggressive MNEs may fear transparency less (in line with Adams et al. 2022); also, less aggressive MNEs may have interest in preventing misleading interpretations by public
- Potential confounding factor: high sample ETRs may result from correct accounting of dividends and JV profits, which may distinguish sample from average MNE in aggregate CbCR
- As EU-wide mandatory public CbCR becomes effective (from first financial year after 22 June 2024), it may become possible to assess differences between voluntary and compelled publishers more systematically
