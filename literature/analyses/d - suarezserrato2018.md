# Suarez Serrato (2019), *Unintended Consequences of Eliminating Tax Havens*

### Page 1

* Motivation: While the debate on profit shifting typically focuses on lost tax revenues (e.g., the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017), this paper argues that policies succeeding in limiting profit shifting also raise the cost of domestic investment and incentivize multinationals to shift real activity abroad.  
* Policy: The paper analyses the repeal of §936, which essentially exempted income from Puerto Rican affiliates of US corporations from corporate taxes. Unlike other tax havens that offer *deferral* of taxes, §936 allowed for the immediate repatriation of income without paying taxes.  
* Primary Finding: The repeal raised the effective tax rate (ETR) of exposed multinationals by 4.65–6 percentage points (pp) relative to other firms.  

* Firm-Level Outcomes: Using firm-level data, the author finds that exposed firms:  
  * Reduced global investment by 10%.  
  * Increased their share of investment abroad by 12%.  
  * Reduced US employment by 6.7%.
  * More exposed local labour markets saw slower growth in employment, persistent for 15 years
  * More exposed places experienced relative decreases in income, wages, home value => more reliant on government transfers
  * Domestic employment and investment may decrease when enforcement efforts limit profit shifting

### Page 2

* Implication: Domestic employment and investment may decrease when enforcement effectively limits profit shifting. Policy analysis must account for these "unintended consequences" alongside revenue gains.  
* Model Logic:  
  * Firms with access to tax havens face a lower effective cost of investing in high-tax countries (like the US) because profits generated there can be shifted to low-tax jurisdictions.  
  * Repealing §936 raised the effective cost of investing in the US.  
  * Slemrod’s Hierarchy: When financial/accounting responses ("paper profits") are restricted by enforcement, firms may resort to real responses (adjusting production/employment).

* Methodology 1 (Firm-Level): A difference-in-differences approach comparing exposed §936 firms to control firms.  
  * Investment: Exposed firms decreased overall investment by 10%. Relative to the ETR change, this implies a semi-elasticity of 1.77.  
  * Employment: Exposed firms reduced US employment by 6.7%.  
  * Parallel Trends: Supported by graphical evidence, robustness to other tax policies, firm fixed effects, and inverse probability reweighting.  

p.3 
* Methodology 2 (Local Labor Markets): Using variation in the density of §936-exposed establishments across US counties.  
  * Increasing exposure (IQR) from 25th to 75th percentile of exposure distribution reduces employment growth by 7.2pp and income growth by 12.5pp.  
  * Auxiliary effects: Declines in housing prices/rents and increases in unemployment benefits ($16/person) and income replacement ($30/capita).

* Robustness of Local Estimates: The parallel trends assumption holds (no pre-repeal correlation). Results are robust to industry-year fixed effects, potential confounders (trade, demographics), and different geographic units (commuting zones).  
* Placebo Tests: Results are not driven by firms with similar characteristics to §936 firms or by the pharmaceutical industry trend specifically.  
* Spillovers: Firm-level layoffs had multiplier effects.  
  * Every §936 layoff resulted in the loss of an additional 3.87 jobs in the local market.  
  * Sectoral Timing: Tradable employment (where most §936 firms are) declined first, followed by non-tradable and construction sectors.

p.4

* Contribution: Challenges the view (e.g., Gravelle, 2016) that profit shifting is purely a revenue issue without real economic distortion.  
* Unique Context: Puerto Rico was a "unique tax haven" due to its institutional link to the US and the zero-tax repatriation benefit.  
  * Evidence shows firms did *not* simply substitute §936 with other tax havens (Garrett and Suárez Serrato, 2019) and firm values dropped upon repeal news.  
  * This lack of substitution makes it a clean case study for *successful* enforcement.  
* Literature: Connects to estimates of profit shifting (e.g., Clausing, 2009 estimates §936 accounted for 10-15% of shifted profits).

2 The Possessions Tax Credit and the Repeal of §936

* History: Originated in the Revenue Act of 1921; formalized as a tax credit in 1976 Essentially exempted Puerto Rican affiliates from US tax.  
* Mechanism: Facilitated profit shifting via intangibles (assigning patents to PR affiliates to receive untaxed royalties) and lax transfer pricing.  
  * Example: Drug patents developed in the US (deducting costs) were transferred to PR (shielding income).  
* Legislative Change: Repealed by the Small Business and Job Protection Act of 1996.  
  * Included a 10-year transition period (phase-out).  
  * Value of credits dropped to 40% by 1998 for many firms.

### Page 7

* Attractiveness of Puerto Rico:  
  1. Strong Governance: US legal protections made it safe for IP.  
  2. Condoned by Treasury: Less risk of audit/aggressive planning flags compared to other havens.  
  3. Repatriation: "Immediate repatriation of income at an effective zero US tax rate." This is distinct from the deferral benefit of other havens.  
* Fiscal Cost: Peaked around 1995  
  * Industry Composition: Chemical manufacturing (including Pharma) claimed 57% of credits. Food/Beverage and Equipment claimed \~26%.  
  * The "deferred" benefit of other havens was shown to be less valuable than repatriation (evidenced by the 2004 tax holiday responses).

### Page 8

* Employment: A small number of firms accounted for massive employment. 682 firms with PR affiliates employed \~11 million workers in the US (NETS data).  
  * Major firms: Abbott, Bristol-Myers Squibb, PepsiCo, GE.  
* Effective Tax Rate (ETR) Analysis:  
  * $ETR_{i,t}^{Repeal}$ remained stable around 27%.  
  * $ETR_{i,t}^{No Repeal}$ (Counterfactual): Absent repeal, ETRs would have *declined* (consistent with general trend in Dyreng et al., 2017).  
  * Result: Exposed firms saw a relative ETR increase of 5.73pp (with behavioral adjustments) or up to 7pp (without accounting responses).  
  * Sector-specific increase: 4.65–6pp.

### Page 9

* Enforcement Success: Evidence suggests firms could not fully avoid the fiscal cost.  
  * No expansion to other havens (Garrett and Suárez Serrato, 2019).  
  * Firm value decreased upon repeal news.  
* Puerto Rico Crisis: The economic crisis in PR began *after* the full phase-out (2006). The cointegration of US and PR economies broke down after 2012, suggesting domestic US effects were not driven by the PR economic collapse itself.

3 A Model of Profit Shifting with Tax Complementarities

* Setup: Firm operates in US (high tax), PR (tax haven), and Country C.  
* Profit Shifting: Firms misreport profitability $r_j$ versus true profitability $\bar{f}_j$.  
* Cost of Misreporting: Convex costs proportional to local capital: $C(r_j, \bar{f}_j, K_j) = \frac{K_j(r_j = \bar{f}_j)^2}{2a}$, high alpha is low misreporting costs
  * *Note:* Real investment in a country lowers the marginal cost of profit shifting ("avoidance-facilitating effect").

### Page 11

* Proposition 1 (Optimal Profit Shifting): Firms over-report profits in low-tax countries and under-report in high-tax countries.  
  * $r_j = \bar{f}_j + a(\tilde{t} = t_j)$, where $\tilde{t}$ is the capital-weighted average tax rate.  
* Proposition 2 (User Cost of Capital = UCC): Profit shifting lowers the UCC in *all* countries.  
  * The "income shifting adjusted user cost of capital" ($\tilde{\rho}$) is lower than the cost of equity ($\rho$).  
  * $\tilde{\rho} = \rho = \frac{a(\tilde{t} = t_j)^2}{2}$.  
  * Intuition: The ability to shift profits increases the return on investment in high-tax countries because the effective tax burden is reduced.

### Page 12

* Proposition 3 (profit shifting generates Tax Complements and subsitutes):  
  * Investment in a high-tax country (US) is decreasing in the tax rate of the tax haven ($t_{PR}$).  
  * Mechanically: Repealing §936 (raising $t_{PR}$) raises the effective cost of capital in the US, reducing US investment.  

"Investment in high-tax countries ($t_j > \tilde{t}$) is decreasing in $t_i$ for all $j \neq i$;
Investment in low-tax countries ($t_j < \tilde{t}$) is increasing in $t_i$ for all $j \neq i$; and, formally,

$$sign \left( \frac{\partial K_j}{\partial t_i} \right) = sign (\tilde{t} - t_j) .$$
To clarify the intuition behind Proposition 3, note that the repeal of §936 can be viewed as Puerto Rico introducing a corporate tax, $t_{PR}$. Differentiating the expression in Proposition 2, we see that repealing §936 would increase the effective cost of capital and reduce US investment:

$$\frac{\partial K_{US}}{\partial t_{PR}} = \underbrace{\frac{a(\tilde{t} - t_{US})}{-f''_{US}(1 - t_{US})}}_{>0} \underbrace{\frac{\partial \tilde{t}}{\partial t_{PR}}}_{>0} = \frac{r_{US} - \bar{f}_{US}}{-f''_{US}(1 - t_{US})} \frac{\partial \tilde{t}}{\partial t_{PR}} < 0 \iff r_{US} < \bar{f}_{US} .$$
In Appendix D, we show that increasing $t_{PR}$ raises the firm's average rate $\tilde{t}$ even after firms reallocate capital across countries. The second equality substitutes the optimal profit-shifting rule from Proposition 1, which shows that repealing §936 would lower US investment as long as profits are under-reported in the US, $r_{US} < \bar{f}_{US}$. We show this graphically in Panel B of Figure 2, where the dot-dashed line shows that a tax increase in a tax haven raises the cost of investing in a high-tax country, $\tilde{\rho}_1$, which lowers domestic investment: $K_j^0 > K_j^1 > K_j^{NPS}$.

7 Conclusion

* Summary: The repeal of §936 successfully limited profit shifting but caused significant real economic declines in the US. Exposed firms cut domestic investment and employment, shifting focus abroad.  
* Regional Impact: These shocks were not absorbed but amplified within local labor markets, causing persistent stagnation in affected regions.  
* Policy Lesson: "Policies that succeed in limiting the use of tax havens are subject to the standard tradeoffs between tax revenue and economic activity."  
* Recommendation: Cost-benefit analyses of international tax reform must include these unintended real economic consequences, not just revenue projections.  
* Context: Puerto Rico's unique status (zero-tax repatriation) meant firms had no close substitute, making this a clear test of enforcement efficacy.
