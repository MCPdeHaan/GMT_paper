## Related literature

p.6
Hines and Rice (1994) data; profit shifting estimated with regression:

log(π_ic) = α + β (T_p − T_c) + δ_firm_i + γ_country_c + ε_ic

where π_ic = pre-tax profits by company i in country c
T_c = tax rate in country c
T_p = tax rate in parent country (or average of foreign affiliates)
Firm and country fixed effects

Positive β is evidence of profit shifting → then extrapolation to global in most papers.

p.7
Problems with this methodology:
- Consolidation profits is reliable but micro data of profits of MNEs in low-tax jurisdictions, since MNEs not required to publish profits country by country.
- Bermuda no data, France nearly no data, US, Ireland, Switzerland no income information.
→ In 2012 only 17% of global profits of MNEs can be traced in Orbis; for 83% subsidiaries were unknown to Orbis.
→ Estimates of β are then downward biased.

p.8
This paper global perspective of profit shifting to see how tax competition redistributes profits internationally.

Accessible data via: missingprofits.world

p.9
Macro profitability ratios:

π = ratio of pre-tax profits to wages. π_f foreign-owned, π_l local-owned. Easy to compute, closely related to factor shares of output, combine well with ETR.

Y = corporate output of country (combining AC and K), at factor costs (thus before indirect taxes).
Y = F(K, AL) = rK + wL, where r includes normal return to corporate capital and above-normal returns.

p.10
α = capital share of corporate output, α = rK / Y. Ratio of operating surplus to wages is α / (1 − α).

Corporations’ net interest p percent of operating surplus rK.
Pre-tax profits = (1 − p) rK.
π = profits made by resident firms per $ of wage paid; wage also includes non-wage employment compensation (SNA code D.1).

π = α / (1 − α) since net interest paid by corporations is small, unless tax haven since they receive net interest from foreign countries → π > α / (1 − α).

Subtract capital depreciation from profits since deductible → Y, α, π are net of capital depreciation.

π = s · π_f + (1 − s) · π_l where s is share of wages paid by foreign firms.

p.11
3 forms of profit shifting:

– Manipulate intra-group export and import prices: subsidiaries in high-tax countries export at low prices to low-tax and import at high prices (→ TP manipulation).
– Intra-group interest payments: high-tax countries borrow money at high interest from affiliates in low-tax countries → higher π_f in tax havens.
– Move intangibles produced/managed in high-tax jurisdictions to affiliates in low-tax jurisdictions, e.g. book due to royalties, interest or payment from final customer.

Which part of profits reported by foreign firms in tax havens is profit shifting versus movement of tangible capital?

Assumption: all countries have same Cobb-Douglas production function (Y = K^α (AL)^(1−α)) and stock of tangible capital does not affect π.

p.12
Countries have constant elasticity of substitution (CES) → effect of capital stock on profits-to-wage ratio is ambiguous.

Contribution of capital intensity to high reported profits-to-wage ratios of tax havens:

π_f = (K / wL)^(1−1/σ) · (1 − e_f)

where e_f = measured return on capital of foreign firms, including abnormal returns due to profit shifting.
σ = capital-labour elasticity of substitution.

p.13
Micro literature finds σ < 1, while macro literature finds σ > 1.

2 assumptions for amount of profits shifted:
– σ = 1, elasticity of substitution between capital and labour equal to 1 → allows for different α between countries.
– Assume tax reported profitability of real firms in tax havens π_l not inflated by profit shifting.

Under these assumptions: excess of π_f over π_l reflects inward profit shifting.
→ In tax havens π_f and π_l are equalised in model.

p.13
Allocate shifted profits: based on all countries having same ETR.

1. Collect bilateral balance of payments data by type of service and FDI income.
2. Allocate shifted profits proportionally to bilateral service export and intra-group interest receipts (sum of these two).

p.14
This follows ALF and subsidiary-by-subsidiary accounting.

2 independent and consistent measures:
– Intra-group interest received by tax havens + amount of services exported by tax havens → $616 billion in 2015.
– Profit shifting by setting π_f = π_l in tax havens → $616 billion in 2015.

p.15
Data sources:

National Accounts: harmonised national accounts that follow 2008 System of National Accounts (UN 2009) → OECD national accounts by sector extend with national statistics offices from non-OECD countries; includes data for profits-to-wage, corporate operating surplus, net interest, wages paid.

p.16
Foreign Affiliate Statistics (FATS): profitability of foreign firms (π_f) based on FATS → if have π and π_f → π_l obtained as residual, since π = s · π_f + (1 − s) · π_l where s is share of wages paid by foreign firms.

– Inward FATS: foreign firms operating in France.
– Outward FATS: affiliates of French firms operating abroad.

EU countries data from 2015 onward, US already from BEA data, but BEA → risk of double counting.

p.18
If publish foreign affiliate statistics, compute π_f based on inward FATS.

If no FATS: impute profits and wages by:

1. Estimate pre-tax profit of foreign firms with amount of inward direct investment income reported in BoP → adjusted based on A3 section related to Wright and Zucman (2018).
2. Estimate wages paid by foreign firms by applying wage/profit ratio in outward FATS of counterpart countries, from US mostly.

p.19
Inward FATS of host countries should be (close to) consistent with mirror outward FATS → generally this holds.

Exception: inward FATS of Ireland, Netherlands, Luxembourg less profit made by US affiliates than in mirror outward FATS of US.

Similar gap: $98 billion in 2015 in bilateral BoP data, less direct investment income paid to US affiliates, reason:

Most likely:
– Ireland, Netherlands, Luxembourg might underestimate profits that affiliates of US firms book in territory. Cause: data issues.
– US overestimate profits booked in Ireland, Netherlands, Luxembourg.

Solution: upgrade inward data of Ireland, Luxembourg and Netherlands to match outward data reported by US (add $98 billion in foreign profits).

Level and Rise of Global Profit Shifting

Profitability in tax havens compared to non-havens: difference in π.

p.23
Tax haven countries highly profitable: Singapore and Hong Kong around profits-to-wage ratio of 100%; Ireland, Puerto Rico and Luxembourg above 200%.

High π driven by high recorded capital shares, not by net interest.
Only 5% of profits shifted come from interest → debt shifting second-order relative to TP and stockpiling location of intangibles.

p.24
Rise of profitability in tax havens over time is not specific to Ireland, since outward FATS show the same.

Profitability local versus foreign firms: compare π_f and π_l for each country.

π is average of π_f and π_l, weighted by share s of wages paid by workers employed by foreign firms and 1 − s.

Finding: high overall profit-to-wage ratio of tax havens are entirely driven by high profitability of foreign firms (high π_f), π_f ratios of 800%, which correspond to capital share of corporate value-added α close to 90% in Ireland and 162% Puerto Rico (capital share 94% and corporate labour share less than 6%).

p.25
High-tax countries: foreign firms less profitable than local firms.

Local firms in tax havens are as profitable as local firms in non-haven countries.

Therefore, π_l ratio of tax havens good reference point to estimate what fraction of large π_f of tax havens attributed to profit shifting.

p.25
Decomposing high profits of haven affiliates: relative contribution of high capital intensities vs profit shifting to high recorded π_f of tax havens:

– Compare firms’ financial capital stocks K relative to wages wL in tax havens and non-tax havens.
– Within tax havens, foreign firms have similar capital stock to local firms.
– Averages show heterogeneity → different capital intensities could explain some of variation in π_l and π_f across countries.

p.26
Haven affiliates of US MNEs have higher capital intensities than non-haven affiliates, but not by great margin.

High rates of return for affiliates in tax havens due to:
– Intangibles booked in low-tax affiliates → lack of data.
– Total capital (tangible + intangible): affiliates can report high profits due to TP manipulation. Macro data cannot explain difference between intangibles and intra-group transfer pricing.

p.27
Some intangibles could be made by R&D personnel in tax havens: FATS US statistics show that 9.8% of R&D personnel was employed in tax havens, concession: most intangibles booked in low-tax countries that have been produced in high-tax countries.

Estimate profits shifted to tax havens: π_f = π_l → assume that without profit shifting foreign affiliates as profitable as local firms → find that more than $616 billion in profits shifted to tax havens in 2015 (close to 40% of MNE profits).

– 55% of shifted profits come from EU, 25% from US.

p.30
