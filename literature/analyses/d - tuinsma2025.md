# Tuinsma, De Witte, Janský, Palanský & Titl (2025) Effects of Corporate Transparency on Tax Avoidance: Evidence from Country-by-Country Reporting
International Tax and Public Finance, 32(5), 1366–1398


## p. 1366 — Abstract

Core question: does private CbCR (introduced 2016 for MNEs with revenue > €750m) reduce tax avoidance?

Method: regression discontinuity design (RDD) around the €750m revenue threshold.

Main findings:
- Overall: CbCR increased ETRs of affected MNEs (i.e. increased tax compliance)
- But the most aggressive MNEs (those with known tax haven presence) were only moderately affected
- Effect concentrated in "medium-aggressive" firms: those with low ETRs but no tax haven affiliates to shift profits to
- Policy thus effective against some avoidance, but profit shifting to tax havens remains an issue
- This explains the push for further measures: Global Minimum Corporate Tax Rate (GMT/Pillar Two)

JEL codes: F23, H25, H26.



## p. 1367 — Section 1: Introduction

Scale of the problem: estimates suggest approximately 10–30% of MNEs' profits shifted to tax havens, "amounting to hundreds of billions of dollars worldwide" (citing Blouin & Robinson 2023; Clausing 2016; Janský & Palanský 2019; Tørsløv et al. 2023). Tax revenues lost due to profit shifting are increasing, with significant negative consequences for national budgets and shown to increase global cross-country inequality (Bilicka 2022; OECD 2015; Wier & Zucman 2022).

CbCR: Country-by-Country Reporting, EU Directive 2016/881/EU. Implemented by the OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework on BEPS (58 countries at time of writing, as of November 2023 grown to 145 countries). Applied from 2016. Requires all MNEs with annual revenue ≥ €750 million to report financial information to tax authorities on a country-by-country basis. Information shared automatically between tax authorities, but not made publicly available at the company level (footnote 2: EU agreed to implement partially public CbCR for all companies starting in 2024; previously already public for banking sector and extractive/logging industries, see European Commission 2021). CbCR increases tax authorities' understanding of where MNEs book profits vs. where economic activity is located (Garcia-Bernardo et al. 2021a). Mechanism: increased detection probability of profit shifting → disincentive to engage in such strategies.

Research question and design:
- Does CbCR affect MNE tax avoidance behaviour, and do MNEs respond differently depending on the aggressiveness of their avoidance practices?
- Data: Bureau van Dijk's Orbis database, EU-headquartered MNEs with revenues around the €750m threshold
- Method: sharp RDD around this threshold
- Finding: significant increase in ETRs of ca. 6 percentage points (>20% relative effect)
- In absolute terms: yearly additional tax revenue from private CbCR estimated at around €26 billion (footnote 3: back-of-envelope: before-tax profits of treated firms over 3 years = €1.3 trillion; 6% of that = €78 billion over 3 years = €26 billion/year)
- Authors caution against over-interpretation of the exact point estimate

Contribution: documenting heterogeneity of CbCR's effects across firms. Novel theoretical model + empirical analysis.

Three aggressiveness categories defined:
- Most aggressive: low pre-CbCR ETRs AND tax haven affiliates (profit shifting)
- Medium-aggressive: low pre-CbCR ETRs but NO tax haven affiliates (other forms of tax optimisation)
- Non-aggressive: above-median ETRs

Key heterogeneity finding: almost all of the overall CbCR effect is concentrated in medium-aggressive MNEs. Most aggressive firms show only a limited increase in ETRs (low statistical significance). No effect for non-aggressive firms (as expected).



## p. 1368 — Section 1 (continued): Related literature and contribution

Related literature:
- CbCR in extractive sector: Johannesen & Larsen (2016) find decrease in firm value for European gas/oil/mining companies after public CbCR, suggesting transparency can reduce rents from tax evasion
- CbCR in banking sector (EU Capital Requirements Directive IV, 2014): Overesch & Wolff (2021) find increase in ETRs for affected banks, mainly driven by banks with tax haven operations; Joshi et al. (2020) find public CbCR deterred tax-motivated income shifting but did not affect overall tax avoidance of banks

Important distinction: prior public CbCR studies cannot disentangle effects of information disclosure to authorities vs. public scrutiny. The current paper studies private CbCR, so any effects can be attributed exclusively to information disclosure to tax authorities. Also first cross-industry CbCR study.

Three closely related papers:

1. Joshi (2020): also studies private CbCR effects using RDD and DiD. Difference: this paper shows empirically that anti-avoidance effects are concentrated in medium-aggressive MNEs while most aggressive remain unaffected. Very different policy conclusion: "private CbCR in its current form has not deterred profit shifting by the most aggressive MNEs, and...



## p. 1369 — Section 1 (continued): Related literature and policy implications

...that public availability of CbCR data at the company level is more likely to achieve that goal."

2. Hugger (2024): DiD setting, finds average ETR increase of about 1 percentage point, and some evidence for bunching just below the threshold. This paper adds: significant heterogeneity depending on tax aggressiveness leads to very different conclusions about policy effectiveness. Also: an RDD is appropriate here if bandwidths are small enough and control group excludes non-MNEs (unlike Hugger's approach). Using the correct RDD, the authors cannot replicate evidence for bunching/avoidance of reporting threshold.

3. De Simone & Olbert (2022): CbCR effects on capital and labor investments, finding MNEs close tax haven subsidiaries but increase real activity in European low-tax jurisdictions. Focus on real effects at subsidiary level; does not study MNE-group-level heterogeneity. This paper uses ETRs at ultimate owner level for a more direct measure of tax avoidance and to study heterogeneity.

Policy implications of this paper's heterogeneity findings:
- Prior research (Joshi 2020) argues private reporting may be sufficient and public CbCR not necessary
- This paper shows average improvement in tax compliance may mask significant heterogeneity
- Private CbCR with "insufficient changes in the cost-benefit analysis of tax avoidance" may even increase inter-firm inequality (only improving compliance in firms that were not the least compliant)
- The paper provides an argument to proceed with public CbCR to increase detection risks further
- Policy design lesson: consider whether and how the reform targets the most aggressive tax avoiders
- Researchers should acknowledge that reforms with small deterrence may not affect worst offenders

EU implemented public CbCR in 2024. US Disclosure of Tax Havens and Offshoring Act would implement public CbCR there. Australia publishing CbCR at company level from July 2024.



## p. 1370 — Section 2: Institutional Background

BEPS (Base Erosion and Profit Shifting): MNEs exploit gaps and mismatches in different countries' tax systems to lower global tax burden, shifting profits away from high-tax to low-tax jurisdictions. OECD/G20 BEPS project and Action Plan (OECD 2013). Final BEPS report: 15 Actions; 4 are minimum legal requirements for countries joining. As of November 2023: 145 countries joined, including major offshore financial centers.

CbCR = BEPS Action 13 (one of the four minimum standards). Objective: "provide tax administrations with a high-level overview of the operations and tax risk profile of the largest multinational enterprise groups" (OECD 2020, p. 11). Using this information, tax authorities can assess transfer pricing risks and determine audit strategies (footnote 7: OECD Handbook on Effective Tax Risk Assessment lists specific "tax risk indicators that may be detected using information contained in CbC Reports", e.g. jurisdictions with little economic activity but significant profits). Higher perceived detection risk → indirect pressure to refrain from aggressive tax planning.

CbCR requirements:
- MNEs must provide all relevant governments with information on global allocation of income, economic activity and taxes paid, by country
- Filed in country of ultimate parent
- Shared through automatic exchange of information agreement (footnote 8: not all countries signed; but all EU countries have, so for sample of EU MNEs this is not an issue)
- Three tables: (1) financial information at country level for every country of activity (revenues, profit/loss before income tax, income tax paid, income tax accrued, capital, accumulated earnings, number of employees, tangible assets); (2) listing of all subsidiaries with tax jurisdiction and main business activities; (3) additional information/comments

EU legislative basis: Directive 2016/881/EU amending Directive 2011/16/EU (mandatory automatic exchange of information in field of taxation). The 2011 Directive set rules for automatic exchange of residents' income information, but did not yet require MNEs to report global activities by country. 2016 amendment inserted this obligation.

CbCR requirements in EU: from fiscal years starting 1 January 2016. Apply to MNEs with consolidated revenue ≥ €750 million. This excludes 85–90% of MNEs by number, but affected MNEs represent approximately 90% of corporate profits (OECD 2015).

Information was entirely new for most EU tax authorities: before CbCR, MNEs only reported local taxes to local authorities; under CbCR, authorities receive for the first time country-level information on all operations in other jurisdictions including geographic breakdown of profits and taxes paid. OECD (2017): "this information has never previously been available to tax authorities and represents a great opportunity for tax authorities to understand the structure of a group's business in a way that has not been possible before." Note: in the US, the IRS already required US MNEs to disclose foreign operations pre-CbCR, so CbCR may provide less incremental information there (Nessa et al. 2024) — another reason to focus on EU.

Prior sector-specific CbCR regimes:
- Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative: public CbCR for extractive sector
- EU Capital Requirements Directive IV (2014): public CbCR for multinational banks
- These differ from BEPS CbCR in objective (preventing corruption, not tax transparency) and in their public nature
- Since banks and extractive firms were already subject to CbCR, they are excluded from the analysis



## p. 1372 — Section 2 (continued): Move towards public CbCR and GMT

Future developments:
- EU approved public CbCR legislation in 2021, entering into force 2024 (European Commission 2021) — will provide additional scrutiny from public, shareholders, journalists, academics
- US Disclosure of Tax Havens and Offshoring Act passed House of Representatives in 2021
- Australia: publicly available CbC reports from July 2024

Minimum tax:
- OECD and Biden administration introduced plans for a minimum effective corporate tax rate for MNEs (OECD 2021b; US Dept. Treasury 2021)
- A minimum tax rate "is capable of decreasing the incentives of MNEs to use tax havens, especially the ones with tax rates below the set minimum"
- Hugger et al. (2024): GMT may decrease profit shifting by half
- Johannesen (2022): theoretical model in which GMT creates incentives both for raising and lowering tax rates in non-havens, with ambiguous welfare effects in non-havens



## p. 1373–1374 — Section 3: Methodology / Section 3.1: Model

Overall structure of methodology section:
- First: theoretical model of MNE tax avoidance choice based on Allingham & Sandmo (1972)
- Then: graphical illustration of heterogeneous effects
- Then: data description
- Then: identification strategy

Section 3.1 Model:

MNEs decide at group level on tax avoidance through a cost-benefit analysis. Theoretical innovation: incorporates corporate social responsibility (CSR) dimension. MNEs differ in degree to which they value pure profit-maximisation vs. broader social welfare (Graafland 2002; Kolstad 2007). Empirical evidence: firms less engaged in CSR have higher likelihood of tax avoidance (Hoi et al. 2013; Kovermann & Velte 2021).

Aggressiveness (A_i): a discount factor ranging between 0 and 1. High aggressiveness = preference for pure after-tax profit-maximisation without regard for social principles. Low aggressiveness = mixed preference. Assumed to be a "time-invariant firm characteristic" (factors driving heterogeneity: cultural norms, industry sector, institutional backgrounds, management practices; e.g. Dyreng et al. 2010, Lanis et al. 2017 find relationship between tax aggression and executive/board characteristics).

Perceived benefit from avoiding t_i taxes for firm i:
Payoff_i = t_i · A_i (equation 1)

Two types of costs: (i) tax planning costs C(t_i) with increasing marginal costs (C' > 0, C'' > 0; Mills et al. 1998; Slemrod 2001); (ii) risk of detection followed by monetary sanction S(t_i) (S' > 0, S'' ≥ 0). Sanction includes repayment of tax + potential fine + costs of audits + reputational costs. Perceived detection probability p(t_i) is increasing and non-convex (p' > 0, p'' ≥ 0).

Expected utility:
E[u_i | A_i] = (1 - p(t_i)) · t_i · A_i - p(t_i) · S(t_i) - C(t_i) (equation 2)

First-order condition (equation 3) and resulting optimality condition (equation 4):
A_i = [p'(t_i)S(t_i) + p(t_i)S'(t_i) + C'(t_i)] / [1 - p(t_i) - p(t_i)t_i]

More aggressive firms avoid more taxes (numerator of equation 4 strictly increases in t_i, denominator strictly decreases). Corner solutions possible: low-A firms may have t*_L = 0 (no avoidance); high-A firms may have t*_H = T_H (maximum avoidance).

Effect of CbCR: increases perceived detection probability from p to p_cbcr > p for all firms, and may increase tax planning costs from C to C_cbcr ≥ C. Post-CbCR optimality condition (equation 5) leads to lower optimal tax avoidance t*_i,cbcr < t*_i for interior solution firms. However, corner solution firms may remain unaffected: most aggressive firms may still find that A_H ≥ the right-hand side fraction at their previous maximum avoidance level, meaning CbCR "did not increase the detection probability and tax planning costs sufficiently to change the outcome of their cost-benefit analysis of tax avoidance."

Key theoretical novelty: "Such potential heterogeneous effects of anti-tax avoidance policy that does not sufficiently increase the perceived costs of tax avoidance for all firms, have not been theorized before."

Implications for further policies (derived from the model):
- Public CbCR: may raise p(t_i) and increase S(t_i) through reputational damage and consumer reactions. May affect most firms previously avoiding taxes, but depending on size of increase, most aggressive firms may still stick to high avoidance. Evidence on reputational costs is mixed: Dyreng et al. (2016) find public pressure changes cost-benefit outcomes; Graham et al. (2014) find perceived reputational costs play a role; but Gallemore et al. (2014) unable to find empirical reputational effect; Asay et al. (2024) unable to find evidence of consumer boycotts from negative tax news.
- Global minimum tax: decreases benefits of avoidance by "raising the floor" of race to the bottom. Decreases T_H (maximum possible tax savings). Will reduce avoidance levels of all firms with previous optimal avoidance between old and new corner solution.



## p. 1376 — Section 3.2: Example: Graphical illustration (Figure 1)

Linear example with costs and benefits of tax avoidance both linearly increasing in t, but cost curve with larger slope. Cost curve is independent of aggressiveness A_i. Benefits curve plotted for A_L < A_M < A_H.

Results in Figure 1 (notes from figure description):
- Low aggressive firm (A_L): costs always exceed benefits → no tax avoidance
- Medium aggressive firm (A_M): interior solution at intersection point B (before CbCR) → optimal avoidance t*_M
- High aggressive firm (A_H): benefits always exceed costs → maximum avoidance t*_H = T_H

After CbCR (cost curve shifts up for all firms):
- Low aggressive: still no avoidance
- Medium aggressive: new intersection at A (lower level) → t*_M,cbcr < t*_M
- High aggressive: cost curve not raised high enough to intersect benefits curve before T_H → still avoids maximally



## p. 1376–1377 — Section 3.3: Data

Source: Bureau van Dijk's Orbis Historical database, detailed firm-level yearly consolidated financials and ownership data, 2010–2018 (6 pre-reform years, 3 post-reform years).

Sample: EU-headquartered MNEs. EU focus justified because: (i) all EU member states implemented CbCR simultaneously (1 January 2016) with the same €750m cutoff; (ii) better Orbis data availability for European MNEs; (iii) pre-CbCR and post-implementation tax and legal systems more comparable within EU (Joshi 2020; Nessa et al. 2024).

Exclusions: financial sector and extractive sector (subject to form of public CbCR prior to BEPS). Loss firms dropped. ETR winsorized at 0 and 1. Other variables winsorized at 1st and 99th percentile.

Final dataset: 12,946 firms (unbalanced), 54,017 firm-year observations.

Ownership data: from Orbis Historical database, GUO50 link used (footnote 10: Global Ultimate Owner identified for companies with ownership ≥ 50%, ensuring sufficient control for tax planning). Used to: (i) exclude companies with no known foreign subsidiaries (may not be MNEs and may respond differently to CbCR); (ii) construct tax haven dummy.

Tax haven classification: follows De Simone & Olbert (2022), combining Bennedsen & Zeume (2018) list, Tax Justice Network blacklist of European preferential tax regimes, and "big seven" tax havens of Hines & Rice (1994) plus Puerto Rico (see Table 3 in appendix). Robustness checks with Garcia-Bernardo et al. (2021b) and Corporate Tax Haven Index. Note: contemporary ownership used for better data availability; results robust to using 2015 (pre-CbCR) ownership instead (footnote 11). Limitation acknowledged (footnote 12): Orbis may not observe all affiliates → some highly aggressive firms may appear less aggressive → conservative aggressiveness indicator. Coverage improved recently (Olbert et al. 2024). List includes larger European tax havens (Ireland, Netherlands, Luxembourg) with better Orbis coverage.

Treatment/control group: control = MNEs with < €750m revenue; treatment = MNEs with ≥ €750m. About one fifth of observations constitute the treatment group (Table 4, Panel B). Average ETR slightly lower for treatment group (larger firms have more means to decrease tax base). Both groups show declining ETRs over time (race to the bottom in statutory rates), but treatment group saw smaller decline — initial indication that CbCR may have increased compliance. Treated firms larger on average in terms of revenue, number of tax haven subsidiaries, and assets. Return on assets and leverage similar for both groups.



## p. 1379–1381 — Section 3.4: Identification strategy

Primary outcome variable: consolidated effective tax rate (ETR), defined as taxation / pre-tax profits. Justification: widely accepted proxy for corporate tax planning (Dyreng et al. 2010; Joshi 2020; Overesch & Wolff 2021). Consolidated ETR captures total tax expense relative to worldwide pre-tax income: "a comprehensive perspective on a firm's tax burden." Unlike single-jurisdiction measures or book-tax differences, it aggregates cross-border outcomes. Captures non-conforming tax avoidance (such as profit shifting to low-tax jurisdictions).

Important limitation of ETR: does not capture conforming tax avoidance (in which taxable income itself is manipulated; Hanlon & Heitzman 2010). Also affected by legitimate tax features: R&D credits, sector exemptions, depreciation rules (Schwab et al. 2022). However, these ETR determinants "are not systematically related to whether a firm is just below or just above the country-by-country reporting threshold" — so using ETR as a proxy remains appropriate in the RDD setting.

Identification: sharp RDD around the €750m revenue threshold (footnote 13: treatment is technically the reporting obligation, not actual reporting; non-compliance also attracts tax authority attention so cutoff is treated as sharp; no other BEPS actions use this threshold within the sample period — no confounding factors).

Identifying assumption: no manipulation of revenue to fall just below the threshold. Healy & Wahlen (1999) find little evidence for widespread income manipulation. Distribution of revenue shows no sorting in the data (Fig. 4, Panel A). Formal test: McCrary (2008) and Cattaneo et al. (2018) local polynomial density test. Result: overlapping 95% confidence intervals around the threshold → smooth distribution → identifying assumption not invalidated. Consistent with Joshi (2020) and De Simone & Olbert (2022) who also do not find bunching. Hugger (2024) does find a discontinuity, but in a wider scope including non-MNEs and non-European MNEs.

Bandwidth choice: MSE-optimal bandwidth (Cattaneo et al. 2019). Trade-off: smaller bandwidth reduces misspecification error of local polynomial approximation but increases variance. Robustness checks with other bandwidth sizes provided.

Specification: non-parametric RDD with local linear polynomial regressions, triangular kernel (more weight to observations close to threshold). Bias-correction following Cattaneo et al. (2018). Standard errors clustered at MNE level. Controls not necessary for consistency in RDD setting (quasi-random treatment assignment), but included to reduce sampling variability (Lee & Lemieux 2010): size, return on assets, leverage. Fixed effects: year, headquarter country, country-year, industry, and firm FE. Country-year FE absorb changes in statutory tax rates.

Aggressiveness categorisation (for cross-sectional analysis):
- Two dummy variables: (1) pre-reform ETR below country-year median in at least 2 out of 3 pre-CbCR years; (2) whether MNE has affiliates in tax havens in Orbis
- Most aggressive: low pre-CbCR ETR AND tax haven affiliates
- Medium-aggressive: low pre-CbCR ETR but NO tax haven affiliates (e.g. only economically active in headquarter country and a low-tax neighbouring country that is not a tax haven; footnote 18 gives concrete illustration)
- Non-aggressive: above-median ETR (independent of tax haven presence)
- Non-aggressive group split into with/without tax haven affiliates to create four roughly equal subsamples, but results same when combined
- Robustness: results robust to using mean ETR instead of median (footnote 16)
- Limitation: Orbis may not observe all affiliates, so highly aggressive firms may appear less aggressive — "conservative indicator" (footnote 17)

## p. 1383–1385 — Section 4.2: Cross-sectional variation

Sample splits by aggressiveness category. Four groups based on pre-CbCR ETR × tax haven presence.

Table 2 (main heterogeneity results):

- Column (1): High pre-CbCR ETR, no tax haven — coefficient -0.00811 (SE 0.0150), insignificant. No effect, as expected.
- Column (2): High pre-CbCR ETR, tax haven present — coefficient -0.00537 (SE 0.0142), insignificant. No effect, as expected; high ETR indicates tax haven presence more likely reflects real economic activity.
- Column (3): Low pre-CbCR ETR, no tax haven (medium-aggressive) — coefficient 0.0618*** (SE 0.00656), significant at 1%. CbCR increased detection risk sufficiently to offset benefits of avoidance.
- Column (4): Low pre-CbCR ETR, tax haven present (highly aggressive) — coefficient 0.0155* (SE 0.00879), significant at 10% only. Benefits of profit shifting still outweigh risks.

Key observation: cross-sectional variation is "not driven by MNEs closing their tax haven affiliates" (footnote 21): within the highly aggressive subsample, only 5 MNEs closed tax haven affiliates between 2015 and 2016 (2 treatment, 3 control); 30 MNEs in main sample had fewer tax haven subsidiaries in 2016 vs. 2015 (9 treatment, 21 control). No evidence CbCR caused closures.

Robustness of heterogeneity:
- Tables 11, 12, 13: conclusion holds with specifications without covariates/FE, with only covariates, or with only FE
- Table 14: robustness to alternative tax haven definitions (Garcia-Bernardo et al. 2021b and Corporate Tax Haven Index with cutoff score 70). Using the CTHI, no significant increase in ETRs even found for most aggressive firms.

Summary: "while CbCR did have a positive overall effect on tax compliance, there is significant cross-sectional variation. The most aggressive MNEs mostly continued to shift their profits to tax havens. Most of CbCR's effectiveness is concentrated in firms that are likely to have previously avoided taxes but without shifting profits to affiliates in tax havens."



## p. 1385–1386 — Section 5: Conclusion and Discussion

Summary of paper: private CbCR in EU (2016) led to an increase in ETRs overall, but concentrated in medium-aggressive MNEs (low pre-CbCR ETR, no tax haven affiliates). For these firms, CbCR increased detection risk sufficiently to offset the benefits of avoidance. Most aggressive MNEs (tax haven presence + low pre-CbCR ETR) only minimally affected.

Comparison with Joshi (2020): both find that private CbCR deters general tax avoidance. Joshi concludes public CbCR therefore not necessary. This paper disagrees: private CbCR "may not be sufficient to fight tax avoidance, since the most aggressive MNEs were not affected." Private CbCR "did not sufficiently increase the expected cost of profit shifting to offset its benefits."

Forward-looking notes in the conclusion:
- Public CbCR (EU from 2024): extends existing reporting obligation, further increases detection risks and costs. Whether this is sufficient to deter aggressive strategies requires additional research.
- US Disclosure of Tax Havens and Offshoring Act: additional scrutiny channel; increases MNE accountability to the general public.
- Global minimum corporate tax rate (OECD proposal, Biden administration): estimated to decrease benefits of profit shifting (Hugger et al. 2024).

Footnote 22 (important caveat): ETRs only capture non-conforming tax avoidance. Medium-aggressive MNEs may have substituted conforming for previous non-conforming practices. If so, heterogeneity findings are "not as strong, but would strengthen our conclusions about the lack of effectiveness of private CbCR in curbing overall tax avoidance."
