# Van 't Riet, Brun, Pycroft & Stöhlker (2025) A – Potentially Positive – Welfare Assessment of the Global Minimum Tax
CPB Discussion Paper 462



## Abstract (article p. 1)

- Paper assesses the welfare implications of the GMT on corporate income using a multi-country macroeconomic model (CORTAX)
- Stated objectives of GMT: mitigate harmful tax competition and curb wasteful profit shifting
- Theoretical literature suggests welfare effects of GMT are "ambiguous": positive through revenue gains and limited profit shifting, but potentially negative because higher effective taxation raises firms' capital costs, dampening economic activity
- Paper claims to produce "the first comprehensive and quantitative welfare assessment of the GMT"
- Simulation scope: GMT of 15% implemented by all 27 EU MS, the US, UK, Japan, and a generic tax haven
- Two scenarios (differing in how additional CIT revenues are redistributed):
  1. Transfer closure: additional CIT revenues redistributed as direct lump-sum transfers to households; mixed welfare results across countries; slightly positive global welfare overall
  2. CIT rate adjustment closure: additional CIT revenues redistributed back to firms as lower CIT rates (provided rate remains at or above GMT rate); in most countries the reduction in cost of capital from lower CIT more than offsets the increase from reduced profit shifting; welfare outcomes widely (though not universally) positive; modest increase in global welfare
- Both scenarios satisfy the Hicks-Kaldor compensation test, indicating a potential Pareto improvement (overall gain positive, so winners can compensate losers)
- Optimal GMT rate analysis: 16% yields highest level of global welfare in the model (under CIT rate adjustment scenario); 18% under transfer closure


## Section 1: Introduction (article pp. 2–4)

### Profit shifting and the context of the reform

- Profit shifting: MNEs exploit presence in multiple jurisdictions by reporting more profits in countries with lower tax rates
- Lower-tax jurisdictions benefit by receiving some revenue and transaction fees from profits generated elsewhere
- Practice leads to inefficiencies and distortions in global economic activity

### OECD/G20 reform

- October 2021: OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework agreed to comprehensive reform based around two pillars
- Pillar One: grants taxing rights on income of highly profitable businesses to market jurisdictions
- Pillar Two: establishes 15% minimum ETR for MNEs with consolidated global revenues exceeding EUR 750 million (= GMT)
- Progress on Pillar One has stalled
- Since 2024, several jurisdictions have begun implementing Pillar Two rules
- Adopted by EU MS and over thirty other jurisdictions, including UK and Japan
- Pillar Two requires net corporate profits of in-scope MNE to be subject to minimum ETR of 15% in each jurisdiction where it operates
- System of top-up taxes grants taxing rights to multiple jurisdictions (rule order, footnote 3):
  1. First: jurisdiction where profits are reported, through a Qualified Domestic Top-Up Tax (QDMTT)
  2. If first jurisdiction does not levy sufficient taxes: jurisdiction where MNE is headquartered, through Income Inclusion Rule (IIR)
  3. If neither sources impose sufficient taxation: all jurisdictions where MNE operates can claim a share of undertaxed profits through the Under-Taxed Profits Rule (UTPR) — this serves as a safeguard against non-implementing countries

### Existing empirical literature

- OECD (2020): used aggregate CbCR data from 2016, later updated to 2017-2020 (Hugger et al., 2024); estimates combined effects of top-up taxes and reduced profit shifting could raise CIT revenues globally by 6.5% to 8.1%
- EU Tax Observatory (Baraké et al., 2022): potential CIT revenue increase of up to 16% for EU MS, but only considers partial implementation (only IIR, no QDMTT, footnote 4) and does not account for changes in profit shifting behaviour

### Key theoretical papers

- Johannesen (2022): welfare effects for non-tax-haven jurisdictions are ambiguous; tax havens benefit unambiguously; long-run Nash equilibrium where MNEs maximise profits and national governments optimise welfare; tax havens endogenously respond to GMT by setting rate to exactly GMT rate
- Hebous and Keen (2023): GMT could lead to potential Pareto improvement under specific conditions

### Brun et al. (2025) — predecessor study

- Uses calibrated general equilibrium model (CORTAX) to produce quantitative long-term revenue estimates
- Explicitly models all EU MS, Japan, UK, US, and a generic tax haven
- Estimates that implementing GMT results in a 7% increase in CIT revenues for EU MS
- Suggests whether the USA opts in or out of GMT is of second-order importance for the rest of the world
- This paper builds on Brun et al. (2025) using the same model (CORTAX) with enhanced calibration and a welfare assessment

### This paper's contribution and approach

- Welfare in CORTAX: utility that the representative consumer in each country derives from material consumption and leisure time
- GMT can increase welfare by: increasing CIT revenues (which governments allocate to welfare-enhancing transfers) and reducing profit shifting (increases efficiency)
- GMT may also reduce welfare by: raising effective taxation and thus the cost of capital for firms, discouraging investment
- CORTAX allows quantitative evaluation of these trade-offs

Two fiscal closure scenarios:
- Transfer closure: increased CIT revenues redistributed as lump-sum transfers to households; mixed results; overall slightly positive
- CIT rate adjustment closure: iterate over domestic CIT rates to find new equilibrium leaving CIT revenues constant at initial absolute levels (offsetting additional revenues from reduced profit shifting by lowering CIT rates, provided they remain at or above GMT); welfare gains for nearly all countries and global welfare gain

Both scenarios satisfy Hicks-Kaldor test (potential Pareto improvement). The paper describes this as "the first comprehensive and quantitative welfare assessment of the GMT."

Three extensions relative to Brun et al. (2025):
1. Differentiate ETRs that apply to MNEs from CIT rates that apply to domestic firms; GMT shock affects only the former
2. Produce a welfare assessment (Brun et al. 2025 focus on tax revenue effects)
3. Loop over different GMT rates to identify the welfare-maximising level; additional revenue either transferred to households or used to reduce CIT rates such that national CIT revenues remain unchanged (provided all rates remain at or above GMT)

Optimal rate finding: 16% under CIT rate adjustment closure, 18% under transfer closure.


## Section 2: Profit shifting and related GMT literature (article pp. 4–7)

### Stated objectives of the GMT

- Two stated objectives behind introduction of 15% GMT:
  1. Mitigate harmful tax competition among jurisdictions, preventing race to the bottom in tax rates
  2. Curb wasteful profit shifting
- The 15% minimum rate establishes "a floor below which governments cannot lower their CIT rates" and constrains national governments in their use of tax instruments to compete for mobile international capital

### Profit shifting strategies — documented channels (Beer et al., 2020)

- Debt shifting: operations of an entity in a high-tax country are financed with loans from an entity of the same group in a low-tax country; interest payments reduce taxable profits in high-tax country, become taxable income in low-tax country
- Royalty payments: IP rights located in a zero-tax haven; company in high-tax country pays royalties (e.g., for trademarks); tax advantage can be substantial when IP is in zero-tax haven
- Transfer pricing: entity in high-tax jurisdiction uses inputs from a related party in a lower-tax country at an artificially high price

### Inefficiency of profit shifting

- Distorts the optimal allocation of resources
- Directly costly through resources required for shifting activities
- Resources required to set up an entity in a low-tax country may be a fixed cost, but risks associated with underreporting and concealment costs may increase with amount of shifted profits (Johannesen 2022 assumes the latter)

### Johannesen (2022)

- Model: MNEs optimise net worldwide profits given tax rates; heterogeneous countries (including tax havens) set tax rates to maximise national welfare
- Comparison of Nash equilibria before/after GMT: net welfare effect for non-haven countries is ambiguous
- GMT reduces profit shifting → increases tax revenues → can be spent on valued public good → welfare benefit
- GMT also lowers welfare of capital owners in non-haven countries because of increased tax payments
- Tax havens endogenously respond to GMT by setting rate to exactly GMT rate (footnote 5: setting lower rate is suboptimal because other jurisdictions would have taxing rights up to 15% through IIR or UTPR if the tax haven chose a lower rate)
- Net welfare effect depends on elasticity of profit shifting and weight of capital owners in governments' welfare function

### Hebous and Keen (2023)

- Model: profit shifting by MNEs and tax competition for "paper profits" between a low-tax and a high-tax country
- Starting observation: low-tax countries have opposed the reform, expecting to lose from GMT
- Two benchmarks:
  1. Pareto-efficient minimum rate: maximises welfare of the low-tax country
  2. Maximal Pareto dominant rate: highest minimum rate still yielding welfare gain for the low-tax country compared to the initial uncoordinated Nash equilibrium
- Parameterised estimates: dominant rate ranges from 17% to 20%; corresponding efficient minimum rates are 2 to 4 pp lower
- Note: while the range is similar to this paper's results, Hebous and Keen focus on welfare of the low-tax country (no country worse off), whereas this paper focuses on global welfare
- Key condition: high-tax country's optimal response to an increase in the low-tax country's rate is to raise its own rate ("strategic complements"); this result is also found in OECD (2020)
- Vrijburg and de Mooij (2016): countries may view each other's tax rates as "strategic substitutes"
- In this paper's second scenario, CIT rates are adjusted iteratively to maintain revenue — this is not an optimal response in the game-theoretic sense and should not be regarded as evidence for strategic complements or substitutes, but as "an exploration of the policy space that affords the increased tax efficiency from reduced profit shifting"

### Limitation of Johannesen (2022) and Hebous and Keen (2023)

- "True" profits of MNEs are taken to be fixed — no mobile international capital seeking highest after-tax return

### Janeba and Schjelderup (2023)

- Explicitly model tax competition for real capital
- Countries strategically set CIT rates to maximise tax revenue given firm behaviour; firms have real activity in two non-havens and shift profits to a tax haven
- GMT leads to reduced profit shifting → higher tax base and revenues for non-haven countries
- Secondary effect: reduced profit shifting increases value of attracting real capital → may intensify international tax rate competition; result depends on initial level of competition
- Also model competition with lump-sum subsidies: revenue gains from reduced profit shifting will be offset by higher subsidies
- Perry (2023): substance-based exclusions/carve-outs in Pillar Two rules leave door open for competition; while tax rate competition is limited by GMT, alternative instruments (subsidies, tax credits, other incentives) could allow race to the bottom by modifying definition of tax bases. This paper does not address these issues specifically.

### Hines (2024)

- Alternative approach: current tax rate taken to be revealed preference of each jurisdiction given (unknown) extent of international competition
- Under this framework, if one assumes sufficiently large impact from international competition, a 15% GMT could be welfare improving
- However, even in that case, the framework suggests this would be inferior to a much larger GMT rate of above 27%

### This paper's position in the literature

- Focus on welfare consequences of GMT in a model with capital mobility and endogenous profit shifting
- Does not directly incorporate endogenous tax rate competition between jurisdictions (footnote 6: in standard CORTAX, CIT rates are exogenous; in some simulations rates are adjusted iteratively)
- Models the long-run "end state" where GMT has been fully implemented (as in Johannesen, 2022 and Hebous and Keen, 2023) and capital allocation has fully adjusted
- Countries with CIT rate below 15% will raise it to this level — same strategy as Brun et al. (2025)


## Section 3: The CORTAX model (article pp. 7–12)

### Overview

- CORTAX = CORporate TAXation model; a computable general equilibrium (CGE) model with a particular focus on corporate taxation
- Covers: 27 EU MS, UK, USA, Japan, and a generic tax haven (footnote 7: single tax haven, so competition between havens cannot be modelled; end state assumed where all havens have implemented GMT, as in Nash equilibrium of Johannesen, 2022)
- Previously used for: CCCTB (Bettendorf et al., 2010; Álvarez-Martínez et al., 2016a; European Commission, 2011, 2016), debt bias (de Mooij and Devereux, 2011; European Commission, 2022a), digital economy taxation (European Commission, 2018), and size of BEPS (Álvarez-Martínez et al., 2021)
- Further information on current model calibration: Bratta et al. (2023)

### Firm types

Three types of firms in each jurisdiction:
- Domestic firms
- Multinational headquarters (owns a foreign subsidiary in each foreign country)
- Multinational subsidiaries (one owned by each foreign country's MNE)

### Key model features

- Captures international trade in intermediate goods and FDI by multinationals
- Multinational firms maximise their global equity value, including by engaging in international profit-shifting
- Profit-shifting: can occur between countries (modelled as transfer pricing) and into a tax haven; incurs an administrative cost modelled as a non-linear function increasing in the ratio of profit shifted over realised profits, and convex

### Government behaviour

- Governments do not play an active strategic role; no tax competition mechanism
- Tax instruments (e.g., CIT rates) are treated as given parameters, exogenous to the model (footnote 8: CIT rates are exogenous in standard CORTAX, with the exception when the tax rate is used to balance the government budget; in the second closure, CIT rates are changed endogenously to leave CIT revenues unaffected)
- Abstracts from strategic tax setting by governments

### Household sector

- Overlapping generations model: young households working, old households retired
- Households maximise inter-temporal utility (consumption + leisure) subject to inter-temporal budget constraint
- Net savings from young workers (wages + current transfers minus consumption) = negative value of net savings from old households (who do not work and only receive transfers)
- Domestic and foreign goods: perfect substitutes, so international trade can satisfy domestic demand; net exports endogenously determined
- Tax revenues: indirect taxes on consumption, direct taxes on corporate and labour income, dividends, capital gains, and interest

### Section 3.1: Profit Shifting in CORTAX (article pp. 8–9)

Transfer pricing (between non-tax-haven countries):
- Multinationals differ from domestic firms by using cross-border intermediate goods and services in production
- Parent headquarters charges a transfer price for intra-firm deliveries, artificially adjusted to shift profits to low-tax jurisdictions
- Organisational costs: convex cost function with an elasticity parameter determining the gradient
- If subsidiary's country has lower tax rate than HQ country: incentive to set higher price, shifting profits to subsidiary
- If subsidiary's country has higher tax rate than HQ country: price adjusted downward, shifting profits to HQ

Tax haven profit shifting:
- Firms can shift part of their tax base to a notional tax haven with a very low tax rate
- Driven by the differential between the firm's home country CIT rate and the tax haven rate
- Elasticity parameter calibrated to the consensus estimate in Heckemeyer and Overesch (2017) (footnote 11: robustness conducted in Section 6 using lower and upper bounds of 95% CI from Heckemeyer and Overesch, 2017)

Relative quantitative importance: tax haven channel dominates in magnitude over transfer pricing between non-haven countries. Example Germany: estimated net profit outflows from transfer pricing ≈ EUR 156 million; tax revenue losses from profit shifting to tax havens ≈ EUR 8.5 billion (Álvarez-Martínez et al., 2016a).

### Section 3.2: Welfare and Compensating Variation (article pp. 9–10)

- Households' utility: CES combination of consumption and leisure, maximised across lifetime (both young and old generations)
- Welfare comparison method: compensating variation (CV) — the amount of money that would be needed to compensate households for a change in their economic environment such that they would be indifferent between the original and new situations
  - Negative welfare change: reform reduced well-being; CV = amount needed to compensate
  - Positive welfare change: reform improved well-being; CV = amount that if subtracted from household income would exactly offset the welfare change
- Note (footnote 12): in the tables, the sign is changed so positive values = welfare gain
- Welfare change for the tax haven is measured as the change in its tax revenue (a money measure, comparable to household CVs, and thus can be aggregated)

### Section 3.3: Parametrisation and FDI Stock Data (article pp. 10–11)

Data sources: Eurostat, OECD, UN, ZEW-Mannheim, Bureau van Dijk Orbis database
- Labour supply: AMECO employment data and UN population statistics
- Tax rates on labour and consumption: Eurostat (EU), OECD (non-EU)
- Tax compliance costs: European Commission (2022b)
- Government collection costs: OECD data
- CIT details (statutory rates, allowances): Spengel et al. (2020)
- Capital shares: Eurostat and Orbis
- ETRs on MNE HQ and subsidiaries: Garcia-Bernardo et al. (2023), using country-specific mean of ETR2 (paid CIT / sum of operating profits, excluding financial revenue and expenses, in line with the corporate tax base definition in CORTAX) (footnote 14)
  - If ETR from Garcia-Bernardo et al. exceeds the statutory rate: statutory rate is applied instead
  - Exception United States: ETR based on a single observation in Garcia-Bernardo et al. (2023), so Clausing, Saez, and Zucman (2021) is used instead, who report an average ETR of 17% in 2018
- Domestic firms: statutory tax rates applied

FDI data — two reasons for special attention:
1. FDI is commonly reported on an immediate origin/destination basis; conduit country routing leads to double counting of FDI (Van 't Riet and Lejour, 2018); some countries may have unrealistically high incoming and outgoing FDI relative to the size of their economies, which would distort calibration of the return to investment
2. Real bilateral FDI investment is needed to calibrate the size of foreign subsidiaries of MNEs (relevant for the UTPR, which determines each jurisdiction's share of undertaxed profits based on participation in MNE profit shifting, footnote 15)

FDI data: IMF Coordinated Direct Investment Survey (CDIS) 2019; missing values complemented manually; values normalised to average USD-EUR exchange rate for 2019.

Refinement: approach of Damgaard et al. (2019) adopted to separate genuine FDI from "conduit" FDI (routed through particular countries for tax or regulatory benefits rather than substantive business operations). Uses FDI-to-GDP ratios and a regression-based predictive model. Most countries show real FDI proportions near 100%, but Luxembourg, Malta, Cyprus, and Ireland show notably lower real FDI shares per the authors' estimations.

### Section 3.4: Implementation of Pillar Two in CORTAX (article pp. 10–12)

- GMT modelled as: all countries with current MNE ETRs below 15% (including the tax haven) adjust CIT rates (applicable to MNEs) to the 15% minimum standard
- In practice, 15% ETR attained by combination of top-up taxes (with revenue accruing to different jurisdictions); this paper assumes all achieved through QDMTTs, so all extra revenue accrues to the country where profits are made
- Consequence: IIR and UTPR are rendered unnecessary (all corporate profits already taxed at ≥ 15%)
- This modelling approach focuses on changes in national CIT tax bases, including from reduced profit shifting
- Equivalent to modelling an "end state" or long-run equilibrium; rationale: if a country does not collect the tax for itself, MNEs will still pay up to the minimum tax to other jurisdictions through IIR or UTPR; hence there is no benefit to the country in choosing not to collect the tax for itself

EU MS classification:
- Low-tax countries (average MNE ETR below 15% before GMT): Croatia, Ireland, Luxembourg, Cyprus, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Slovenia, and Bulgaria
- High-tax countries (MNE ETRs above 15% before GMT): remaining 17 EU MS; non-EU countries (US, UK, Japan) also classified as high-tax

Effects of GMT in the model:
- Low-tax EU countries: (1) GMT constrains MNE ETR to exactly 15%; (2) tax haven rate also increases to 15%, so profit shifting out of EU low-tax countries stops entirely with introduction of GMT
- High-tax EU + non-EU countries: (1) changed transfer pricing incentives with low-tax trading partners; (2) changed incentives to shift tax base to tax haven; both profit shifting incentives reduced, increasing effective taxation of MNEs in high-tax countries; US has ETR above 15% in calibration, so US participation in Pillar Two not critical (modelling through QDMTT approach)

Modelling caveats and approximations:
- MNE ETRs are calibrated averages; in reality, even if average ETR is above 15% in a jurisdiction, there may still be some undertaxed profits (OECD, 2024); these undertaxed MNE entities not captured → would increase quantitative impact if included
- Not all MNEs are in scope (only those with global revenues > EUR 750m); paper assumes representative MNE fully in-scope → would decrease quantitative impact of the GMT shock if accounted for
- GMT contemplates substance-based carve-outs (based on employees and tangible assets) → not modelled; would also decrease the GMT shock
- Since elements that both amplify and cushion the shock are excluded, the paper considers the exercise "overall a good approximation"


## Section 4: Main Results and Discussion of Mechanisms (article pp. 11–17)

### Two competing channels on welfare

1. Through reduced tax differentials: higher GMT → reduces extent of profit shifting → reduces associated costs → higher corporate tax bases and revenues → revenues can be used for government transfers → stimulates consumption → enhances welfare
2. Through higher tax burden: higher GMT rate → increases tax bill for firms → lower after-tax income for capital owners → decreases welfare; also raises cost of capital → diminishes investment → lower capital stocks, labour demand, and equilibrium wages → negatively impacts household welfare

### Results under transfer closure (Table 1, article p. 12)

Group-level results, % change and EUR for CV:

EU low-tax countries:
- Cost of capital: +0.097 pp (most pronounced; "more than twofold increase" in cost of capital for MNEs in some of these low-tax jurisdictions, including Ireland, Cyprus, Hungary, Latvia, Bulgaria)
- CIT revenues: +20.58% (as domestic statutory tax rates rise to meet GMT)
- GDP: -1.13%
- CV: +€2.96bn (+0.30% of GDP) — positive overall

EU high-tax countries:
- Cost of capital: +0.052 pp
- CIT revenues: +5.64% (exclusively driven by reduced profit outflows to tax havens)
- GDP: -0.22%
- CV: -€4.80bn (-0.04% of GDP) — negative overall

Non-EU countries (US, UK, Japan):
- Cost of capital: +0.041 pp
- CIT revenues: +5.76%
- GDP: -0.15%
- CV: -€1.69bn (-0.01% of GDP)

Tax haven: CV = +€7.21bn

Total:
- Cost of capital: +0.046 pp
- CIT revenues: +6.12%
- GDP: -0.20%
- CV: +€3.68bn (+0.01% of GDP)

Important qualification: "the simulation results indicate a small, positive change in global welfare...however, this overall gain arises only when the welfare effect in the tax haven is included."

Footnote 16: Luxembourg excluded from Figure 1 for readability. GMT increases the ETR for MNEs headquartered in Luxembourg by approximately 7.7 pp and would lead to a welfare gain of about 4.5% of GDP with the transfer adjustment closure.

Footnote 17: additional revenues allocated exclusively to transfer payments for the older generation. The younger generation makes some adjustment to their labour supply and savings decisions, knowing that the increased transfer will be available when they are old. This closure results in a "more muted behavioural response by the young generation than other closures."

Footnote 18: changes in GDP and welfare do not necessarily move in the same direction. For instance, a decline in labour supply may lead to lower production and thus lower GDP, but can coincide with an increase in welfare as households benefit from more leisure time.

### Country-level heterogeneity under transfer closure (Figure 1, article p. 13)

- France, Germany, and Spain experience relatively larger increases in the cost of capital (under pre-GMT regime, these countries saw considerable profit shifting to tax havens due to large tax differentials; GMT narrows differentials, profit shifting curtailed, tax burden on MNEs increases, cost of capital rises; contractionary effects of reduced investment, lower productivity, and declining wages dominate; welfare losses only partially mitigated by increased transfers)
- GDP-weighted average for EU high-tax group turns out negative because France, Germany, and Spain carry substantial weight, "thereby overshadowing the positive welfare effects observed in many smaller high-tax EU countries"
- Most other EU high-tax countries (ETR above 15% but not as high as France/Germany): welfare gains; increase in cost of capital is modest; rise in CIT revenues sufficient to fund transfers that more than offset negative macroeconomic effects
- These outcomes "align with the theoretical predictions outlined in the previous section"

Group-level results, % change and EUR for CV:

EU high-tax countries:
- CIT rate lowered by about 1 pp on average → cost of capital decreases by 0.03 pp → stimulates investment
- GDP: +0.05%
- CV: +€14.85bn (+0.11% of GDP) — all positive

EU low-tax countries (CIT rate reduction not possible as still constrained by GMT):
- Cost of capital: +0.10 pp
- CIT revenues: +19.67%
- GDP: -1.40%
- CV: +€3.04bn (+0.31% of GDP) — "in line with the findings in Hebous and Keen (2023), who suggest that low tax countries could also gain"

EU total:

- CIT revenues: +1.37%
- GDP: -0.14%
- CV: +€17.89bn (+0.13% of GDP)

Non-EU (US, UK, Japan):
- Cost of capital: -0.03 pp
- CIT revenues: approximately 0%
- GDP: -0.03%
- CV: +€19.22bn (+0.09% of GDP) — increased wages, small reduction in employment, small net increases in both consumption and leisure

Tax haven: +€1.94bn

Total:
- Cost of capital: -0.02 pp
- CIT revenues: +0.55%

Footnote 20: Luxembourg again excluded from Figure 2. Same footnote 16 result applies.

Key policy interpretation: "This result suggests that if countries were welfare maximizing in our model, their likely response to the GMT would be to lower CIT rates. This is a departure from some of the literature, suggesting that CIT rates may be strategic substitutes, rather than strategic complements."

### Country-level heterogeneity under CIT rate adjustment closure (Figure 2, article p. 15)

- Most countries experience modest welfare gains relative to GDP
- Countries with initially low effective CIT rates see particularly large improvements (driven by substantial CIT revenue increases enabling higher direct transfers)
- Bulgaria: welfare gain of ≈ +0.5% of GDP
- Cyprus: welfare gain of ≈ +1.4% of GDP
- Hungary (notable exception): increase in CIT revenues and associated transfers insufficient to offset contractionary effects of higher cost of capital → small welfare loss of approximately -0.1% of GDP
- Countries with baseline ETRs above 15%: small positive welfare effects from repatriating some of the tax base without facing upward tax pressure

### Welfare-optimal GMT rate (Figure 3, article pp. 16–17)

Range of GMT rates simulated: 10% to 25%.

Two key findings:
1. Both fiscal closure rules lead to overall welfare gains, but magnitude differs substantially. When CIT revenues are recycled through supply-side measures such as CIT rate reductions, the resulting welfare improvements are "significantly larger" than when revenues are allocated to demand-side transfers.
2. Welfare-optimal GMT rate:
   - CIT rate adjustment closure: welfare peaks at 16%; beyond 16%, welfare gains decline as contractionary economic effects become more pronounced
   - Transfer closure: welfare peaks at 18%

Additional note on rate sensitivity: as GMT rises from 17% to 18%, there is a significant jump in the number of constrained countries. A GMT of 15% under the transfer closure would replace the current ETR in five major countries in the sample of 30, namely the United States, the United Kingdom, Poland, Portugal, and Austria, "driving the global welfare results into negative territory" (under transfer closure).

Number of constrained countries (Figure 4, article p. 17): 10 out of 30 countries have ETR below 15% in the calibration; this number increases as the hypothetical GMT rate rises, with a significant jump between 17% and 18%.


## Section 5: Robustness test (article pp. 18–19)

- Profit-shifting semi-elasticity parameter (γ) calibrated to Heckemeyer and Overesch (2017) meta-regression study
- Two sensitivity checks using lower bound (γ = 0.54) and upper bound (γ = 1.03) of 95% CI from Heckemeyer and Overesch (2017), compared to baseline (γ = 0.78)

Results (Figure 5, article p. 18):
- As the semi-elasticity increases, the total welfare gains curve becomes flatter
- Mechanism: in the low-elasticity scenario, higher revenues are collected in high-tax countries on introduction of GMT; in the CIT rate adjustment closure, extra revenues returned to firms as lower tax rate → domestic CIT rates reduced more → lower cost of capital → greater increase in welfare; hence achieving a specific welfare gain requires a higher GMT rate when semi-elasticity is larger
- Key finding: despite flattening of curves at higher semi-elasticity values, peak remains at 16% across all three parameter values — "underscores the robustness of the optimal tax rate, even under varying profit-shifting sensitivities"

Footnote 21: under the transfer adjustment method, the welfare-maximising rate shifts slightly downward as profit-shifting semi-elasticity increases: 18% under baseline (γ = 0.78); 17% under lower bound (γ = 0.54). "These results remain within a narrow range around our baseline estimate, confirming the robustness of the welfare-maximising GMT rate to plausible variations in the responsiveness of profit shifting."


## Section 6: Discussion and Conclusion (article pp. 19–20)

### Summary of findings

- GMT of 15% raises CIT revenue for all countries
- Two strategies for balancing government budgets: direct consumer transfers vs. adjustments to domestic CIT rates; results differ substantially

Transfer closure:
- Mixed welfare outcomes, "aligns with the theoretical predictions of Johannesen (2022)"
- Negative results arise when welfare benefits from increased revenue do not outweigh the economic contraction from higher corporate tax burdens

CIT rate adjustment closure:
- Beneficial to all "high-tax" countries (countries with rate above GMT rate, footnote 22: this applies to both EU high-tax countries and non-EU countries)
- Rise in cost of capital for firms reducing profit shifting mitigated by reducing statutory CIT rate → enhances investment incentives, boosts GDP and welfare
- "The CORTAX model's results suggest that the same level of CIT revenues worldwide can be obtained in a welfare-increasing way if countries cooperate and profit shifting is reduced"

Optimal rate:
- Transfer closure: increasing modest total gains peaking at 18%
- CIT rate adjustment: larger gains peaking at 16%
- Robustness analysis confirms welfare-maximising GMT rate "remains stable across a range of parameter values, supporting the baseline findings and aligning closely with the Pillar Two rate"

Low-tax EU countries: "display robust welfare gains" in both scenarios. Overall welfare gains in both scenarios "would be sufficient to compensate the countries with negative welfare outcomes" (Hicks-Kaldor criterion met). Tax haven benefits from increased government revenues in both scenarios, "aligning with the theoretical results of Johannesen (2022)."

### Limitations

1. CORTAX cannot capture dynamic aspects of international tax competition; provides long-run equilibrium outcomes rather than a transition path
2. Does not examine the political dynamics that may influence GMT implementation, such as tax competition among tax havens
3. US participation in Pillar Two: "may influence the political dynamics"; however, in this model's implementation (all low-tax countries including tax haven set rates at 15%, US rate for MNEs already above 15%), US participation is not crucial for the results
4. Model concentrates on EU countries and primary trading partners; excludes China and other developing countries; GMT effects likely to vary across countries depending on exposure to mobile capital, relative significance of CIT revenues within their economies, and degree of tax competition faced (issues explored by Haufler et al., 2025)
5. Abstracts from distributional effects of GMT, including potentially uneven impact on MNE shareholders compared to transfer recipients (referenced as Johanessen (2002) in text, likely intended as Johannesen, 2022)


## Appendix A: Simultaneous capital allocation and profit shifting of a single MNE (article pp. 22–26)

### Purpose

- Bridges the literature (models with fixed real profits) and CORTAX (calibrated general equilibrium model)
- Illustrates impact of variable profits using a small theoretical optimisation model for a single MNE
- Seeks to build intuition for the empirical results in Section 4

### Basic capital allocation problem (Equations 1–3)

- MNE maximises sum of (1 - t_j) * π̃_j over all jurisdictions j, where π̃_j = F_j(k_j), subject to total capital constraint (sum of k_j ≤ K̄)
- Kuhn-Tucker conditions: optimal capital allocation requires that the product of the net return of profit (1 - t_j) and the marginal profit of capital F'_j(k_j) must be equal for all jurisdictions invested in
- Optimal condition (Eq. 3): (1 - t_j) * F'_j(.) = μ for all j
- Thought experiment: if all jurisdictions raise tax rates such that net after-tax returns reduced in same proportion → objective function scaled by same factor, allocation unchanged
- BUT: if all jurisdictions simultaneously join GMT → ETRs raised differently because initial tax rates differ → ETRs affected differently → optimal allocation of capital changes; "This is an important difference with the models with fixed profits"

### Adding profit shifting (Equations 4–9, following Johannesen, 2022)

- Distinguish real profits π̃_j from reported profits π_j
- Profit shifting is to a tax haven with a very low rate t^L (raised when GMT implemented)
- Convex cost function C depending on size of underreporting C(π̃_j - π_j)
- MNE maximises total net after-tax profits minus cost of profit shifting (Eq. 4)
- Kuhn-Tucker conditions (Eq. 5a-c): for interior solution with positive reported profits, the tax haven rate plus the marginal cost of shifting equals the jurisdictional tax rate: t^L + C'(.) = t_j
- If combined costs would be higher than t_j: shifting incurs a loss; if lower: more could be shifted
- Implication: marginal effective tax rate equals the initial jurisdictional tax rate
- "Somewhat unexpected result": if interior solution holds for all jurisdictions, all ETRs (including marginal shifting costs) remain unchanged and investment allocation unchanged and independent of profit shifting
- "This is a consequence of the cost function used above, which has the absolute value of shifting as argument"
- CORTAX however features a cost function based on the share of shifting, "which always impacts the investment allocation"

### Share-based cost function (Equations 6–9)

- θ_j = (π̃_j - π_j) / π̃_j = share of profit shifting from jurisdiction j
- Cost function: C(θ) = A^(-1/γ) * θ^(1+1/γ) / (1+1/γ) * π̃ (Eq. 6)
- Optimal share of shifting (Eq. 9): θ*_j = A(t_j - t^L)^γ — the larger the tax rate differential, the larger the share of profit shifting from jurisdiction j

### Implications for GMT implementation

- GMT means t^L = t^M (minimum rate)
- For low-tax jurisdictions (t^L < t_j < t^M): must raise statutory CIT rates to t^M; raises ETR → will attract less capital; no profit shifting from these jurisdictions (θ*_j = 0 by Eq. 9)
- GMT reduces the net return to capital; increase of tax costs can only "partially be recovered by the reduced profit shifting by the MNE"
- In an open economy: reduction in net return to capital → less investment and lower equilibrium capital stock
- Limitation of this small model: fixed supply of capital; CORTAX (Section 3) allows estimation of the extent to which capital stock might be affected
