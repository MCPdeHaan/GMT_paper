# General notes


## p. 1499 Introduction and Overview

The paper addresses one of the central blind spots in the international corporate taxation literature: the absence of comprehensive, bilateral estimates of profit shifting by multinational firms. Despite decades of academic interest and policy debate, no prior study had produced a global, bilateral account of how much profit is shifted, from where, to which havens, and by multinationals from which parent countries.

The authors draw on a relatively new category of macroeconomic data **foreign affiliates statistics (FATS)** published since the early 2010s by most developed countries, including the main tax havens. These statistics record the wages and profits of foreign-controlled firms (defined as firms more than 50% owned by foreign shareholders) in each host country. The central empirical observation motivating the entire paper is that in tax havens, foreign firms are dramatically more profitable than local firms, whereas in high-tax countries the opposite holds. The authors exploit this differential profitability as the primary signal of profit shifting.

**Core findings, stated at the outset:**

In their preferred estimate for 2015, **36% of multinational profits** defined as profits earned by multinationals outside the country of their parent were shifted to tax havens globally. US multinationals shift comparatively more: over half of their multinational profits end up in havens, versus roughly a quarter for other multinationals. EU high-tax countries are the primary losers, with domestic profits understated by approximately 20%, compared to 10% in the US and 5% in developing countries. Tax haven profits, by contrast, would fall by 55% absent profit shifting.

The paper also provides a corrected international database of GDP, trade balances, and factor shares. The authors argue that the corporate capital share has in fact risen substantially in high-tax EU countries a finding obscured in official statistics by the tax avoidance strategies of multinationals, which cause profits to be recorded in tax havens rather than in the countries where production genuinely occurs.



## p. 1499–1500 Introduction: Mechanism and Global Perspective

The decline in the global average statutory corporate tax rate from 49% in 1985 to 24% in 2018 is attributed in part to international tax competition, as countries cut rates to attract profits and capital. The paper fills the empirical gap by estimating the global extent of profit shifting and its bilateral distribution.

The methodology proceeds in three steps. First, FATS are combined with national accounts data to estimate the profitability of foreign versus local firms within each tax haven. Second, this differential profitability is used to construct estimates (and bounds) for profits shifted into each haven. Third, bilateral balance of payments data are used to reallocate those profits to source countries (where the profits were generated) and to parent countries (where the ultimate parent corporations are headquartered).

The global perspective is specifically highlighted as an advantage: it captures profit shifting by *all* multinationals operating in a given country, not just domestic ones. For instance, estimating the profit shifted out of Germany requires tracking German subsidiaries of US, Dutch, Irish, and other multinationals not only German firms. Similarly, bilateral estimates enable questions such as: do profits shifted out of France first flow to Luxembourg before reaching Bermuda? And does the EU's freedom of capital movement facilitate conduit shifting that eventually leaves the EU entirely?



## p. 1500 Key Quantitative Results

Summarising the main findings:

- **$616 billion** in multinational profits were shifted to tax havens globally in 2015 (preferred estimate), representing 36% of all multinational profits.
- US multinationals shifted **more than half** of their foreign profits to havens; other multinationals shifted about a quarter.
- The EU high-tax countries lost the most: profit losses equivalent to **1.5% of GDP**, versus 0.8% in the US.
- On a bilateral basis, **77% of profits shifted out of EU high-tax countries** initially flow to EU havens (Ireland, Luxembourg, Netherlands), but **46% ultimately end up in non-EU havens** on an ultimate-destination basis important given that EU law prohibits member states from taxing intra-EU payments.
- **About half of globally shifted profits accrue to US parents**; slightly over 25% to EU parents.
- After correcting for profit shifting, the corporate capital share in high-tax EU countries rises by approximately **4 percentage points** over 1985–2015 contrary to official statistics that suggest little change. This challenges the view that the rise of capital's share is a uniquely North American phenomenon.



## p. 1500–1501 Implications for Macroeconomic Measurement

Profit shifting systematically distorts headline economic indicators. GDP, net exports, operating surplus, and trade balances are overstated in tax havens and understated in non-haven countries. When shifted profits are added back to source countries, the corporate capital share rises materially by about 2–2.5 percentage points in the main EU countries and 1.1 points in the US.

The finding that the capital share has risen in Europe (after correction) lends empirical support to theories of global factor share dynamics based on declining relative capital prices (Karabarbounis & Neiman, 2014), capital-biased technological change and automation (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2018), the rise of superstar firms (Autor et al., 2020), and capital accumulation (Piketty & Zucman, 2014). Country-specific shocks alone cannot explain a phenomenon that is global in character but rendered invisible by accounting distortions.



## p. 1502 Related Literature: Microeconometric Estimates

The standard microeconometric approach to estimating profit shifting uses the Orbis database (Bureau van Dijk) and regresses firm-level pre-tax profits on the difference between the home and host country statutory tax rate, controlling for firm and country characteristics. A positive coefficient is interpreted as evidence of profit shifting. The OECD (2015) used this approach for its official BEPS (Base Erosion and Profit Shifting) estimates.

The authors identify a critical limitation: **Orbis is severely incomplete**. In 2012, only 17% of global multinational profits could be traced at the subsidiary level in Orbis (weighted by profit); 83% were booked in subsidiaries either not in Orbis or for which no profit data existed. For Apple in 2016, Orbis correctly reported consolidated worldwide profits of €55.3 billion but could identify only €2.0 billion at the subsidiary level missing entirely the profits booked in the US and in tax havens. Bermuda, the US, and Switzerland, among others, lack public registries with income information. Statistical authorities, by contrast, have access to private income statements and balance sheets.

Bilicka (2019) comparing UK tax returns of foreign multinational affiliates to matched domestic firms finds that foreign multinationals shifted roughly 50% of their profits out of the UK between 2000 and 2014 using comprehensive tax data, consistent with the authors' global figures.

## p. 1503 Related Literature: Macroeconomic Estimates

The macroeconomic literature on profit shifting has largely focused on US multinationals. Clausing (2009, 2016) and Wright & Zucman (2018) estimate shifting by US firms; Guvenen et al. (2021) estimate that $158.3 billion was shifted out of the US by US multinationals in 2015. This is close to the authors' estimate of $143 billion shifted out of the US by US and non-US multinationals combined.

Crivelli, de Mooij & Keen (2015) use a panel model of corporate tax revenue and statutory rates to infer avoidance. Bolwijn, Casella & Rigo (2018) rely on outward FDI statistics. Neither uses FATS or the differential profitability approach. The global estimate of Bolwijn et al. ($700 billion in 2012) is comparable in magnitude to this paper's $616 billion in 2015.

## p. 1503–1506 Conceptual Framework and Methodology

### Profitability Measure

The key variable throughout the paper is the **profits-to-wage ratio** (π), defined as pre-tax corporate profits divided by total employee compensation (including non-wage components such as pension contributions and payroll taxes, in line with national accounts SNA code D.1). This is equivalent to α/(1−α) where α is the net-of-depreciation capital share of corporate value added, adjusted for net interest payments (which are deductible from the corporate tax base). All magnitudes are net of capital depreciation.

The ratio is computed separately for foreign firms (πf) and local firms (πl) within each country. Local firms include both purely domestic firms and domestic multinationals; foreign firms are all corporations more than 50% foreign-owned. By construction, the aggregate π equals a weighted average of πf and πl, where the weight s is the share of wages paid by foreign firms.

The three channels through which profit shifting inflates πf in tax havens are: (i) **transfer price manipulation** (intra-group exports and imports priced to shift profits); (ii) **intra-group debt shifting** (high-tax affiliates borrow from low-tax affiliates, transferring interest deductions); and (iii) **intangible relocation** (moving patents, trademarks, algorithms, and financial portfolios to havens that charge royalties back to high-tax affiliates).

### Tax Haven Classification

The paper includes **41 countries and territories** as tax havens: five OECD members (Belgium, Ireland, Luxembourg, Netherlands, Switzerland) and 36 non-OECD jurisdictions. The list is based on Hines & Rice (1994), augmented by the Netherlands, Belgium, and Puerto Rico. The Netherlands was excluded from the original Hines & Rice list because US multinationals reported high effective tax rates there in 1982, but by 2015 this had fallen to 12%. Belgium's effective rate was 19% (the world average), making it a borderline case; it accounts for only 2% of the baseline estimate and its exclusion would not materially alter results. Puerto Rico is treated as a separate jurisdiction (it is not subject to the US federal corporate income tax and is excluded from US GDP statistics).

Figure 2 in the paper illustrates the classification: tax havens uniformly exhibit both low effective corporate tax rates (below 15% in almost all cases) and πf > πl, often dramatically so. Non-haven countries generally show πf ≤ πl.

### Baseline Assumptions

The baseline estimate of profit shifting is obtained by setting **πf equal to the observed πl** within each tax haven that is, computing how much profit foreign firms would earn if they were as profitable as local firms, and treating the remainder as shifted. Three assumptions underpin this procedure:

1. **The elasticity of substitution between capital and labour (σ) equals 1** (Cobb-Douglas). Under σ = 1, differences in capital intensity between foreign and local firms do not create any gap in profit-to-wage ratios, because higher capital stock is perfectly offset by a lower marginal product. Any observed excess profitability must therefore reflect shifting rather than productive capital.

2. **The degree of competition is the same for foreign and local firms within tax havens.** The authors allow for haven-wide rents (from financial secrecy or lax regulation) but assume these accrue equally to both types of firms. Competition assumptions may vary across havens but must be uniform within them.

3. **Inward profit shifting does not distort the reported πl of local haven firms.** This is supported empirically: local haven firms are generally no more profitable than local firms in non-haven countries they do not appear to be abnormally profitable, which is what one would expect if πl were inflated by inward shifting.

### Validation Using US BEA Data

The baseline methodology is validated using BEA outward FATS (the "Activities of US Multinational Enterprises"), available annually since 1982. A regression of the form in equation (4) of the paper relates the profits-to-wage ratio of US affiliates to capital stocks, R&D expenditures, host-country GDP, population, and a haven dummy, with year fixed effects and interactions. The results show:

- In 2015, the raw profits-to-wage ratio of haven affiliates was **346%**, versus 46% for non-haven affiliates a difference by a factor of seven and a half.
- In the 1960s and 1970s, haven and non-haven affiliates had similar profitability; the divergence emerged from the mid-1980s onward.
- The excess profitability of haven affiliates persists fully after controlling for capital intensity, R&D, industry composition, and host-country characteristics.
- Only **8% of R&D workers** of US multinationals were in tax havens in 2014; 92% were in high-tax countries. The high profitability of haven affiliates cannot be explained by genuine local R&D activity.
- Profit shifting is an **across-the-board phenomenon**, not confined to technology or ICT sectors.



## p. 1511–1513 Allocation of Shifted Profits

### To Source Countries

Shifted profits are allocated to source countries by tracking **excess cross-border transactions** conducive of profit shifting recorded in the bilateral balance of payments of tax havens. These are defined as excess exports of: intellectual property rights (patents, trademarks), headquarter services (administration, management, advertising), ICT services, and financial and insurance services (following Hebous & Johannesen, 2021). Goods exports are excluded as less important channels. Excess intra-group interest received by havens is also included.

"Excess" is defined relative to a predicted value benchmarked to the gross national income of the haven. For each haven *i* and source country *j*, the total excess cross-border transaction t_ij is computed as the sum of excess service exports x̄_ij and excess intra-group interest ḡ_ij. Profits shifted to haven *i* are then allocated to source countries proportionally to t_ij.

Crucially, this "excess transactions" methodology is **entirely independent** of the "excess profitability" baseline. Both nonetheless yield consistent results globally and at the level of individual havens providing a strong cross-validation of the estimates.

### To Parent Countries

A second allocation tracks profits to the countries where ultimate parent corporations are incorporated, using bilateral direct investment statistics compiled by Damgaard & Elkjaer (2017) on an ultimate ownership basis. This measures which parents receive the dividends or retained earnings from haven subsidiaries. About **half of globally shifted profits accrue to US parents**; slightly over 25% to EU parents. This allocation is directly relevant for assessing the revenue potential of a global minimum tax applied by parent countries to their multinationals' haven profits the approach agreed by more than 130 countries in June 2021 under the OECD Pillar Two framework (OECD, 2021).



## p. 1513–1516 Data Sources

### National Accounts

The profits-to-wage ratio π for all tax havens, OECD countries, and the main developing economies is computed from the OECD's detailed national accounts by sector (Table 14A), following the 2008 System of National Accounts (United Nations, 2009). Non-OECD havens (Singapore, Hong Kong, Puerto Rico) are added by manually collecting official national accounts from statistical institutes and central banks. Where gross-of-depreciation operating surplus is the only available series, depreciation is imputed based on average rates across comparable countries.

### Foreign Affiliates Statistics

The profitability of foreign firms πf is computed from **inward FATS** disseminated by Eurostat, the OECD, and national statistical agencies. These are census-type surveys of multinational enterprises, from which value added, operating surplus, and employee compensation of foreign-controlled corporations are extracted. Where inward FATS are unavailable (principally smaller non-OECD havens such as Bermuda and the Cayman Islands), the authors impute foreign profits from inward direct investment income reported in balance of payments statistics and impute foreign wages using the wage-to-profit ratios in the outward FATS of the US (which provides country-level data for small havens).

An important clarification on **double-counting**: The authors explicitly do not use the BEA "net income" tables (D1–D13), which double-count profits flowing through chains of holding companies. Instead, they use the BEA Value Added tables (F1–F9), where "profit-type return" excludes equity income received and does not involve double-counting. This is consistent with international FATS guidelines.

**Bilateral discrepancies** exist: the inward FATS of Ireland, Netherlands, and Luxembourg report less US affiliate profit than the US outward FATS. The authors' investigation suggests US data are more reliable (outward US investment in non-havens matches partner countries' inward data). In the central scenario, the authors **upgrade inward haven data to match US outward data**. Taking haven data at face value would reduce the global estimate by 18%.

### Balance of Payments

Bilateral balance of payments data are from the IMF and Eurostat. EU haven data are particularly comprehensive because of Eurostat harmonisation requirements. Where both haven-reported and counterpart-reported versions of the same flow exist, the haven data are preferred as more complete (the six EU tax havens record service exports to the 22 non-haven EU countries that exceed recorded imports by more than 30%, likely because digital services sold directly by haven subsidiaries to final customers are better captured in the exporter's statistics).

### Summary Statistics (Table 1)

In 2015: global GDP was $75 trillion; net corporate output was $34 trillion; net corporate profits were $11.5 trillion. Foreign-controlled corporations earned $1.7 trillion (15% of total). Of this, **$616 billion (36%) is estimated to have been shifted to tax havens**.



## p. 1517–1522 Estimates of Profit Shifted to Tax Havens

### Baseline Results (Section 5.1)

Figure 4 and Table 2 present the core results. The profits-to-wage ratio of foreign firms in havens is "truly exceptional": πf reaches 800% in Ireland and 1,625% in Puerto Rico. Foreign firms in havens are on average eight times more profitable than local firms in havens. In high-tax countries, the pattern is reversed: in the UK, πf = 26% versus πl = 48%. Local firms in tax havens are generally as profitable as local firms in non-haven countries consistent with Assumption 3 that πl in havens is not distorted by inward shifting.

**Ireland** is the single largest destination, with approximately **$100 billion** in shifted profits in 2015. Singapore, Netherlands, Caribbean havens, and Switzerland follow. The "excess transactions" methodology yields $646 billion globally slightly above the $616 billion baseline, plausibly because it also captures shifting by multinationals headquartered in havens (which the baseline misses).

Interest income accounts for only **15%** of the excess profitability of foreign firms in havens (the main exception being Luxembourg, where intra-group interest receipts are the primary driver). This is consistent with the view in the literature that debt shifting is secondary to transfer pricing and intangible relocation. In haven affiliates of US multinationals, only 1.1 times more interest is received relative to operating surplus compared to non-haven affiliates.

Absent profit shifting, profits booked in tax havens would **fall by 55% globally**, and by as much as 90% in Malta and Bermuda.

### Bounding the Estimates (Section 5.2, Table 2)

The paper systematically explores four sources of uncertainty:

**True profitability differences between foreign and local haven firms.** Drawing on Paul & Isaka (2019), who estimate labour share determinants at the firm level globally, foreign ownership is associated with a 2-percentage-point increase in the capital share; moving from the smallest to largest size category adds another 9 percentage points; a 50% increase in TFP adds 7 points. Taken together (additively), this implies a potential benchmark profitability for foreign haven firms roughly 45 percentage points above πl. Applied in Column 3 of Table 2, this is equivalent to roughly doubling πl. Even so, the adjustment has negligible impact in havens like Puerto Rico where πf is truly enormous, and only a moderate effect in Belgium and the Netherlands where the gap is small to begin with.

**Elasticity of substitution σ.** With σ = 0.7 (from Oberfield & Raval, 2021, for US manufacturing), the global estimate rises by 7%; with σ = 1.3 (from Karabarbounis & Neiman, 2014), it falls by 5%. The differences are small because, as shown in Figure 5, the capital intensity differences between foreign and local firms in havens are modest relative to the enormous profitability gap. In Ireland, even if local firms were as capital-intensive as foreign firms, with σ = 1.3 their πl would rise to 100% still nearly an order of magnitude below the observed πf of 800%.

**Inward shifting in local sectors.** Using Compustat Global, the authors identify listed multinationals headquartered in tax havens. If these firms shifted 36% of their global profits inward, this would raise the global estimate by $76 billion (12%). Significant only for Hong Kong, Singapore, and the Netherlands.

**Bilateral discrepancies.** Taking haven inward data at face value (rather than correcting to match US outward data) reduces the estimate by 18%.

**Lower and upper bounds:** Applying all conservative adjustments simultaneously yields a lower bound of $369 billion (22% of multinational profits); applying all generous adjustments gives an upper bound of $740 billion (43%). The preferred estimate of **36% lies comfortably within this range**.



## p. 1522–1527 International Redistribution of Corporate Profits

### Profit Losses in Source Countries (Table 3)

Of the $616 billion shifted globally: **$143 billion (23%) from the US**, **$216 billion (36%) from the EU**, $76 billion from other OECD countries, and 29% from non-OECD countries. More than 70% of losses originate in high-income countries.

Expressed as a share of GDP, the EU loses most (1.5% of GDP), followed by the US (0.8%), other high-income countries (0.7%), and developing countries (0.7%). Individual country figures from Table 3 include: Germany −1.6% of GDP, France −1.3%, UK −2.1%, US −0.8%, Japan −0.2%.

Expressed as a share of reported domestic profits (Column 6 of Table 3), absent profit shifting, profits would be 17% higher in France, 14% higher in the UK, 8% higher in the US, and approximately 1% higher in Japan. The authors note that for Germany and Italy, official corporate profits overstate true corporate sector profits because they include income from self-employed and non-corporate businesses (as documented by Pionnier & Guidetti, 2015), making the ratio of shifted profits to reported profits in Column 6 a **downward-biased** measure for those countries.

### Tax Revenue Losses (Column 7 of Table 3)

Corporate income tax revenues are reduced by applying the statutory corporate tax rate to shifted profits. Profit shifting reduces corporate tax revenues by **18% in the EU**, **14% in the US**, and **5% in other OECD and developing countries**. Higher-tax countries within the EU (France, Germany) lose more relative to revenues collected than lower-tax Eastern European members consistent with higher statutory rates providing stronger incentives to shift. The authors note that CFC (controlled foreign corporation) rules may partially offset these losses, but keep everything else constant in their calculations, consistent with the broader tax evasion literature.

Tax havens, symmetrically, benefit substantially: Malta, Puerto Rico, Ireland, and Luxembourg derive more than **half of their corporate income tax revenues** from taxes on shifted profits.

### Allocation to Parent Countries and US Dominance

When allocated to parent countries: **~50% of globally shifted profits accrue to US parents**; ~25% to EU parents. US multinationals shift 54% of their foreign profits to havens; other multinationals shift 27%. The authors describe this asymmetry as "a salient and novel pattern": while most countries lose some tax revenues, **US multinationals shift twice as much profit relative to earnings as EU multinationals**, while the EU loses twice as much profit relative to GDP as the US. For the US, profit shifting functions primarily as a tax-saving strategy; for other economies, primarily as a revenue loss.

The higher shifting intensity of US multinationals is attributed to the pre-2018 US tax code provisions and the "check-the-box" regulations introduced in the mid-1990s, which facilitated shifting from foreign high-tax countries to havens (see Wright & Zucman, 2018; Guvenen et al., 2021). About 60% of shifted profits were retained in havens in 2015 (72% for US multinationals specifically), meaning repatriation was not a major channel and, in any case, most countries (unlike the pre-2018 US) do not tax repatriated income.

### Ultimate vs. Immediate Destination of Shifted Profits (Figure 7)

On an **immediate counterpart basis**, 77% of profits shifted out of the EU go to EU havens (Ireland, Luxembourg, Netherlands). On an **ultimate destination basis**, 46% end up in non-EU havens. EU law prohibits member states from taxing payments (royalties, intra-group interest) to other EU member states, but permits anti-avoidance taxation of payments to third countries like Bermuda. By routing profits first to an EU conduit (e.g., Luxembourg) and then onwards to Bermuda, multinationals can circumvent these rules a strategy exemplified by the "double Irish with a Dutch sandwich" (Zucman, 2014, who analyses the Google Alphabet structure). In practice, companies must maintain minimal real activity in conduit EU havens to make these structures work.

Luxembourg, for example, receives $88 billion in shifted profits on an immediate basis but passes roughly half of this to non-EU havens, retaining only $47 billion ultimately.



## p. 1527–1530 Implications for Factor Shares and Macroeconomic Statistics

### Corrected Macro Statistics (Table 4)

The paper provides corrected capital shares and trade balances for all OECD countries and major emerging economies by adding back shifted profits (85% through transfer pricing and intangibles, 15% through intra-group interest) to the operating surplus and value added of source countries. After correction:

- EU countries see capital shares rise by 2–2.5 percentage points (notably: France +2.1 pp, Germany +1.8 pp, UK +2.5 pp).
- The US capital share rises by 1.1 percentage points.
- Trade balances also change materially: the UK, France, Japan, and Greece turn out to have trade **surpluses** in 2015 (versus recorded deficits). The US true trade deficit was 2.1% of GDP, not the officially recorded 2.8%.
- Tax havens show large downward corrections: Ireland's capital share is overstated by 20 percentage points, Luxembourg's by nearly 12.

### Dynamic Capital Share Analysis (Figure 8)

Using the corrected gross corporate labour shares of Gutiérrez & Piton (2020) for Germany, France, and Italy (covering approximately 50% of EU GDP), the authors construct corrected time series. Without correction, the corporate capital share appears flat since 1985 in these countries. After correcting for profit shifting, the capital share rose by approximately **3.0 to 3.5 percentage points** over 1985–2015 using the fraction of corporate income tax revenues lost (from Table 3, Column 7) as the benchmark for shifting intensity in 2015, and extrapolating backwards using the time series of profits shifted by US multinationals as a proxy.

The authors are careful to describe these time-series adjustments as "merely illustrative" due to the simplified assumptions, particularly the use of US outward FATS to proxy the historical trajectory of EU profit shifting.

For context: in the US, the gross-of-depreciation corporate capital share rose **5.8 percentage points** between 1985 and 2015 per official BEA statistics (although different treatments of S-corporations and partnerships produce a range of estimates). After correction, the EU rise becomes closer to though still somewhat below the US magnitude.



## p. 1531 Conclusion

The paper provides what it describes as a "new method to estimate global profit shifting using macroeconomic data." The core contribution is the use of foreign affiliates statistics previously underutilised in the academic literature to document the striking excess profitability of foreign firms in tax havens and to leverage this differential for estimation purposes.

The findings have direct policy relevance. The bilateral nature of the estimates makes them a necessary input for calibrating structural models of multinational production with profit shifting relevant for evaluating formulary apportionment, destination-based corporate taxes, and minimum corporate taxes. The authors explicitly flag the agreement reached by more than 130 countries in June 2021 on a global minimum tax (OECD, 2021) the **Pillar Two** framework as a policy development to which their estimates are directly applicable, both for calibration and for ex-post evaluation. They commit to annual updates (updated estimates for 2016–2018 are available in Tørsløv, Wier & Zucman, 2021, at missingprofits.world).

The paper also identifies statistical deficiencies that limit monitoring: the need for micro-data exchange between statistical authorities to resolve bilateral asymmetries in FATS and FDI data; the need for more comprehensive FATS compilation by more countries; the inadequacy of national accounts in some Caribbean tax havens; and the need for better public corporate registries, especially in the US and in tax havens.

A final avenue identified for future research is the **distributional dimension**: since roughly half of globally shifted profits accrue to US multinational shareholders, and equity ownership is highly concentrated (Saez & Zucman, 2016), profit shifting effectively reduces the effective tax rate of the wealthy and may reinforce inequality a "macro-distributional analysis of globalization" the authors describe as a challenge for future work.
