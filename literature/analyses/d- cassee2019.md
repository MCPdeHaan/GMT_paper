# Cassee (2019) — "International Tax Competition and Justice: The Case for Global Minimum Tax Rates"
Politics, Philosophy & Economics, 18(3), 242–263


## Abstract / General Argument (p. 242)

international tax competition undermines states' capacity for redistributive taxation, which is problematic from both *cosmopolitan* and *internationalist* theories of justice. Cassee examines Dietsch & Rixen's (2014b) "fiscal policy constraint" and argues it is insufficient. Instead, states should, "as a matter of justice, accept global minimum tax rates on mobile tax bases."


## Introduction (pp. 242–243)

- Tax justice was historically treated as a purely domestic matter (Murphy & Nagel, 2002: 41; Rawls, 1971: 8), but globalisation has made this untenable.
- Even if taxing authority stays with individual states, the mobility of parts of the tax base creates justice questions with international reach (Brock, 2008; Dietsch, 2015; Dietsch & Rixen, 2014a, 2014b; Ronzoni, 2009, 2014, 2016).
- Two normative camps are addressed:
  - Cosmopolitanism: distributive justice applies directly beyond state borders; tax competition matters insofar as it produces an unjust global distribution of wealth.
  - Internationalism: distributive justice is domestic in scope, but there are international duties of *background justice* to enable states to realise just institutions and interact fairly (Ronzoni, 2009). Tax competition matters because it undermines "effective fiscal self-determination" of states or produces interstate inequality incompatible with fair international relations (Dietsch & Rixen, 2014b; Ronzoni, 2014, 2016).
- Paper structure: (1) mechanisms/effects of tax competition; (2) normative grounds; (3) Dietsch/Rixen's two principles; (4) critique of their fiscal policy constraint; (5) global minimum tax rates as the preferred solution; (6) how to set the rate; (7) implications for low-income countries.

 

## The Mechanisms and Effects of International Tax Competition (pp. 243–246)

### Theoretical models (pp. 243–244)

Tiebout (1956): Tax competition as a solution to the absence of a market for public goods. Jurisdictions compete; citizens "vote with their feet." But limitations are crucial:
- Assumes taxes merely fund public goods (no redistribution); and
- Assumes perfect mobility of all taxpayers.

Both assumptions fail: (i) virtually all jurisdictions practice redistributive taxation; (ii) capital is mobile but persons are not (legal restrictions on immigration, monetary and personal costs of migration).

Zodrow & Mieszkowski (1986): More realistic model. Fixed global capital stock, perfectly mobile across identical jurisdictions. Each jurisdiction sets capital tax rate to maximise representative consumer's utility over private and public goods. Result: tax-setting takes the form of a prisoner's dilemma, with each jurisdiction individually choosing a rate below the collectively optimal level — i.e., systematic undertaxation of capital.

Extensions and qualifications to Zodrow/Mieszkowski:
- Sørensen (2004): With public infrastructure and redistributive transfers in the model, infrastructure spending may be *above* optimal but redistributive transfers *below* optimal under tax competition.
- Bucovetsky (1991): Asymmetric case — smaller countries have stronger incentives to become tax havens, since capital inflows are proportionally more valuable to them. Becomes an "asymmetric prisoner's dilemma" (Rixen, 2008: Ch. 3) — some jurisdictions benefit from non-coordination, though coordination could be a Pareto improvement with side payments.
- Baldwin & Krugman (2004): Agglomeration effects allow core states to maintain somewhat higher tax rates than periphery.

### Tax evasion and avoidance in practice (p. 245)

The basic model assumes a single uniform capital tax. In reality:
- Corporate income taxes account for roughly one-third of total capital tax revenue; personal taxes on capital income and property account for the remaining two-thirds (Zucman, 2014).
- Personal taxation / illegal evasion: Wealthy individuals hide portfolio capital in tax havens. Estimate: at least 8% of privately owned global financial assets are stashed in tax havens (Zucman, 2013).
- Corporate side / legal avoidance strategies (de Mooij & Ederveen, 2008):
  1. *Profit shifting*: subsidiaries in high-tax jurisdictions pay exaggerated royalties to related entities in low-tax jurisdictions, reducing reported profits.
  2. *Preferential entity taxation*: jurisdictions grant preferential rates to specific corporate forms, creating incentive to adjust legal structure.
  3. *Debt-equity arbitrage*: interest on debt is deductible from the corporate tax base, incentivising debt financing.

Footnote 4 (p. 259) gives IKEA as a detailed example: national subsidiaries pay royalties to Inter IKEA Systems BV (Netherlands); profits further routed via a €5.4 billion loan to Interogo Finance SA (Luxembourg), paying an effective tax rate of just 0.06% between 2012 and 2014, while sending €807.8 million in dividends to Interogo Foundation (Liechtenstein). Based on: Auerbach (2016), study commissioned by Greens/EFA Group in the European Parliament.

### Effects of tax competition (pp. 245–246)

- States engage in both *real* tax competition (attracting actual FDI/residents) and *virtual* tax competition (attracting paper relocations of tax base without real economic relocation) (Dietsch & Rixen, 2014b).
- Empirical evidence suggests profit shifting, not FDI competition, is the main driver of declining statutory corporate tax rates (de Mooij & Ederveen, 2008).
- OECD average statutory corporate tax rate at central government level: fell from 41% in 1981 to 23% in 2014 (Clausing, 2016).
- Widespread demise of inheritance taxation; reduction in top marginal personal income tax rates (partly as a side effect: if personal income were taxed much higher than corporate income, high earners would incorporate to escape personal tax — Rixen, 2016).
- Winners and losers: Some small OECD countries and certain tax havens see net revenue gains. But many countries experience diminished ability to raise capital tax revenue (Genschel & Seelkopf, 2016).
- High-income countries: Main effect is a *shift* of the tax burden toward less mobile factors (labour income, consumption); not so much undersupply of public goods as a reduction of the share of the burden borne by those at the top of the distribution (Clausing, 2016).
- Low-income countries: Often unable to compensate for losses in corporate tax revenue, partly due to lacking institutional capacity to raise alternative taxes (Dietsch & Rixen, 2014b: 156). OECD countries typically have total tax revenue of 30–40% of GDP; developing countries often only 10–20% of GDP. Transfer pricing is at least part of the explanation (European Parliament Directorate-General for External Policies, 2014).

Footnote 5 (p. 259): Tax competition optimists note corporate tax revenue as % of GDP has remained stable/increased in many OECD countries since 1980s (OECD, 2019). But Clausing (2016) provides evidence that *effective* corporate tax rates have in fact dropped, with stable revenues explained by higher corporate profits offsetting downward rate pressure.

 

## A Problem of Justice? (pp. 246–247)

Cassee sets aside right-libertarian (Nozick, 1974) and statist (Nagel, 2005) objections to redistribution/global justice and proceeds on the assumption that both cosmopolitan and internationalist frameworks have reason to see tax competition as a problem of justice (Ronzoni, 2014, 2016).

- Cosmopolitan motive ("individualist equality"): Tax competition is problematic for its effect on global distribution of wealth, basic goods, or welfare among individuals.
- Internationalist motives:
  - *"Fiscal self-determination"*: tax competition undermines the effective ability of states to affect their own economies through fiscal policy.
  - *"Inter-state equality"*: extreme inequality between states may facilitate domination, undermining fair international relations.

Internationalists understand "effective self-determination" / "effective sovereignty" as substantive ability to solve own problems on own terms, not merely freedom from external intervention.

 

## The Membership Principle and the Fiscal Policy Constraint — Dietsch & Rixen (pp. 247–248)

Dietsch & Rixen (2014b) (and Dietsch, 2015) offer two side constraints on national tax policies, aiming to remain neutral between competing substantive distributive theories.

Principle 1 — The Membership Principle: Natural persons and companies must pay taxes in the state(s) whose infrastructure and public goods they actually use. Motivated by the analogy of a customer of a cheap gym using the facilities of an expensive gym without paying its fees. This prohibits:
- Hiding portfolio capital in tax havens ("poaching" — attracting tax base moved only on paper);
- Profit-shifting via transfer price manipulation.

Principle 2 — The Fiscal Policy Constraint (two components) (Dietsch & Rixen, 2014b: 161–166): A tax policy is prohibited if it:
(a) *Outcomes component*: diminishes the aggregate extent of fiscal self-determination internationally; AND
(b) *Intentions component*: is motivated by strategic considerations (i.e., the policy would not be adopted but for the additional tax base attracted from abroad).

If a policy is low-tax but chosen for non-strategic reasons (e.g., a genuine preference for smaller government), it remains permissible under the fiscal policy constraint. Dietsch & Rixen (2014b: 162) use the England/Sweden illustration: if the English genuinely prefer a smaller state than the Swedish, there is no internationalist justification for interfering.

Enforcement: proposed via an International Tax Organisation (ITO) with a dispute settlement body modelled on the WTO (Dietsch & Rixen, 2014b: 166–170).

 

## Three Objections Against the Fiscal Policy Constraint (pp. 248–252)

Cassee accepts the membership principle in principle but finds the fiscal policy constraint unconvincing for three reasons.

### Objection 1: Identifying strategic intentions (pp. 248–249)

How to determine in practice whether a tax policy change is strategically motivated? Dietsch & Rixen (2014b: 168–169) propose using observable implications of intentions, analogous to WTO treatment of non-tariff trade barriers (white-listing permissible intentions based on a means–end relationship to consumer safety/health).

Cassee finds the analogy unconvincing:
- WTO approach is *white-listing* (permissible intentions depend on a verifiable means–end relationship);
- Fiscal policy constraint is *black-listing* (all non-strategic intentions are permissible, including purely normative ones such as claiming that high taxes are unjust in principle) — no objective means–end test available.
- Deeper problem: attributing a "unitary intention" to a parliament or electorate is conceptually problematic (Risse & Meyer, 2016). What if some parliamentarians favour low taxes on principle and others for strategic reasons? What if citizens strategically vote in principled low-tax parties?

### Objection 2: Status quo bias (p. 249)

If the constraint is applied to *changes* in the tax code (as Dietsch & Rixen, 2014b: 164, 168 suggest), it gives an unfair advantage to jurisdictions that already have low-tax policies in place — they are protected from new low-tax competitors without being obliged to change anything.

Alternative (wider) interpretation: states would need to reverse any standing policies that deviate from what they would have chosen absent strategic considerations. But this amplifies the identification problem enormously: requires "rather sophisticated exercises in alternate history," tracing back to when tax competition arguments first entered political debate.

### Objection 3: Asymmetrical restraint — the core objection (pp. 249–252)

The intentions component makes the constraint a version of the "doctrine of double effect" (Risse & Meyer, 2016): the same policy, with identical consequences, is permissible if the capital inflows are *merely foreseen* but impermissible if *intended*.

Cassee uses the noise control analogy: suppose individuals may play music at any volume reflecting genuine preferences, but are banned from adapting strategically to neighbours' noise. This would be unfair for two reasons:
1. Strategic adaptors are unfairly disadvantaged: denied both their first-best preference (quiet music) and their second-best preference (matching the noise level).
2. Silence-lovers' interests are not adequately protected: they are harmed by loud music regardless of whether it reflects a strategic or genuine preference.

Mapped onto tax: the fiscal policy constraint adjudicates the conflict between low-tax and high-tax countries in favour of the low-tax country simply because it acts on a non-strategic preference — without any principled distributive justification.

The stylised England/Sweden scenario (pp. 250–251): Both countries start with the same tax scheme. At t1, England non-strategically lowers capital taxes (preference for small state); Sweden raises them. Capital flows from Sweden to England. At t2, England re-adjusts: larger tax base means it can fund preferred public expenditure at an even lower rate. Sweden must raise rates further, losing more capital. The iteration continues. England's non-strategic choices "will iteratively approximate the tax policies the country would adopt if it were allowed to choose strategically." Sweden is driven ever further from its preferred policies.

Furthermore: the fiscal policy constraint also bans reactive strategic behaviour. If England has non-strategically lowered taxes and attracted Swedish capital, Sweden cannot even enact a moderate tax cut in response without facing a case before the ITO, even if the net capital flow is still from Sweden to England.

Cassee's conclusion on this section (p. 251): even without a substantive theory of distributive justice, a preference-*dependent* rule is not obviously fair compared to a preference-*independent* rule. The fiscal policy constraint does not neutrally protect fiscal self-determination; it structurally favours low-tax preferences.

Note on Dietsch & Rixen's actual position (p. 251, footnote 9, p. 259): Cassee acknowledges that Dietsch & Rixen do not claim a minimum tax rate is unjustifiable *full stop*; their claim is that it cannot be justified on their minimalist internationalist premises alone. Rixen (2011: 460) himself argues in favour of minimum tax rates in other work less focused on neutrality.

 

## A More Robust Solution — Global Minimum Tax Rates (pp. 252–254)

If the fiscal policy constraint is rejected, Cassee argues for a preference-independent mechanism targeting not just strategic tax competition but the broader externality problem of uncoordinated tax-setting under factor mobility (including cases with no strategic intent).

Three possible institutional responses considered:

1. Full centralisation: Transfer power to raise taxes on mobile bases to an international institution. Attractive from a cosmopolitan view; unacceptable from an internationalist view (eliminates fiscal self-determination entirely).

2. Internationalisation mechanism: Retain national tax sovereignty but add an international layer of taxes/subsidies that internalises the external costs of national tax cuts. Each jurisdiction would bear costs equal to the marginal negative externality its tax cut imposes on others. Reference: Wildasin (1989) proposes a subsidy model for US states, estimating that the marginal subsidy should be up to 40% (i.e., $1 of additional local revenue from a tax increase should be matched with a $0.40 national subsidy). Cassee finds this theoretically elegant but politically infeasible: requires an international institution with taxing power, and agreement on one specific implementation scheme would be very difficult.

3. Global minimum tax rates (endorsed): Internationally binding floor below which tax rates on mobile bases are prohibited. States remain free to set rates above the minimum. Compliance enforced by an international institution. Most obvious application: minimum corporate income tax rate; in principle also applicable to property taxes, inheritance taxes, or the top bracket of personal income taxation (given mobility of wealthy individuals).

Advantages over the internationalisation mechanism:
- Simpler to agree on;
- Does not require an international institution with independent taxing power.

Acknowledged suboptimalities:
- States cannot maintain rates below the threshold even if willing to internalise the full external cost;
- Harmful tax competition may persist above the threshold.

But: Minimum tax rates also change the competitive dynamics *above* the threshold. States with rates just above the minimum will face less downward pressure from very-low-tax jurisdictions, permitting them to adjust rates upward, which in turn relieves pressure on states with even higher rates (Keen & Konrad, 2013: 288–292).

Feasibility discussion (pp. 253–254): Cassee acknowledges the political difficulty. Within the EU, little progress on harmful tax competition due partly to unanimity rule (Wasserfallen, 2014). No common definition of the corporate tax base (prerequisite for effective minimum rates and for formulary apportionment under unitary taxation — Dietsch & Rixen, 2014b: 167–168). However:
- The European Commission (2016) and European Parliament (2018) push for a Common Consolidated Corporate Tax Base (CCCTB). See: European Commission proposal for CCCTB (Directive), available at EUR-Lex (CELEX 52016PC0683).
- The European Parliament Committee on Economic and Monetary Affairs (2018) called for a minimum corporate income tax rate of 25% within the EU (Report A8-2018-0051).
- A separate manifesto (Piketty et al., 2014, "Our manifesto for Europe," *The Guardian*, 2 May 2014) called for a 20% minimum corporate tax in Europe plus an additional 10% raised directly at the European level.
- The Baucus Plan (2013, US Senator Max Baucus): proposed minimum tax on income of US corporations' foreign subsidiaries at 80% or 60% of the US tax rate, with a top-up tax in the US to offset any shortfall. Reference: Christians (2013); Dietsch (2015: 70–75).

Cassee's position: global minimum tax rates are "not entirely infeasible in the long run" and are a measure both cosmopolitans and internationalists have reason to support, albeit for different reasons. Cosmopolitans emphasise global distributive justice; internationalists see it as "a circumscription of de jure sovereignty in the interest of protecting de facto sovereignty."

 

## Setting the Global Minimum Tax Rate (pp. 254–257)

Cassee does not determine a precise rate but addresses how the rate should be determined and why it need not be arbitrary (responding to the sufficientarian threshold arbitrariness worry — cf. Casal, 2007).

From a cosmopolitan perspective: Choose the minimum rate that produces a global distribution satisfying the preferred principle of justice, whether sufficientarian ("what rate enables all states to guarantee a social minimum?"), a global difference principle ("what rate maximises the holdings of the globally worst-off?"), or another principle. Normative basis relatively clear; empirical determination difficult.

From an internationalist perspective: The rate should be set with an eye to its effects on effective fiscal self-determination of states.

Definition of effective fiscal self-determination in the literature (Dietsch, 2015: 35; Dietsch & Rixen, 2014b: 153; Ronzoni, 2014: 42–43; van Apeldoorn, 2018: 481–482): a function of the choices available regarding (i) the *size of the public sector* and (ii) the *distribution of burdens and benefits* within the country.

Relative vs. absolute measurement: Most internationalists endorse a *relative* understanding (fiscal self-determination as ability to affect public budget/distribution relative to GDP). Cassee critiques this: a state could "improve" its relative public spending simply by shrinking GDP; it could reduce relative inequality simply by making everyone poorer. These outcomes should not count as high fiscal self-determination. Cassee therefore favours an absolute understanding, which brings internationalism closer to cosmopolitanism by giving internationalists additional reason to care about international wealth distribution.

How does a minimum tax rate affect fiscal self-determination? It affects the option space a jurisdiction has over its corporate tax base, visualised via the Laffer curve: the minimum rate removes all options to the left of the minimum, but also *changes the shape* of the Laffer curve to the right by making higher rates more viable (as competitive pressure from very-low-tax jurisdictions is reduced). Footnote 15 (p. 260): Cassee notes his use of the Laffer curve is merely illustrative of the option-space argument, not an endorsement of the supply-side "Laffer argument" that high rates are always self-defeating.

Key insight: "The formal liberty of choosing your tax rate becomes meaningless if choosing a high rate will result in capital outflows that seriously dent revenues" (Dietsch, 2016: 244, quoted by Cassee). Up to a certain level, constraining *de jure* sovereignty enhances *de facto* sovereignty.

Distributional principle among states: Just as cosmopolitans disagree on the preferred distributive pattern among individuals, internationalists disagree on the preferred distribution of effective fiscal self-determination among states (van Apeldoorn, 2018). Options include: sufficientarianism (each state achieves a minimum level of fiscal self-determination), strict egalitarianism, maximising the worst-off state's fiscal self-determination, or sum-maximisation. Dietsch & Rixen (2014b) appear implicitly to endorse a sum-maximising view (prohibit strategic policies that reduce the *aggregate* extent of fiscal self-determination — footnote 17, p. 260).

Cassee does not resolve which principle is correct but notes these questions arise equally in the operationalisation of the Dietsch/Rixen solution — they are not specific to the minimum tax proposal.

 

## Accommodating the Needs of Low-Income Countries (pp. 257–258)

Worry: if competition for mobile capital via low taxes is curtailed, other location factors (infrastructure, education) gain importance. Low-income countries lacking these advantages would have no instrument to attract FDI, potentially trapping them.

Should low-income countries be exempt from the minimum rate?
- If there were only *one* low-income country, a blanket exemption might be defensible (it could set a somewhat lower rate and still raise substantial revenue from inflows).
- In reality, multiple low-income countries exist. A blanket exemption for all would trigger a race to the bottom among them, preventing any from raising adequate revenue for public investment.

Preferred solutions:
1. *First-best*: direct redistribution of financial means to low-income countries (Dietsch & Rixen, 2014b: 166), enabling improvement of infrastructure and education — making them competitive on non-tax grounds.
2. *Second-best*: a differentiated two-tier system with a lower minimum for low-income countries and a higher minimum for others.
3. A blanket exemption is explicitly rejected: it would harm high-income countries *and* would not serve the interests of low-income countries themselves.

 

## Conclusion (p. 258)

- Credit given to Dietsch & Rixen for substantiating Ronzoni's (2009) claim that tax competition undermines international background justice and for proposing a normative solution to profit shifting and offshore havens.
- The fiscal policy constraint is found unconvincing.
- Both cosmopolitans and internationalists should support globally binding minimum tax rates on mobile tax bases.
- Long-run visions differ: internationalists aim to protect fiscal self-determination of states; cosmopolitans may prefer moving away from a state-centred system entirely.
- Nevertheless, global minimum tax rates are "a step in the right direction from both perspectives."
