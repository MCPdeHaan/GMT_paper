# Dowd, Landefeld & Moore (2017) - Profit Shifting of U.S. Multinationals
*Journal of Public Economics*, 148, pp. 1–13

## p. 1 - Introduction & Motivation

**Context and policy relevance**

- Paper analyses profit-shifting behaviour of U.S. multinational firms using a panel dataset of U.S. tax returns, 2002–2012.
- OECD BEPS project (reports released 5 October 2015): Action 11 describes data, methodologies, and new evidence on profit shifting. OECD estimates up to **$240 billion annually** in global corporate income tax revenue lost. (fn. 3: Clausing (2011) and Clausing (2015) estimate U.S. revenue loss at $57–$90bn in 2008, and $77–$111bn in 2012.)

**Key research question**

Estimating the **elasticity of reported profits with respect to tax rates**, using large dataset of controlled foreign companies (CFC) informational tax returns for U.S. multinationals, allowing elasticities to differ for tax haven affiliates and across the tax rate distribution.

## p. 2


Figure 1
- Plots mean log profits of CFCs for each percentile net-of-tax rate (= 1 minus average tax rate of affiliate's country).
- Separate linear fits for top 50th and bottom 50th percentile of tax rates.
- Bottom 50th (low-tax): steep positive correlation between net-of-tax rate and log profits (right)
- Top 50th (high-tax): approximately flat relationship (left)
- Raw data therefore already suggests a **nonlinear** relationship - simple linear model unlikely to capture full curvature.

Contribution to the literature on profit shifting

1. Uses U.S. *administrative* tax data capturing entire corporate structure, including island tax havens and developing countries with higher rates
2. Resolves a "puzzle" noted by Dharmapala (2014): disconnect between relatively small estimated profit-shifting elasticities from recent research and anecdotal evidence of extensive income shifting among MNCs. By introducing **nonlinearities**, elasticities in low-tax jurisdictions turn out to be multiples of traditional linear estimates.
3. Simulation of raising haven countries to 29% statutory / 17% average rate: linear semi-elasticity would indicate $57bn reduction in reported profits; flexible methodology indicates up to **$116bn lower**, roughly double.

Prior literature on semi-elasticity

- Literature traces to Grubert & Mutti (1991) and Hines & Rice (1994), both using aggregate country-level data. Hines & Rice estimated **semi-elasticity** of profits w.r.t. marginal tax rates using semi-log specification - this has become standard. Specification controls for real economic activity via capital and labour; tax rate captures profit-shifting incentive.
- More recent work uses firm-level data (financial reporting). Generally finds **smaller semi-elasticities** than aggregate-data papers.
- Heckemeyer & Overesch (2013) meta-analysis of 25 studies: "consensus" estimate of semi-elasticity = **0.8**, far smaller than Hines & Rice (1994).
- Dharmapala (2014): raises question of disconnect between descriptive statistics on money in tax havens and smaller elasticities in recent papers.
- Key limitation of financial reporting data (Orbis/Amadeus): poor coverage of island tax havens (Bermuda, Cayman Islands) - Cobham & Loretz (2014) document Orbis coverage is weak outside Europe, particularly in Latin America and the Caribbean.

Papers with firm-level data 

- Huizinga & Laeven (2008): Amadeus data, semi-elasticity = **1.3**.
- Dharmapala & Riedel (2013): Amadeus, 25 EU countries, uses earnings shocks at parent level - finds somewhat smaller effect than Huizinga & Laeven.
- Markle (2012): Orbis database (broader than Amadeus), similar but slightly smaller semi-elasticity than Huizinga & Laeven; finds no significant difference in shifting incentives between territorial and worldwide tax systems. But: includes very few observations from island tax havens due to reliance on financial reporting data.

Papers based on administrative taxation data from the US

- Grubert (2013): 754 U.S. MNCs, 1996–2004, finds share of foreign profits owing to tax rate differentials could be between 5 and 15 percentage points of total profits (preferred estimate: 12 pp). Advantage: can isolate shifting directly out of the U.S. Disadvantage: Hines-Rice method applied subsidiary-by-subsidiary captures total profit shifting (foreign-to-foreign and U.S.-to-foreign) but cannot isolate U.S.-to-foreign.
- Christian & Schultz (2005): U.S. tax return data 2001, rate-of-return methodology - estimates approximately **$58bn shifted abroad**, net of inbound shifting. (fn. 4: actual reported domestic profits in 2001 were $147bn.)

## p. 3 

Nonlinearities in the data

- Benefit of shifting profits greatest by seeking out the lowest-tax jurisdiction, if costs of shifting to very low-tax jurisdictions are not prohibitive.
- If fixed costs to setting up profit-shifting structures exist (e.g. hiring lawyers, agency effects for managers of profitable subsidiaries), firms are further incentivised to funnel profits into a few very low-tax jurisdictions.
- Semi-log form dominant in literature only allows elasticity to change linearly with the tax rate. Hines & Rice (1994) themselves noted "significant curvature" in a quadratic specification but subsequent literature largely reverted to linear form.


Main findings

- Log-linear model: semi-elasticity of reported profits w.r.t. net-of-tax rate = **1.44** (statutory) and **1.08** (average tax rates).
- Nonlinear (quadratic) model: 1 pp reduction in statutory rate from 30% to 29% -> **0.7%** change in reported income. But 1 pp reduction from 5% to 4% -> **4.7%** increase in reported income. Linear model predicts 1.44% in both cases - understates at low rates, overstates at high rates.

## p. 3

**U.S. = hybrid system**

- Most countries sit somewhere between pure worldwide and pure territorial.
- **Worldwide system**: corporations taxed on worldwide income by country of residence.
- **Territorial system**: income taxed only in country where earned.
- U.S. system: "hybrid" = taxes all income of U.S. companies (domestic and foreign), but with **limited deferral** for foreign income earned by foreign subsidiaries of U.S. companies (fn. 5: JCT (2014) gives detailed overview). Income of foreign subsidiaries eligible for deferral, not taxed until repatriated as dividend to U.S. parent.
- Some passive or highly mobile income earned by foreign subsidiaries is **not** eligible for deferral -> taxed in U.S. in year earned -> "subpart F rules" and "subpart F income" (after the relevant section of the Internal Revenue Code).

**Check-the-box regulations (relevant for profit-shifting mechanism)**

- U.S. firms can elect whether foreign subsidiaries are treated as foreign corporations or **foreign disregarded entities (FDEs)** (treated as branches). (fn. 6: JCT (2010) provides useful history of check-the-box regulations and FDEs.)
- Payments (interest, dividends, royalties) between CFCs normally subject to subpart F. But if one subsidiary is elected as an FDE of another CFC, payments between them are **disregarded for U.S. tax purposes** -> valuable tax planning tool for shifting income to low-tax jurisdictions without triggering subpart F inclusion.

**Foreign tax credit = subpart F rules**

- Tax credit available for foreign taxes paid, to avoid double taxation. Can offset U.S. tax owed on foreign-source income (not domestic). Limited ability to use credits from highly-taxed foreign income to offset U.S. tax on more lightly taxed foreign income (including royalties).
- Over three-quarters of OECD countries have territorial systems (meaning that income earned in foreign juridictions is never subject to tax) -> increases importance of incentives to "shift income to low-tax jurisdictions relative to pure worldwide system".
- Many territorial countries also have base protection rules - e.g. France taxes income from subsidiaries in low-tax regimes (less than 50% of the 33.33% French corporate rate). (fn. 7: Deloitte (2016).)

## p. 4–5 - Section 3: Data

**3.1 SOI Sample**

- Primary dataset: Statistics of Income Division (SOI) of the IRS. SOI performs annual studies of corporate income tax returns (Form 1120). (fn. 8: Statistics of Income (2008) for details.)
- Firms with >$50m in assets or >$5m in proceeds sampled every year; smaller firms sampled at lower rate (proportional to asset classification and net income).
- SOI also performs **biannual** study collecting information from **Form 5471** (Information Return of U.S. Persons with Respect to Certain Foreign Corporations): detailed financial data on CFCs owned by firms in the SOI corporate sample. (fn. 9: 2002 sampling methodology slightly different - see Master & Oh (2006) for details.)
- Paper forms **unbalanced panel** of CFCs from Form 5471 for years 2002, 2004, 2006, 2008, 2010, 2012. Each observation = one CFC owned by a U.S. parent firm.
- Key advantage: includes CFCs in tax havens (Bermuda, Cayman Islands) for which financial reporting data is normally unavailable.
- Key disadvantage: data reported on **consolidated basis** - income attributable to branches or "check-the-box" subsidiaries of CFCs reported under the controlling CFC's country. So taxes, capital, and wages may be attributed to wrong country. (fn. 10: one can think of tax reported for a Swiss CFC as the overall tax cost associated with income flowing into that CFC and any entities below it.)

**3.2 Data Definitions**

- **Profits**: Form 5471, Schedule C, Line 18 - "Net income or (loss) before extraordinary items, prior period adjustments, and the provision for income, war profits, and excess profits taxes." (fn. 11: Ideally would compare taxable income and financial income, but many countries have less divergence between book and tax income than the U.S.)
- **Wages**: "Compensation not deducted elsewhere" (fn. 12: incomplete - excludes labour costs included in cost of goods sold).
- **Tangible capital**: Schedule F - sum of buildings and other depreciable assets (less depreciation), depletable assets (less depletion), and land (net of amortisation). (fn. 13: line items 8b, 9b, 10.)
- **GDP per capita and population**: World Bank (2014); for missing dependent territories, data from UN Statistics Division and government websites.
- **Tax rates**: Two measures - statutory and average.
  - *Statutory rates*: KPMG and EY annual summaries as main sources. (fn. 14: EY editions 2004, 2006, 2008, 2010, 2014; KPMG 2002, 2003, 2004, 2006, 2014.) Important caveat: statutory rates may not reflect true tax cost - patent boxes, negotiated lower rates (cf. LuxLeaks). (fn. 15: Guardian, 5 Nov 2014.) OECD BEPS Action 11 report noted "headline statutory tax rates are often not the tax rate applicable at the margin of BEPS behaviour." (fn. 16: OECD (2015).) -> important one
  - *Average tax rates*: calculated from total taxes paid by CFC (Schedule E, Form 5471, "Income, War Profits, and Excess Profits Taxes Paid or Accrued"). (fn. 17.) Computed by dividing taxes paid by profits; winsorised by country-year at 5% in both tails (always winsorising at least 3 observations per tail). (fn. 18.) Country-year average = profit-weighted average of firm-specific rates, **excluding the reference CFC** (because individual CFC rates are endogenous).
  - As shown in Figure 2: difference between statutory and average rates can be quite large; spread is skewed toward positive (statutory > average) - consistent with tax preferences and negotiated rates.

## p. 5 - Section 3.3: Analysis Sample & Summary Statistics

**Endogeneity concerns**

- Both statutory and average rates suffer from endogeneity: "tax policy not necessarily exogenous to pre-tax profits". Firms may generate rents in countries with "large markets, natural resources, or industry clusters" -> higher profits and potentially higher tax rates. High public inputs -> high profits and high taxation. Governments may also adjust tax policy in response to business cycle or other countries' tax policy (Altshuler & Goodspeed, 2015). Much of this endogeneity would **bias estimated profit-shifting elasticity downward** (agglomeration and public goods -> high taxes and high profits).

**Sample restrictions**

- Restricted to CFCs with positive profits, parent not primarily in extractive industries, finance, insurance, or utilities.
- Country must have minimum 10 subsidiaries present in every year of the sample (for country-level average tax rates).
- Starting sample: just over **220,000** observations of active CFCs with positive profits over six years.
- Restriction to positive wages and capital: reduces to ~**99,000** observations.
- Drop further ~1,900 for missing statutory tax rates, GDP per capita, or population. (fn. 20: missing data is non-random with respect to tax haven status - ~11% of analysis sample are in tax havens, but ~16% of dropped observations are from havens -> potential downward bias on estimates. Larger firms better at filling in Form 5471.)
- **Final analysis sample: nearly 97,000 firm-level observations in 80 jurisdictions.**

## p. 6 - Section 4: Methodology

**Baseline equation (Eq. 1) - Hines & Rice (1994) modification**

$$\log(\pi_{it}) = \alpha + \beta_1 \cdot \log(K_{it}) + \beta_2 \cdot \log(L_{it}) + \beta_3 \cdot (1 - \tau_{it}) + \beta_4 \cdot \chi_{it} + \mu_t + \psi_p + \epsilon_{it}$$

- $\pi_{it}$: profits before taxes of CFC $i$ in year $t$.
- $K_{it}$: tangible capital assets; $L_{it}$: wage bill.
- $\tau_{it}$: tax rate of CFC $i$ in year $t$.
- $\chi_{it}$: country-level controls - GDP per capita (and its square), population (and its square).
- $\mu_t$: year fixed effects.
- $\psi_p$: **corporate group level fixed effects** - controls for unobserved profit differences at the group level.
- $\beta_3$: **parameter of interest** - semi-elasticity of profits w.r.t. net-of-tax rate. $\beta_3 \times (1 - \tau_{it})$ = elasticity of profits w.r.t. net-of-tax rate (linearly increasing in net-of-tax rate).

**Exploring nonlinearity: Step 1 - excluding low-tax/haven CFCs**

- Estimate Eq. (1) excluding CRS-defined tax havens (Gravelle, 2013) - expect smaller $\beta_3$.
- Alternative: exclude bottom decile of tax rates (year-by-year to avoid skewing toward present day). (fn. 25: do not remove reference CFC when calculating this.)

**Step 2 - Discontinuous specification (Eq. 2)**

$$\log(\pi_{it}) = \alpha + \gamma_1 \cdot TH_{it} + \beta_1 \log(K_{it}) + \beta_2 \log(L_{it}) + \beta_3(1-\tau_{it}) + \gamma_2 \cdot TH_{it} \cdot (1-\tau_{it}) + \beta_4 \chi_{it} + \mu_t + \psi_p + \epsilon_{it}$$

- $TH_{it}$: indicator for tax haven (or bottom decile) status.
- $\gamma_2$: differential semi-elasticity response for CFCs in low-tax/haven jurisdictions.
- $\beta_3$ captures overall semi-elasticity; $\gamma_2$ captures the additional effect for havens.

**Step 3 - Continuous nonlinear form (Eq. 3) - Taylor approximation**

$$\log(\pi_{it}) = \alpha + \beta_1 \log(K_{it}) + \beta_2 \log(L_{it}) + \beta_3(1-\tau_{it}) + \beta_4(1-\tau_{it})^2 + \beta_5 \chi_{it} + \mu_t + \psi_p + \epsilon_{it}$$

- Second-order (quadratic) approximation. Avoids arbitrary designation of which countries count as tax havens. Hines & Rice (1994) estimated a version with quadratic term and noted "significant curvature" - but subsequent literature largely ignored this.

## pp. 7–8 - Section 5: Results

- Results likely biased **downward** due to over-attribution of capital/wages to tax havens; profits probably less overstated than capital/wages. Results should be treated as lower bound.

**Table 2 - Linear semi-log (Eq. 1)**

- Statutory rate: semi-elasticity $\beta_3$ = **1.437** (s.e. 0.155, significant at 1%). Interpretation: 10 pp decrease in statutory tax rate -> 14.4% increase in reported profits.
- Average rate: semi-elasticity = **1.076** (s.e. 0.108, significant at 1%). Interpretation: 10 pp decrease in average tax rate -> 10.8% increase in reported profits.
- Capital, wages, GDP per capita, population all significant with expected signs.
- (fn. 27: restricting to European member states and pre-2008 period yields lower elasticities - 1.16 for statutory, 0.83 for average - consistent with Amadeus-based literature.)

**Table 3 - Linear specification, excluding low-tax/haven CFCs**

- Excluding CRS-defined tax havens: statutory rate coefficient drops to 0.033 (not significant); average rate 0.710.
- Excluding bottom decile: statutory 0.305 (marginally significant); average 0.690.
- Dropping just over 10% of observations causes dramatic fall in semi-elasticity - strong evidence that results in Table 2 are **driven by CFCs in low-tax jurisdictions**. (fn. 29: running the opposite experiment - only including low-rate jurisdictions - yields coefficients of 4.6 and 2.4 for statutory and average rates respectively.)

**Table 4 - Discontinuous model (Eq. 2)**

- Haven interaction term ($\gamma_2$) is large and significant in all specifications:
  - CRS havens: statutory 3.45, average 3.48.
  - Bottom decile: statutory 4.39, average **7.23**.
- Base effect ($\beta_3$, non-haven) much smaller than Table 2 result.
- Large negative coefficient on haven indicator combined with large positive interaction: "what is driving tax haven profits is the tax rate, and not some other unmeasured aspect of the haven."

**Table 5 - Taylor approximation (Eq. 3)**

- Quadratic term ($\beta_4$) highly significant:
  - Statutory: 8.089 (s.e. 1.239).
  - Average: 7.184 (s.e. 1.101).
- Linear term becomes negative (−10.73 statutory; −10.62 average) - reflecting that the quadratic dominates at low net-of-tax rates.

- Figure 3 (discontinuous estimates, CRS havens): tax haven jurisdictions have much higher elasticities than non-haven jurisdictions. Non-haven line is approximately flat; haven line steeply upward. Pattern more extreme when discontinuity based on bottom decile tax rate rather than CRS definition.
- Figures 4 & 5 (linear vs. quadratic, statutory and average): quadratic elasticities at far right (low net-of-tax, i.e. high-tax) approach those of the linear model; at far left (high net-of-tax, i.e. low-tax), quadratic elasticities are **more than triple** those of the linear specification. Switzerland 2008 indicated as reference point on figures.

## pp. 9–10 - Section 5.2: Check-the-Box and Consolidated Ownership Sensitivity

**Check-the-box background**

- Treasury regulations 1997 allowed taxpayers to select entity classification of subsidiaries (domestic and overseas) -> creation of **foreign disregarded entities (FDEs)**, disregarded for U.S. tax purposes, appearing as branches of parent regardless of classification in country of residence.
- Result: corporations can freely move profits between FDEs and their owner **without triggering subpart F**.
- Data consequence: amounts on Form 5471 incorrectly attributed to country of CFC owner, not country where FDE is incorporated. Capital, wages, and profits of tax havens are inflated by consolidated reporting.
  - Consolidation of capital/wages -> **downward bias** in estimates (capital overstated relative to reported profits in havens).
  - Consolidation of profits -> **upward bias** in estimates.

**Robustness check 1: Excluding capital and wages from regressors**

- Eliminates wrong attribution of capital/wages but introduces omitted variable bias; does not fix incorrect income attribution.
- Statutory rate estimate drops slightly: 1.44 -> 1.17 (not statistically different).
- Average rate estimate increases significantly: 1.08 -> **1.88** - suggests consolidated FDE reporting is biasing average-rate estimates downward.

**Robustness check 2: Excluding CFCs that own an FDE (Table 6 & 7)**

- Capital and wages in tax havens dramatically reduced - now comparable to non-haven levels. Spread between haven and non-haven profits roughly preserved.
- Point estimates drop: statutory 1.44 -> **0.77**; average 1.08 -> **0.72**.
- Despite this significant decline (eliminating foreign-to-foreign shifting channel), "striking that the point estimates remain large even after eliminating subsidiaries potentially engaged in some of the most aggressive foreign-to-foreign profit shifting."

**Figure 6 (quadratic, non-FDE sample)**: Non-FDE quadratic elasticities smaller than full-sample, but curvature remains; estimated elasticity still **exceeds 2** for very low-tax countries. Large difference between linear estimate of 0.7 and quadratic estimates for low-tax countries persists.

## p. 10–11 - Section 5.3: Simulations of Tax Rate Changes

**Table 8 - Marginal effect of 1 pp change in tax rate**

- Linear (statutory): 1 pp tax reduction -> **1.4%** increase in profits, same regardless of whether starting at 5% or 30%.
- Quadratic (statutory): 5% -> 4% -> **4.7%** increase; 30% -> 29% -> **0.7%** increase.
- Discontinuous (linear break): 5% -> 4% -> 4.9%; 30% -> 29% -> 0.5%.
- Average rate quadratic: 5% -> 4% -> 3.1%; 30% -> 29% -> −0.5%.
- Key conclusion: linear specification **overstates** tax sensitivity at high rates and **understates** it at low rates.

**Concentration of profits (IRS Statistics of Income 2014 - 2010 data)**

- Bermuda + Cayman Islands: 9% of worldwide U.S. corporate assets held abroad; **17%** of U.S. corporate foreign earnings and profits.
- Ireland, Luxembourg, Netherlands, Switzerland combined: assets = $4.3 trillion = **29.4%** of U.S. foreign subsidiary assets; earnings and profits = **36%** of U.S. corporate foreign earnings.
- Combined 6 nations: **$435 billion** = **53%** of U.S. corporate foreign earnings in 2010. Figure 7a shows distribution graphically.

**Table 9 - Simulated change in 2010 reported profits (raising haven countries to 29% statutory / 17% average)**

- Quadratic statutory ($29%$): Bermuda −59%; Cayman Islands −59%; Ireland −29%; Luxembourg ~0%; Netherlands −4%; Switzerland −15%.
- Linear statutory ($29%$): Bermuda −34%; Cayman −34%; Ireland −21%; Luxembourg −1%; Netherlands −5%; Switzerland −14%.
- Luxembourg and Netherlands have statutory rates close to 29%, so 29% statutory simulation has minimal effect; however, average rate simulation shows substantial reduction (reflects low effective rates via negotiated deals / tax preferences).
- Using **quadratic specification**: reported profits in all six countries in 2010 would have been **$116bn (statutory)** and **$111bn (average rate)** lower. Linear specification: $55–$76bn - roughly half.
- Note: estimates do not include dynamic effects - if countries raised rates significantly, capital and labour in those countries would also decline -> additional reduction in profits.

**Figure 7b - Simulated 2010 distribution (17% average rate for six countries)**

- 20% of overall foreign profits would be reallocated out of those six countries to other jurisdictions under nonlinear (quadratic) specification.
- Same simulation under linear elasticities: only 11% reallocation.
- Nonlinear specification "can go some way to answering the question of why elasticity estimates in the prior literature cannot account for the large amounts of money located in tax havens."
