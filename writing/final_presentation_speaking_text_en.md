# Speaking Text, Final Thesis Presentation


## Slide 1, Title

Thank you for being here.

Today I present my thesis on Global Minimum Tax revenue, using public country-by-country reporting data. The legal idea behind the Global Minimum Tax, or Pillar Two, sounds simple at first. Large multinational groups should pay at least 15 percent tax on their profits.

The difficulty begins when we ask a more practical question: how much revenue does that rule actually generate for EU Member States?

That question is harder than the headline rate suggests. The revenue estimate depends on the profit that is actually below the 15 percent floor. It depends on how that profit is measured in public data. It depends on the exclusion for real economic substance, on possible behavioural responses by firms, and on the legal rules that decide which treasury may collect the top-up tax.

The purpose of this thesis is to separate those channels. The revenue number matters, and the explanation behind the number matters just as much. Why does it move? How much uncertainty comes from measurement? How much from substance? How much from behaviour? And how much from collection rights?

The contribution is therefore an economic policy decomposition of a contested fiscal forecast. The aim is to make a complicated revenue model readable enough for policy use.

## Slide 2, Research Question and Empirical Route

The research question is: how much of the uncertainty in EU Global Minimum Tax revenue estimates is generated by measurement, substance, behaviour and collection rules?

I answer that question in five steps.

First, I measure the base. In plain terms, I ask how much profit appears to fall below the 15 percent floor. That is the starting point. If the low-taxed profit is measured wrongly, everything downstream inherits that error.

Second, I add substance. Pillar Two does not apply the top-up calculation to the full accounting profit in a mechanical way. It allows an exclusion linked to payroll and tangible assets. That means part of the profit is protected because it is connected to real economic activity.

Third, I add behaviour. If a minimum tax starts to bind, firms may change where they report profit or how they structure activity. I estimate a behavioural response and use it carefully, as an input into scenarios.

Fourth, I allocate revenue. Even after the top-up tax is calculated, we still need to know who can collect it. That depends on domestic minimum taxes, parent-country rules, backstop rules, and the Side-by-Side treatment.

Fifth, I read the results country by country. The same EU-wide total can mean very different things for Germany, the Netherlands, France, Luxembourg, Ireland and Hungary.

The design is deliberately controlled. I switch five modelling and policy choices on or off in one 32-scenario model. Then I decompose which choices move the forecast. This is the simple intuition: by changing one policy assumption at a time, the model shows which assumption carries the uncertainty.

## Slide 3, Data Used

The empirical base is public aggregate country-by-country reporting data from the OECD.

This matters for the interpretation. The unit is an aggregate cell by parent jurisdiction, affiliate jurisdiction and year. Firm microdata are outside the public source. That limits what can be identified, but it also makes the exercise relevant for public policy, because many budget forecasts are made under similar information constraints.

The first source is CbCR Table I. It reports profit, accrued tax, employees and tangible assets by parent-affiliate relationship. I use it for the corrected profit base, effective tax rates and proxies for economic activity.

The second source is CbCR Table IV. This table is central, because it reports profit by subsidiary-level effective tax rate bracket. It helps answer the question that matters most for Pillar Two: how much profit is actually taxed below 15 percent?

Where Table IV is absent or incomplete, I use OECD percentile ETR data to approximate the distribution of effective tax rates within jurisdictions. That is necessary because an average effective tax rate can hide a large low-taxed mass.

I then add FATS and EU KLEMS data to model payroll, turnover and tangible-capital structures. These sources are used for the substance shield and for the capacity of substance-based tax incentives.

Finally, I use fiscal and legal sources for CIT revenue, GDP, population and implementation status. Those variables matter when the model translates tax bases into country-level policy readings.

The scope is shown at the bottom. The panel contains 15,758 parent-affiliate-year cells, 58 parent jurisdictions, 228 affiliate jurisdictions and the years 2017 to 2022.

That scope is broad enough to make the exercise informative. It is also transparent enough to show where public data stop short.

## Slide 4, Measurement Framework

The first substantive stage is measurement. I describe it as three translations.

The first translation corrects the profit base. Raw CbCR data can contain double-counting exposure, especially in domestic parent cells. After correction, measured positive profit in the model falls from US$46.7 trillion to US$34.8 trillion. This is a denominator correction. It is a correction of the profit base before any Global Minimum Tax revenue is estimated.

The second translation locates the low-taxed slice. This is where average effective tax rates become dangerous. A jurisdiction can have an average rate above 15 percent while still containing a large amount of profit taxed below 15 percent. Pillar Two is concerned with that low-taxed profit inside the jurisdictional total.

For that reason, the thesis uses Table IV brackets where possible, and distributional evidence where the public data are incomplete.

The third translation applies the top-up gap. For the profit below 15 percent, the model measures the distance to the floor. This is done before substance, behaviour and allocation rules enter.

The right-hand side of the slide states the key distinction. An average ETR approach asks whether a jurisdiction is below 15 percent on average. A distributional ETR approach asks how much profit inside that jurisdiction is below 15 percent.

That difference is large enough to move the forecast. In the EU27 table, the distributional column is consistently above the average column. The reason is concrete: the distributional branch identifies both how much profit is low-taxed and how far below 15 percent that profit sits.

If I reduce this slide to one sentence, it is this: the largest uncertainty starts with whether the public data correctly identify the low-taxed profit mass.

## Slide 5, Behavioural Response: What Is Estimated

The next stage is behaviour. The equation on the slide is technical, but the question behind it is simple.

Within the same parent-affiliate relationship, I compare years with different effective tax rates. I hold fixed the relationship itself, so permanent differences between a given parent jurisdiction and a given affiliate jurisdiction do not drive the estimate. I also hold fixed year effects, so general shocks in a specific year are absorbed.

In plain language, the regression asks: when the effective tax rate is higher within the same bilateral relationship, is reported profit lower?

The preferred semi-elasticity is 1.50. This means that a one percentage point higher effective tax rate is associated with about 1.5 percent lower reported profit.

Translated into the scenario model, an ETR increase from 5 to 15 percent implies that about 13.9 percent of reported profit is reallocated or no longer observed in that low-taxed cell. An increase from 10 to 15 percent implies about 7.2 percent.

This is an important result, and it needs a careful boundary. I use the coefficient to discipline behavioural scenarios. It is a scenario input, with limited causal ambition. The data are public aggregates, and firms can respond through several margins at once.

The result is still economically meaningful. In the revenue model, behaviour reduces cumulative EU revenue by US$46.7 billion over 2017 to 2022. In the variance decomposition, behaviour explains 5.1 percent.

So behaviour matters. It belongs in the forecast. The evidence also tells us that other channels dominate the uncertainty in this particular design.

## Slide 6, Behavioural Regression Output

This slide shows the regression output behind the behavioural input. I will focus only on what is needed for the modelling choice.

I test three functional forms: linear, quadratic and logarithmic. The Bayesian Information Criterion selects the linear specification. That is useful because it favours fit while penalising unnecessary complexity.

In the linear model, the coefficient on the effective tax rate is negative and statistically strong. The estimate is minus 1.500, with a standard error of 0.272.

All models include parent-affiliate fixed effects and year fixed effects. That means the model compares variation within the same bilateral relationship over time, while also controlling for common annual shocks.

The standard errors are two-way clustered by parent and affiliate jurisdiction. That matches the structure of the data, because the errors may be correlated on both sides of the reporting relationship.

The conclusion from this slide is narrow and useful. The regression gives a parsimonious behavioural coefficient for scenario analysis. It gives enough support to include behaviour in the forecast. The full decomposition gives it a clearly bounded role.

That is why I keep the behavioural section short. The regression is necessary, but the decomposition shows that the revenue forecast is moved more strongly by measurement and allocation.

## Slide 7, Substance Result: 15 Percent Is Not the Economic Floor

The next channel is substance.

This slide matters because the 15 percent rate can easily be misread. Pillar Two sets a 15 percent minimum rate, but the substance-based income exclusion protects part of the profit linked to payroll and tangible assets.

The economic floor is therefore lower than the headline rate for part of the base.

The table gives the magnitude. For the EU27 weighted result, the floor before SBTI is 14.2 percent. After SBTI, it falls to 11.9 percent. Germany falls from 13.9 to 10.7 percent. France falls from 13.8 to 10.2 percent. The Netherlands, Ireland and Luxembourg show smaller changes.

The policy reading is direct. For the EU27, the weighted SBIE ratio is 10.4 percent and the SBTI capacity is 3.7 percent. The relevant policy object is therefore the effective floor after substance, before behaviour and allocation.

This has two implications.

First, substance reduces expected revenue. Protected payroll and tangible assets reduce the amount of profit exposed to top-up tax.

Second, substance changes the distribution of risk across countries. Economies with more payroll and tangible assets connected to the low-taxed base receive more protection in the model. Countries where the low-taxed profit is less connected to real activity receive less.

The interpretation remains bounded. The public data show capacity and structure. They do not show every firm-level election or every detailed GloBE calculation. The estimate should therefore be read as a modelled substance channel.

## Slide 8, Side-by-Side: EU27 Collection Example, 2022

Up to this point, the question has been how much top-up tax exists. This slide asks who can collect it.

Pillar Two has a collection hierarchy. A source jurisdiction can collect first through a qualified domestic minimum top-up tax. If the source country does not collect, the parent jurisdiction may collect through the income inclusion rule. If revenue remains, undertaxed profits rules can operate as a backstop.

The Side-by-Side variant in my model restricts the residual backstop for qualifying US-parented groups. The low-taxed profit does not move in the data. What changes is the legal ability of EU countries to collect the residual top-up tax.

The EU27 example for 2022 shows the mechanism clearly.

Protected collection remains US$35.4 billion. These are cases where source-country or EU-parent rules remain available. The residual enforcement backstop falls from US$27.8 billion to US$9.6 billion. Total EU collector revenue falls from US$63.2 billion to US$45.0 billion. The difference is US$18.3 billion.

The country examples show that the loss is uneven. Germany loses US$3.2 billion in this example. The Netherlands loses US$2.0 billion. France loses US$2.7 billion. The share protected by source rules also differs strongly, with the Netherlands more protected than Germany and France in this table.

The policy point is simple. A Global Minimum Tax revenue estimate is also an estimate of legal collection rights. Two countries may face the same underlying low-taxed profit, while their budgetary exposure differs because their position in the collection hierarchy differs.

## Slide 9, Revenue Decomposition

This is the main results slide.

The table decomposes the uncertainty in cumulative EU revenue across the 32 scenarios. The main share column reports how much of the scenario variance is explained by each channel. The effect column reports the average change in cumulative revenue.

The dominant result is low-taxed base measurement. It explains 69.1 percent of the scenario variance and raises the cumulative revenue estimate by US$197.0 billion when the distributional low-taxed base is used.

The economic interpretation is straightforward. The public data must identify the low-taxed mass inside jurisdictions. If they do not, the revenue forecast moves by more than it does under the other modelling choices.

The second channel is collection-right rules. It explains 15.0 percent of the variance and changes cumulative revenue by minus US$87.5 billion. This is the legal incidence channel: the rules change where revenue can accrue.

Behaviour explains 5.1 percent and changes the estimate by minus US$46.7 billion. Substance explains 4.6 percent and changes it by minus US$43.6 billion. The rate endpoint explains only 0.1 percent in the full design.

Interactions add 6.0 percent. The backup slides show that the ranking survives when interactions are allocated across factors. Measurement remains first. Collection rights remain second. Behaviour and substance follow.

This is the central message of the thesis. The largest uncertainty in this model is basic and practical: can we measure which profit is actually below 15 percent?

## Slide 10, Country Policy Readings

The decomposition becomes useful for policy only when it is read by country.

Each row should be read in four steps. First, the preferred revenue estimate. Second, revenue relative to existing corporate income tax, which gives fiscal salience. Third, the scenario spread relative to corporate income tax, which gives budgetary uncertainty. Fourth, the share protected through QDMTT. The final column then states the dominant channel in the country-level spread.

Germany has US$44.5 billion of preferred revenue over the period. That is 9.0 percent of corporate income tax revenue, with a spread of 16.2 percent of CIT. The dominant channel is ETR measurement, at 84.1 percent. Germany is therefore mainly a measurement case.

The Netherlands has US$87.1 billion of preferred revenue, equal to 41.4 percent of CIT. The QDMTT share is 92.2 percent. That means a large part of the preferred revenue is domestically protected in the model. The dominant uncertainty still comes from ETR measurement.

France has US$20.2 billion, equal to 5.4 percent of CIT. ETR measurement remains dominant. The fiscal salience is lower than in the Netherlands, although the measurement issue is still central.

Luxembourg is the exceptional row. The preferred estimate is US$51.7 billion, equal to 225.1 percent of CIT. This should be read as a fiscal stress statistic. Ordinary budgetable cash would be the wrong interpretation. The dominant channel is Side-by-Side, at 58.0 percent. For Luxembourg, collection rights are central.

Ireland has US$27.1 billion, equal to 30.3 percent of CIT. Its dominant channel is substance, at 38.1 percent. That reflects the importance of the substance shield for the Irish policy reading.

Hungary has a smaller absolute amount, US$3.9 billion, but a high spread relative to CIT. ETR measurement remains the dominant channel.

The broader lesson is that EU revenue is a composite policy object. For some countries, the main issue is measurement. For others, it is collection rights. For Ireland, the substance channel becomes especially important. A single EU total cannot show these differences.

## Slide 11, Policy Next Steps

The policy recommendations follow directly from the ranking in the decomposition.

The first step is to measure the base better. If low-taxed base measurement is the largest source of uncertainty, reporting should focus there. The concrete recommendation is bracket-level ETR reporting below 15 percent. Policymakers need to see how much profit falls into the relevant low-tax brackets.

The second step is to secure and monitor collection rights. Revenue estimates should explicitly model QDMTT status, residual IIR exposure and UTPR exposure. Side-by-Side changes who can collect, while the underlying low-taxed profit may be unchanged.

The third step is to publish ranges. A single budget number can look more precise than the evidence allows. The 32-scenario range in this thesis is economically material. A preferred estimate should therefore be accompanied by a decomposition of the assumptions that move it.

The fourth step is ex post validation. Once implementation data exist, forecasts should be reconciled with observed GloBE, QDMTT, SBIE and incentive-claim data. That is how policymakers can learn which scenario assumptions were realistic.

The order matters. Distributional measurement comes first. Legal collection-right modelling comes second. Behavioural and substance validation follow. All four are relevant, but the decomposition shows where better information would reduce uncertainty most.

## Slide 12, Conclusion

I close with four points.

First, the EU revenue object is economically material. The preferred cumulative EU collector revenue is about US$304 billion over 2017 to 2022. This is a counterfactual estimate on historical data. Actual tax receipts are outside this estimate.

Second, forecast disagreement is mainly about the measured low-taxed base. The most practical question is whether the data show enough detail below the 15 percent threshold.

Third, behaviour matters. The estimated semi-elasticity gives a disciplined behavioural scenario. In the full decomposition, measurement and allocation move the forecast more.

Fourth, collection-right rules change fiscal incidence. The same underlying low-taxed profit can reach different treasuries depending on QDMTTs, IIRs, UTPRs and Side-by-Side treatment. Domestic minimum taxes are therefore strategically important.

The thesis contributes an economic policy decomposition of a contested fiscal forecast. The amount itself matters. The explanation behind the amount matters even more: why does the estimate move, and what information would make the forecast better?

Thank you. I am happy to take your questions.

## Backup Notes for Questions

## If asked why 2017 to 2022 is used

The period is a historical counterfactual. I use the available CbCR panel to simulate what the relevant top-up tax and collector-country revenue would have been under different modelling and policy choices. Actual tax receipts in those years are outside the claim. The purpose is to learn the size and uncertainty structure of the policy.

## If asked why average ETR is insufficient

An average effective tax rate hides the distribution inside a jurisdiction. A jurisdiction can average above 15 percent while still containing a substantial amount of profit taxed below 15 percent. Pillar Two is triggered by the low-taxed profit, so bracket-level or distributional information is necessary.

## If asked how strong the behavioural claim is

The behavioural coefficient is suitable as a scenario input. The regression uses parent-affiliate and year fixed effects, the linear form is selected by BIC, and the coefficient is statistically strong. The data are still aggregated, so I treat the estimate as disciplined scenario evidence with a bounded causal interpretation.

## If asked about Luxembourg

Luxembourg should be read carefully. The estimate is extremely high relative to corporate income tax revenue, so it is best interpreted as a fiscal stress statistic. The core result is that Luxembourg is highly sensitive to collection-right assumptions, especially the Side-by-Side treatment.

## If asked about substance and the 15 percent rate

The headline minimum rate is 15 percent, but the substance-based income exclusion protects part of the profit linked to payroll and tangible assets. In the model, this lowers the effective economic floor. For the EU27, the weighted floor falls from 14.2 percent before SBTI to 11.9 percent after SBTI.

## If asked what policymakers should improve first

The first improvement is reporting of effective tax rate brackets below 15 percent. That addresses the largest uncertainty source. The second improvement is explicit modelling of QDMTT, IIR and UTPR positions. The third is routine publication of scenario ranges next to central revenue estimates.
